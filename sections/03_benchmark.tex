\section{Building a Benchmark with Known Truth}
\label{sec:benchmark}

\subsection{What a benchmark needs}
\label{sec:benchmark-needs}

For a real planet the true spectrum is unknown. An injection test supplies a
truth: a known transmission spectrum is added to detector data, and the
spectrum that each method recovers is compared with it.
Section~\ref{sec:validation-problem} listed what such a test needs for SOSS: a
real exposure, a measured image of the star, injection into the raw reads,
and several pipelines that learn the truth only after delivering their
results. The image of the star matters because only starlight dims during a
transit, so the injector has to decide which of the observed light is
starlight. If it decides this with the same kind of model that a pipeline uses
to separate starlight from background, the errors of that model are hidden and
the pipeline is favoured, a problem known as an inverse crime
\citep{KaipioSomersalo2007}. A measured image of the star avoids this
particular risk. My first
injectors were built from the WASP-17~b data alone, and this section describes
what they taught me.

\subsection{Injecting into the raw reads}
\label{sec:raw-injection}

My first injector added the transit to the count-rate images, after the
detector stages that fit the ramps. This shortcut skipped the stages in which
the methods differ, such as their treatment of $1/f$ noise, saturated pixels
and cosmic rays, so I moved the injection to the raw reads. For the reference
atmosphere, a model atmosphere used throughout these tests, NOVA's
root-mean-square error then rose from 125 to 182~ppm on the same data, and its
spectrum was about 90~ppm too deep.

The main identified cause was the $1/f$ correction that NOVA's detector
processing takes from exoTEDRF (Section~\ref{sec:detector-processing}). It
estimates a column offset from the difference between each group and a
reference image scaled by a light curve measured from the data, using pixels
outside the trace cores. If the injected dimming does not follow that scaled
reference, part of the transit enters the estimated noise correction, and
subtracting it makes the transit deeper. In this test the correction deepened
the injected transit by about 1 to 2\%. Using instead the correction calculated
from the same data without the injected transit removed this effect and
lowered NOVA's error to 150~ppm. This was a diagnostic, since such transit-free
data do not exist for a real transit, and it did not establish which mismatch
between the injected light and the reference caused the effect.

Injecting into the raw reads therefore tests effects that an injection into
the count rates misses. Light outside an extraction box can still change the
recovered spectrum, because it enters the $1/f$ correction.

\subsection{Which light belongs to the star}
\label{sec:which-light}

Near the centre of each trace the star dominates, but in the faint wings and
between the traces part of the light may come from the zodiacal background,
other stars or the detector, and the WASP-17~b data alone cannot say how
much. My lower estimate of the starlight dims a calibrated model of the star
within 16 pixels of the traces, the measured light after background
subtraction between 16 and 80 pixels, and nothing further out. In the 40-row
extraction box of exoTEDRF, its starlight is typically 0.4, 2.2 and 4.0\% below
the observed light after background subtraction at 1.0 to 1.8, 1.8 to 2.3 and
2.3 to $2.8\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, and that difference never dims. My upper estimate
scales the model of the star up, so that approximately all of the remaining
box light dims.

Figure~\ref{fig:flip} shows what this choice did. Under the lower estimate,
NOVA recovered the reference atmosphere with an error of 124~ppm and exoTEDRF
with 319~ppm. Under the upper estimate the order reversed, with 358~ppm for
NOVA and 150~ppm for exoTEDRF. The two injections share the same
out-of-transit data, and only the light removed during the transit differs.
Both errors therefore moved in the same direction, by about 500~ppm in the
red, whilst the difference between the two pipelines barely changed. The
benchmark rewarded whichever pipeline agreed with the injector about which
light is starlight.

exoTEDRF counts all the light left in its box after background subtraction as
starlight, which matches the upper estimate. Under the lower estimate, a grey
transit, whose depth is the same at every wavelength, showed the resulting
dilution: exoTEDRF recovered typically 0.969 of the injected depth in the red.
Applying this wavelength-dependent response to the known injected spectrum
predicted most of exoTEDRF's error on the reference atmosphere: removing the
predicted error left 116 instead of 319~ppm. This was a diagnostic that used
the known truth. NOVA's smooth background model is of the same kind as the
background against which the model star of the lower estimate was built, so
the lower estimate risked favouring NOVA.

I also tested whether the real WASP-17~b data could tell the two estimates
apart. In each column, I fitted the fractional transit depth of the whole
extraction box and of its five brightest rows, and divided the first by the
second. If the brightest rows hold almost only starlight and the processing
preserves both depths, a ratio of one means that the box light is also all
starlight, whereas the lower estimate predicts a ratio below one in the red.
The red ratios were about 0.996 after the $1/f$ correction and about 1.012
before it. However, the same measurement, repeated in intervals without a
transit, gave spurious differences between the box and the brightest rows of
up to about 11\% of the transit depth in the red, against the 1\% needed to
decide, so the test was inconclusive even without the $1/f$ correction. More generally, a broad
component of the starlight can be confused with the background, or with a slow
electronic drift that partly follows the transit. Separating them needs extra
information, such as exposures in which the star sits elsewhere on the
detector. Such calibration exposures are the basis of the benchmark in the
next section.
