\section{Building a Benchmark with Known Truth}
\label{sec:benchmark}

\subsection{What a benchmark needs}
\label{sec:benchmark-needs}

An injection test writes a known transmission spectrum into detector data and
compares the spectrum that each method recovers with it.
Section~\ref{sec:validation-problem} listed four things that such a test needs
for SOSS: a real exposure, a measured image of the star, injection into the
raw reads, and several pipelines that learn the truth only after delivering
their results. The measured image of the star keeps the injected starlight
independent of the assumption about starlight that is being tested. Using the
same model to generate and analyse data can hide that model's errors, a
problem known as an inverse crime \citep{KaipioSomersalo2007}. The injectors
I built during the first year to provide such a test failed, and what their
failures taught me is set out below.

\subsection{Injecting into the raw data}
\label{sec:raw-injection}

The first lesson concerned where the transit is added. My first injector added
it to the calibrated integrations, after the detector stages of the pipeline.
This was fast, but it skipped the stages in which the methods differ in their
treatment of $1/f$ noise, saturated pixels and cosmic rays. I therefore moved the injection to the
raw ramps, and each method received its own native input made from the same
injected ramps. The new injector took the real pre-transit integrations of
WASP-17~b and removed, read by read, the light that the injected transit would
block.

\subsection{Which light belongs to the star}
\label{sec:which-light}

To remove this light, the injector needs a map of the starlight on the
detector. Near the centre of each
trace the star dominates, and there the map is well determined. Further out, in the faint
wings of the traces and between them, part of the light may come from the
zodiacal background, other stars or the detector itself, and the WASP-17~b
data alone cannot say how much. My first map, the lower estimate, contained 0.9
to 5.0\% less light than observed in the extraction box and treated the rest
as background. I therefore bracketed this choice with an upper estimate, in
which all the light in the box is starlight.

Figure~\ref{fig:flip} shows what this choice did for one injected model
atmosphere, the reference atmosphere.
Under the lower estimate, \nova{} recovered the injected spectrum with a
root-mean-square error of 124~ppm and exoTEDRF with 319~ppm. Under the upper
estimate the order reversed, with 358~ppm for \nova{} and 150~ppm for
exoTEDRF.

The out-of-transit data are identical in the two cases, and only the light
removed during the transit differs. Each pipeline measures the depth as the
light lost divided by its own estimate of the starlight. Both errors therefore
moved in the same direction, by about 500~ppm in the red, whilst the
difference between their mean errors barely changed, from 276 to 289~ppm. The
benchmark rewarded the pipeline whose estimate of the starlight agreed with
the injector's, but the WASP-17~b data could not determine which estimate was
right. The test showed that the ranking depends on
this assumption for one atmosphere, but not which pipeline is right on the
real data.

This is the danger of an inverse crime (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark-needs}).
\nova{}'s model of the starlight closely matches the lower estimate,
which treats about 2 to 4\% of the red light in the box as background, so the
lower-estimate benchmark risked favouring \nova{}. After its own background
subtraction, exoTEDRF treats all the remaining light in its box as starlight,
and so matches the upper estimate. A grey transit, whose depth is the same at
every wavelength, showed the effect directly. Under the lower estimate,
exoTEDRF counted light that the injector had treated as background as
starlight, which diluted the transit: it recovered only 0.969 of the injected
depth in the red.
Correcting the reference-atmosphere result for this measured dilution reduced
exoTEDRF's error from 319 to 116~ppm.

A direct test did not resolve the split between starlight and background in
the WASP-17~b data. If all the light in the extraction box is starlight,
the transit dims the box by the same fraction as the centre of the trace, so
the ratio of the two dips is one. The measured ratios, between 0.995 and
1.012, could not tell the two estimates apart. Repeating the measurement in
intervals without a transit produced spurious differences between the box and
trace-centre depths larger than one per cent of the transit depth, the precision
needed to distinguish the estimates. More generally, a broad component of the
starlight can be confused with the background, or with an additive signal that
partly follows the shape of the transit, such as a slow electronic drift. Separating
them needs extra information, for example exposures in which the star sits
elsewhere on the detector. Such calibration observations are the basis of the
benchmark in the next section.
