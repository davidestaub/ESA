\section{NOVA}
\label{sec:nova}

NOVA fits the transmission spectrum directly to the detector pixels, so the
split between starlight and background is decided in the same fit as the
transit. One forward model predicts every fitted pixel from the spectrum
$D(\lambda)$, the limb darkening $\bm{u}(\lambda)$, the stellar brightness and
the background, and all four are fitted together by minimising one objective.
The other ingredients are held fixed. Some of them are estimated beforehand
from each input: the spatial profile, the reference spectrum of the star, a
slow bend of the baseline, the time pattern of the background and its expected
amplitudes, a noise scale for each pixel and the orbital geometry. The others
are the same for every input: the depth bins, the limb-darkening reference,
the apertures, the off-trace pixels, the background maps and two noise scales.

In this section, I describe NOVA as it is currently implemented, and end with a
first, preliminary spectrum of the real WASP-17~b visit.

\subsection{Detector processing}
\label{sec:detector-processing}

NOVA's input is prepared from the raw ramps with the JWST calibration pipeline
\citep{JWSTPipeline2025} and exoTEDRF \citep{Radica2024}. The JWST group steps
run up to and including the reference-pixel correction. exoTEDRF then
subtracts a scaled background model from the groups, removes the $1/f$ noise
once from each group by subtracting the median of each column outside the
trace cores, and adds the background back. Field stars found in the F277W
exposure are left out of these medians. The linearity, jump-detection,
ramp-fitting and gain steps then give count rates. Stage~2 of exoTEDRF applies
the flat field, subtracts a scaled zodiacal background model and corrects bad
pixels. I apply no further mask to the scene.

\subsection{The detector forward model}
\label{sec:forward}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/nova_schematic.pdf}
  \caption{Schematic of the detector forward model of \nova{}. (a) The
  fitted pixels are grouped by order and column (a sketch, not data), and the
  two orders do not overlap. (b) The count rate of each pixel is modelled as
  starlight, dimmed by the transit, plus a background that is not dimmed. The
  spectrum is shared by both orders, whilst the limb darkening is fitted for
  each order as penalised deviations from a theoretical reference.}
  \label{fig:nova-schematic}
\end{figure}

Within one detector column, the pixels of a trace see nearly the same
wavelength. NOVA therefore groups the pixels by order and column: each group
$g$ contains the pixels $P_g$ of one order in one column
(Figure~\ref{fig:nova-schematic}). The fitted pixels lie in two fixed
apertures along the traces. Four edge columns, the 51 reddest columns of
order~1, which the wavelength calibration does not directly support, and
flagged samples are left out. For the calibrated count rate $Y_{tp}$ of pixel
$p$ in integration $t$, NOVA predicts
\begin{equation}
 \widehat Y_{tp}
 = \gamma_t\, C_{tg}\,
   \frac{\sum_{\lambda} R_{p\lambda}\, s_\lambda\,
         \mathcal{T}_{t}(\lambda)}
        {\sum_{p'\in P_g}\sum_{\lambda} R_{p'\lambda}\, s_\lambda}
 + B_{tp},
 \label{eq:nova-forward}
\end{equation}
where $C_{tg}$ is the stellar brightness of the pixel's group $g$ at time $t$,
$\gamma_t$ is a fixed correction for a slow bend of the baseline
(Section~\ref{sec:continuum}) and $B_{tp}$ is the background left after
Stage~2. The response $R_{p\lambda}$ is the count rate in pixel $p$ per unit
stellar flux at wavelength $\lambda$. Each pixel has a response to its own
order only, so the light of the other order is not modelled in it. The
reference spectrum of the star, $s_\lambda$, is defined on a fine wavelength
grid. It is fitted to the out-of-transit column fluxes of the input and then
held fixed. The transit factor $\mathcal{T}_t(\lambda)$ dims the light at
$\lambda$, given the depth $D$ of its bin, the limb darkening $\bm{u}$ of the
order and the orbital state $\Omega$. Each pixel is therefore dimmed by its own
mix of wavelengths, weighted by its response. Only the starlight is dimmed;
light assigned to the background is not.

The line-spread kernel spreads the light of one wavelength over a few
neighbouring columns. $R_{p\lambda}$ contains this kernel, so each pixel sees
a weighted mix of nearby wavelengths, and NOVA accounts for this mixing when it
fits the depths. NOVA builds $R_{p\lambda}$ for each input with the SOSS
extraction code of the JWST pipeline
\citep{DarveauBernierEtAl2022,JWSTPipeline2025}, from the public trace and
wavelength calibration \citep{BainesEtAl2023Trace,BainesEtAl2023Wavelength},
kernel and throughput, and its own spatial profile
(Section~\ref{sec:response}).

\subsection{The shared spectrum and transit model}

A planet has one transmission spectrum, whichever order records its light, so
both orders share $D(\lambda)$. It consists of $K=160$ top-hat bins from 0.62
to $2.84\,\mu\mathrm{m}$. Below $2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ the bins are 0.0097 to
$0.0100\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ wide, above it $0.0382\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, and the bluest
bin merges two. The two reddest bins, above $2.764\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, receive no
light in the model: order~2 does not reach them, and order~1 is cut at
$2.758\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, where its direct calibration ends. They are held at
the white-light depth and not reported. The other 158 depths, the supported
set $I$, are
\begin{equation}
  D_j = \bar D\,
  \frac{\exp(h_j)}{\sum_{k\in I} w_k \exp(h_k)}, \qquad \bm{h}=\bm{H}\bm{z},
  \qquad j\in I,
  \label{eq:depth-basis}
\end{equation}
where $\bar D>0$ is their width-weighted mean, $w_k$ are the bin widths
normalised over $I$, and $\bm{z}$ are 157 free shape coordinates. The fixed
$158\times157$ matrix $\bm{H}$ comes from a QR decomposition of a vector of
ones. Its columns are orthonormal and each sums to zero. Adding the same
constant to every $h_j$ would not change the depths. $\bm{H}$ excludes this
direction, so each spectrum has exactly one set of coordinates. The fitted values are only
$\bar D$ and $\bm{z}$. 

The orbital state $\Omega$ is a circular orbit with the period of
\citet{LouieEtAl2025}, and with the mid-transit time $t_0$, scaled semi-major
axis $a/R_\star$ and impact parameter $b$ from NOVA's white-light fit. The
transit $\mathcal{T}_t$ is computed with jaxoplanet \citep{Jaxoplanet2025} and
averaged over each integration of about 44~s.

The limb darkening follows the quadratic law \citep{MandelAgol2002}, written
in the coordinates $q_1,q_2\in[0,1]$ of \citet{Kipping2013},
\begin{equation}
 u_1=2\sqrt{q_1}\,q_2, \qquad
 u_2=\sqrt{q_1}\,(1-2q_2).
 \label{eq:kipping-coordinates}
\end{equation}
Limb darkening and depth are partly degenerate. The spectral fit therefore
allows only a smooth deviation from a reference computed from STAGGER
intensities \citep{MagicEtAl2015}. For each order, the logits of $q_1$ and
$q_2$ may differ from the reference by an offset and a slope in normalised
log-wavelength, eight numbers in total. A straight line in log-wavelength
allows the same change for each factor in wavelength, and order~1 spans a
factor of 3.3. The deviation is penalised for departing from the reference and
for its curvature in wavelength. Here the curvature term only restrains the
slope, mainly in order~2. The white-light fit, by contrast, leaves $u_1$ and
$u_2$ free for each order, with uniform priors from 0 to 1
(Section~\ref{sec:own-white}).

\subsection{The empirical spatial response}
\label{sec:response}

The response needs the spatial profile: the share of a column's starlight that
falls on each of its pixels. The starlight dims during the transit and the
background does not, so the part of a pixel's time series that follows the
transit measures its starlight. This assumes that only starlight varies like
the transit. For each order $o$, I first build a template $\phi_o(t)$ of the
transit shape. It is the median fractional departure from a straight-line
out-of-transit baseline, taken over the brightest 65\% of the order's valid
aperture pixels. A straight line in time is then removed, and the template is
scaled so that its lowest value is $-1$. Its size therefore passes to the
pixel amplitudes. Every pixel is fitted, with Huber weights, as
\begin{equation}
 Y_{tp} = A_{pg}\,\phi_{o}(t) + \ell_p + \kappa_p\,\tau_t + \varepsilon_{tp},
 \label{eq:qstar-fit}
\end{equation}
where $A_{pg}$ is the amplitude that follows the transit,
$\ell_p+\kappa_p\tau_t$ is a straight line in time and $\varepsilon_{tp}$ is
the residual. The time $\tau_t$ is centred and scaled on the out-of-transit
integrations. The profile is $q^\star_{pg}=A_{pg}/\sum_{p'\in P_g}A_{p'g}$.
The depth, nearly the same for all pixels of a column, cancels in this ratio.
The response is built from this profile. The normalised stellar term of
equation~(\ref{eq:nova-forward}) is not identical to $q^\star_{pg}$, because
it also weights by the kernel and the reference spectrum.

Each amplitude rests on a dip of only 1.5 to 2\%, so I smooth the profile
across columns. The smoothing links pixels in the same row of the column
windows, which are 36 rows tall in order~1 and 35 in order~2. Its strength is
the one at which the smoothed profile from alternate integrations best
predicts the unsmoothed profile from the others. Two checks follow, and each
must hold separately in at least 95\% of the columns. The smoothed profiles
from the two halves must place at most 15\% of the starlight differently, and
their centres, the profile-weighted mean rows, must agree within 0.5~pixel.
The window moves by whole rows, about every 24 columns in order~1 and every 5
in order~2, whereas the trace moves smoothly. The centre of the smoothed
profile therefore tends to jump by about a pixel at each shift.

An earlier test on simulated data with known starlight (August 2026), which
reached further to the red than the present cut, found that the model put 0.8
to 1.2\% too much transiting starlight in the reddest fitted columns of
order~1, and depths 1.7 to 2.0\% too shallow there. The profile is still
estimated in the same way, so this bias is not resolved. Its size and sign for
the real visit are not known, and the benchmark of
Section~\ref{sec:measured-benchmark} is designed to measure them.

\subsection{The continuum, the background and the noise}
\label{sec:continuum}

The continuum of each group is linear in time,
$C_{tg}=\beta_{0g}+\beta_{1g}\tau_t$. The transit lies between the two
out-of-transit windows, so a slight bend in the baseline would leak into the
depth. I therefore measure one small bend, common to all columns, from the
out-of-transit integrations of each input, and hold it fixed. For each order,
I subtract the expected background from the out-of-transit trace flux, fit a
parabola and a straight line to it in time, and divide the parabola by the
line. The factor $\gamma_t$ is the geometric mean of the two orders' ratios,
normalised to unit mean out of transit. A free quadratic term in each column
would be fitted together with the transit and could trade against that
column's depth. A single bend measured outside the transit cannot. The
correction $\gamma_t$ is used only if it stays within 0.1\% of unity, and if
versions from alternate out-of-transit integrations correlate by at least 0.95
and their mean values during the transit differ by at most 250~ppm. Its
uncertainty is not propagated, and the white-light fit does not use it.

The background is a sum of eight fixed spatial maps $\Phi_{pk}$. Each map has
a constant amplitude $\alpha_{k0}$ and an amplitude $\alpha_{k1}$ that follows
a time pattern $G_1(t)$,
\begin{equation}
 B_{tp}=\sum_{k=1}^{8}\Phi_{pk}
 \left[\alpha_{k0}+\alpha_{k1}\,G_1(t)\right].
 \label{eq:background-model}
\end{equation}
The maps and the 244,657 off-trace pixels were chosen once, from the
out-of-transit integrations of the WASP-17 visit, and are reused for every
input. The maps are built from the zodiacal-light template that Stage~2
subtracted, plane~10 of the CRDS file \texttt{jwst\_niriss\_bkg\_0033}, and
the polynomials $1$, $y$, $x$, $xy$, $y^2$, $x^2$ and $xy^2$ in scaled
detector coordinates, which follow smooth gradients. These eight are centred
and scaled, keeping the constant, and made orthonormal over the off-trace
pixels. Eight maps predicted held-out, trace-shaped strips of an off-trace
image better than two or four maps did.

From each input, a least-squares fit of the maps to the off-trace pixels of
every integration gives eight amplitude series. The fit weights each pixel by
its inverse squared pipeline uncertainty ERR. The pattern $G_1$ is the first
principal component of these series whose absolute correlation with the mean
of the templates $\phi_o$ is at most 0.25. This limits how much
transit-shaped variation the background can absorb. On the real visit, the
criterion excludes the leading component, whose correlation is 0.95. Whether
that signal is starlight is the question of Section~\ref{sec:which-light}.
Finally, one weighted fit of all sixteen amplitudes to all off-trace data
gives their expected values $\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{off}}$, and the normal
matrix of this fit is the precision matrix $\bm{N}_{\mathrm{off}}$. The anchor
term of the objective (equation~\ref{eq:objective}) therefore acts as the
$\chi^2$ of the off-trace pixels. The background is refitted at every step,
but held close to what those pixels show.

Each fitted sample has the uncertainty
$\sigma_{tp}=\mathrm{ERR}_{tp}\,N_{p}\,s_{p}$. The scale $N_p$ mixes two fixed
order scales, $N_1=3.07$ and $N_2=4.19$, in proportion to the pixel's
calibration response to each order, taken before the response is restricted to
the pixel's own order. Where both responses are zero, $N_p=4.19$. These two
factors were derived from earlier reductions of the out-of-transit data of the
WASP-17 visit, and they include model mismatch as well as excess noise. The
pixel scale $s_p\geq1$, estimated from each input, catches single pixels that
are noisier than the rest of their order. Within each out-of-transit window,
alternate integrations form two halves, and a weighted straight line in time
fitted to either half predicts the other. The scale $s_p$ is the larger of 1 and the
combined root-mean-square prediction error in units of $\mathrm{ERR}\,N_p$,
with four fitted degrees of freedom removed.

\subsection{The objective and its minimisation}

The standardised residual of each sample is
\begin{equation}
 r_{tp}=\frac{Y_{tp}-\widehat Y_{tp}
 (D,\bm{u},\bm{\beta},\bm{\alpha};\,\gamma,R,s,\Omega,\Phi,G_1)}{\sigma_{tp}},
 \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
with the fitted parameters before the semicolon and the fixed inputs after it.
The NOVA estimate is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
 &(\widehat D,\widehat{\bm{u}},\widehat{\bm{\beta}},
  \widehat{\bm{\alpha}})
 = \arg\min\Big\{ \\
 &\quad\sum_{(t,p)\in\mathcal{M}}\rho_{1.345}(r_{tp}) \\
 &\quad+\tfrac{1}{2}(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{off}})^{\mathsf T}
   \bm{N}_{\mathrm{off}}(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{off}}) \\
 &\quad+\tfrac{1}{2}\|\bm{r}_{\mathrm{LD,theory}}(\bm{u})\|_2^2
  +\tfrac{1}{2}\|\bm{r}_{\mathrm{LD,curv}}(\bm{u})\|_2^2
 \Big\},
\end{split}
 \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{M}$ is the set of fitted samples and $\rho_{1.345}$ is the
Huber loss, which is quadratic for $|r_{tp}|\leq1.345$ and linear beyond, so
that outliers do not dominate. The second term is the background anchor, and
the last two are the limb-darkening penalties.

The prediction is linear in the continuum and background coefficients
$(\bm{\beta},\bm{\alpha})$, and nonlinear in $\bm{\theta}$, which holds 166
numbers: the mean depth, 157 shape coordinates and eight limb-darkening
coefficients. NOVA minimises in three nested loops. In the innermost loop,
with $\bm{\theta}$ and the Huber weights $w_{tp}$ fixed, variable projection
\citep{GolubPereyra1973} solves exactly for the linear coefficients, with
weights $w_{tp}/\sigma_{tp}^2$ and the background anchor. Each group has its
own two continuum coefficients, so the groups are coupled only through the
sixteen background amplitudes. The derivatives with respect to $\bm{\theta}$
are exact: JAX differentiates the transit model automatically, and the
derivative of the linear solution is analytic. In the middle loop, the
trust-region reflective method \citep{BranchColemanLi1999},  adjusts $\bm{\theta}$. It takes steps within a
region where a quadratic approximation of the objective is trusted, and keeps
the mean depth positive. In the outer loop, the weights
$w_{tp}=\min(1,\,1.345/|r_{tp}|)$ \citep{Huber1964} are updated.

Each cycle uses the weights from the cycle before, whereas the Huber solution
must reproduce its own weights. A start is therefore accepted only after two
successive cycles that each pass the following tests. No weight changes by
0.001 or more. The objective changes by less than a relative $10^{-8}$. The
spectrum changes by less than 0.01~ppm RMS on a 40-bin grid and 0.05~ppm RMS
on a 96-bin grid. The optimiser reports success, the parameters stay within
their bounds and pass the physical checks, and the rank of the linear solve
does not change. 

NOVA runs three starts and keeps the accepted one with the lowest objective.
The first start has the white-light
depth and the reference limb darkening. The second adds a 250~ppm wave in
log-wavelength and small limb-darkening shifts, fixed in advance for every
input. The third lies halfway between them.

\subsection{White-light geometry inference}
\label{sec:own-white}

The spectral fit holds the orbital geometry fixed at the result of a joint
white-light fit to the same input. This fit infers $t_0$, $a/R_\star$ and the
inclination $i$. Each order's white-light curve is the sum of the light of its
columns. Each column is estimated by optimal extraction \citep{Horne1986} with
a fixed profile: each pixel's median out-of-transit count rate in this input,
divided by the sum of these medians in its column. The two curves are fitted
together with the light-curve stage of Eureka! \citep{BellEtAl2022}. They
share $t_0$, $a/R_\star$ and $i$. Each order has its own radius ratio $k_o$,
quadratic limb darkening with uniform priors from 0 to 1, linear baseline and
white-noise multiplier. The geometry has broad uniform priors: $t_0$ anywhere
in the observed window, $a/R_\star$ from 1.01, just outside the star, to 100,
and $i$ from $0^\circ$ to $90^\circ$. The posterior is sampled with dynesty
\citep{Speagle2020}.

For a circular orbit, $b=(a/R_\star)\cos i$ is the distance between the
centres of planet and star at mid-transit, in stellar radii. In samples with
\begin{equation}
 b > 1-\max(k_1,\,k_2),
 \label{eq:grazing}
\end{equation}
the planet's disc is not wholly inside the stellar disc in at least one order,
and depth and geometry trade off. Two conditions come first. The sampler must
have stopped with a remaining $\Delta\log Z\leq0.01$, and the 95\% interval of
the posterior weight of these samples must have a half-width of at most one
percentage point. If both hold, the geometry is accepted when the upper end of
this interval is below 1\%, and accepted with the weight reported when the
upper end is at least 1\% but below 5\%.

\subsection{The uncertainty ensemble}
\label{sec:ensemble}

I plan to estimate the effect of uncertain calibration products and orbital
geometry by repeating the analysis on an ensemble of simulated observations.
This uncertainty estimate has not yet been validated. The error bars shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:nova-real} remain conditional on the fitted calibration
products and geometry.

\subsection{A first spectrum of WASP-17~b}
\label{sec:nova-real}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/nova_vs_published_wasp17_preliminary.pdf}
  \caption{Preliminary \nova{} transmission spectrum of the real WASP-17~b
  visit (blue) and the published Ahsoka spectrum of \citet{LouieEtAl2025} at
  a resolving power of 100 (orange; orders 1 and 2 shown separately). The
  \nova{} error bars are conditional on the fitted calibration products and
  geometry, and do not yet include the uncertainty from the planned ensemble.
  All points of both spectra are shown.}
  \label{fig:nova-real}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:nova-real} compares a preliminary NOVA spectrum of the real
WASP-17~b visit, from a reduction of 18 September 2026, with the published
Ahsoka spectrum of \citet{LouieEtAl2025}. This reduction used NOVA's own joint
white-light geometry, but it was fitted before the criterion of
Section~\ref{sec:own-white} was written. Below about $1.8\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ the
two spectra show similar features, such as the water bands, but the NOVA
spectrum is deeper throughout. With the published spectrum interpolated to the
NOVA bins, NOVA is deeper by a median of about 230~ppm below
$1.6\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, 550~ppm between 1.8 and $2.3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, and
490~ppm above $2.3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$.

Different geometry, limb-darkening and background treatments may all
contribute to this difference, and this comparison does not separate them. The
larger difference in the red matters most, because there the starlight is
faintest and the background matters most. The real data cannot decide whether
NOVA or the published reduction is closer to the truth. Separate injection
tests also found nonzero biases of NOVA, but these cannot be subtracted from
the difference on real data. This motivates the benchmark of the next two
sections, which is designed to measure the errors of each method when the
injected truth is known.
