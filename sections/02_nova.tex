\section{NOVA}
\label{sec:nova}

NOVA infers the transmission spectrum from the detector pixels themselves, so
that the split between starlight and background is decided in the same fit as
the transit. A single forward model maps the spectrum $D(\lambda)$, the limb
darkening $\bm{u}(\lambda)$, the stellar brightness and the additive
background onto the fitted detector samples, and all four are estimated by
minimising one objective. The other ingredients are held fixed. The spatial
profile, a slow bend of the baseline, the temporal pattern of the background
and its expected amplitudes, a noise scale for each pixel and the orbital
geometry are estimated beforehand from each input. The depth bins, the
limb-darkening reference, the apertures, the off-trace pixels, the background
maps and two noise scales are the same for every input.

In this section, I describe NOVA as currently implemented and end with a
first, preliminary spectrum of the real WASP-17~b visit.

\subsection{Detector processing}
\label{sec:detector-processing}

NOVA's input is prepared from the raw ramps with the JWST calibration pipeline
\citep{JWSTPipeline2025} and exoTEDRF \citep{Radica2024}. After the JWST group
steps, up to and including the reference-pixel correction, exoTEDRF removes
the $1/f$ noise from each group, once only, by subtracting the median of each
column outside the trace cores. Field stars found in the F277W exposure are
left out of these medians. The linearity, jump-detection, ramp-fitting and
gain steps give count rates. Stage~2 of exoTEDRF applies the flat field,
subtracts a scaled zodiacal background model and corrects bad pixels. I apply
no further mask to the scene.

\subsection{The detector forward model}
\label{sec:forward}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/nova_schematic.pdf}
  \caption{Schematic of the detector forward model of \nova{}. (a) The
  fitted pixels are grouped by order and column (a sketch, not data), and the two orders do not overlap. (b) The count rate of each
  pixel is modelled as starlight, dimmed by the transit, plus a background
  that is not dimmed. The spectrum is shared by both orders, whilst the limb
  darkening is fitted for each order as penalised deviations from a
  theoretical reference.}
  \label{fig:nova-schematic}
\end{figure}

Within one detector column, the pixels of a trace see nearly the same
wavelength. NOVA therefore organises the samples into non-overlapping groups
$g$, each containing the pixels $P_g$ of one order in one column
(Figure~\ref{fig:nova-schematic}). It fits the pixels of two fixed
apertures along the traces. Four edge columns, the 51 reddest columns of
order~1, which the wavelength calibration does not directly support, and
flagged samples are left out. Denoting by $Y_{tp}$ the calibrated count rate
of pixel $p$ in integration $t$, NOVA predicts
\begin{equation}
 \widehat Y_{tp}
 = \gamma_t\, C_{tg}\,
   \frac{\sum_{\lambda} R_{p\lambda}\, s_\lambda\,
         \mathcal{T}_{t}(\lambda)}
        {\sum_{p'\in P_g}\sum_{\lambda} R_{p'\lambda}\, s_\lambda}
 + B_{tp},
 \label{eq:nova-forward}
\end{equation}
where $C_{tg}$ is the stellar brightness of the group $g$ of pixel $p$ at time
$t$, $\gamma_t$ is a fixed correction for slow curvature of the baseline
(Section~\ref{sec:continuum}) and $B_{tp}$ is the additive background left
after Stage~2. The response matrix $R_{p\lambda}$ is the count rate in pixel
$p$ per unit stellar flux at wavelength $\lambda$, and $s_\lambda$ is a fixed
reference spectrum of the star. Each pixel takes its response from its own
order only, so the light of the other order is not modelled in it. The
transit factor $\mathcal{T}_t(\lambda)$ dims the light at $\lambda$ according
to the depth $D$ of its bin, the limb darkening $\bm{u}$ of the order and the
orbital state $\Omega$. Each pixel is therefore dimmed by its own
response-weighted mix of wavelengths. Only the stellar term is dimmed, so light
assigned to the background does not dim.

The line-spread kernel describes how the optics spread light of one wavelength
over a few neighbouring columns. Because $R_{p\lambda}$ contains the kernel,
each pixel sees a weighted mix of nearby wavelengths, and the fitted depths
are not smeared if $R_{p\lambda}$ is correct. NOVA builds $R_{p\lambda}$ for
each input with the SOSS extraction code of the JWST pipeline
\citep{DarveauBernierEtAl2022,JWSTPipeline2025}, from the public trace and
wavelength calibration \citep{BainesEtAl2023Trace,BainesEtAl2023Wavelength},
kernel and throughput, and its own spatial profile
(Section~\ref{sec:response}).

\subsection{The shared spectrum and transit model}

A planet has one transmission spectrum, whichever order records its light, so
both orders share $D(\lambda)$. It is represented by $K=160$ top-hat bins from
0.62 to $2.84\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, 0.0097 to $0.0100\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ wide below
$2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ and $0.0382\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ above, except the bluest, which
merges two bins. The two reddest bins, above $2.764\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, receive
no light in the model, because order~2 does not reach them and order~1 is cut
at $2.758\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, where its direct calibration ends. They are held at
the white-light depth and not reported. The other 158 depths, the supported
set $I$, are
\begin{equation}
  D_j = \bar D\,
  \frac{\exp(h_j)}{\sum_{k\in I} w_k \exp(h_k)}, \qquad \bm{h}=\bm{H}\bm{z},
  \qquad j\in I,
  \label{eq:depth-basis}
\end{equation}
where $\bar D>0$ is their width-weighted mean, $w_k$ are the bin widths
normalised over $I$ and $\bm{z}$ are 157 free shape coordinates. The fixed $158\times157$
matrix $\bm{H}$, from a QR decomposition of a vector of ones, has orthonormal
columns that each sum to zero. Adding a constant to every $h_j$ would leave
the depths unchanged, and $\bm{H}$ excludes this direction, so each spectrum
has one set of coordinates. The fit varies $\bar D$ and $\bm{z}$. There is no
atmospheric template, no smoothing between wavelengths, no prior on the shape
and no term that lets the depths of the two orders differ.

The orbital state $\Omega$ is a circular orbit with the period of
\citet{LouieEtAl2025} and the mid-transit time $t_0$, scaled semi-major axis
$a/R_\star$ and impact parameter $b$ from NOVA's white-light fit. The transit
$\mathcal{T}_t$ is averaged over each integration of about 44~s and computed
with jaxoplanet \citep{Jaxoplanet2025}.

The limb darkening follows the quadratic law \citep{MandelAgol2002}, in the
coordinates $q_1,q_2\in[0,1]$ of \citet{Kipping2013},
\begin{equation}
 u_1=2\sqrt{q_1}\,q_2, \qquad
 u_2=\sqrt{q_1}\,(1-2q_2).
 \label{eq:kipping-coordinates}
\end{equation}
Limb darkening and depth are partly degenerate. The spectral fit therefore
allows only a smooth deviation from a reference computed from STAGGER
intensities \citep{MagicEtAl2015}. For each order, the logits of $q_1$ and
$q_2$ may differ from the reference by an offset and a slope in normalised
log-wavelength, eight numbers in total. A line in log-wavelength allows the
same change per factor in wavelength across order~1, which spans a factor of
3.3. The deviation is penalised for departing from the reference and for its
curvature in wavelength, which here only restrains the slope, mainly in
order~2. The white-light fit leaves the limb darkening free, because limb
darkening shapes the ingress, egress and bottom of the transit partly as
$a/R_\star$ and $b$ do, and a wrong reference would bias the geometry.

\subsection{The empirical spatial response}
\label{sec:response}

The response matrix needs the spatial profile, the share of a column's
starlight that falls on each of its pixels. Because the starlight dims during
the transit whilst the background does not, the part of a pixel's time series
that follows the transit measures its starlight. This assumes that only
starlight varies like the transit. For each order $o$, I first build a
template $\phi_o(t)$ of the transit shape. It is the median fractional
departure from a straight-line out-of-transit baseline, over the brightest
65\% of the order's valid aperture pixels. After a straight line in time is
removed, the template is scaled to a lowest value of $-1$, so its size passes
to the pixel amplitudes. Every pixel is fitted, with Huber weights, as
\begin{equation}
 Y_{tp} = A_{pg}\,\phi_{o}(t) + \ell_p + \kappa_p\,\tau_t + \varepsilon_{tp},
 \label{eq:qstar-fit}
\end{equation}
where $A_{pg}$ is the transit-correlated amplitude and $\varepsilon_{tp}$ is
the residual. The term $\ell_p+\kappa_p\tau_t$ is a straight line in $\tau_t$,
the time centred and scaled on the out-of-transit integrations. The profile is
$q^\star_{pg}=A_{pg}/\sum_{p'\in P_g}A_{p'g}$, in which the depth, nearly the
same for all pixels of a column, cancels. The response matrix is built from
this profile, and the normalised stellar term of
equation~(\ref{eq:nova-forward}) is not identical to $q^\star_{pg}$, because
it also weights by the kernel and the reference spectrum.

A single amplitude rests on a dip of only 1.5 to 2\%. I therefore smooth the
profile across columns, linking pixels in the same row of the column windows
of 36 rows in order~1 and 35 in order~2. The strength is the one at which the
smoothed profile from alternate integrations best predicts the unsmoothed
profile from the others. In 95\% of the columns, these two smoothed profiles
must then place at most 15\% of the starlight differently, and their centres,
the profile-weighted mean rows, must agree within 0.5~pixel. The window shifts
by whole rows, about every 24 columns in order~1 and every 5 in order~2,
whilst the trace moves smoothly. The centre of the smoothed profile therefore
tends to jump by about a pixel at each shift. An earlier test on simulated
data with known starlight (August 2026), whose red end reached beyond the
present cut, found that the model put 0.8 to 1.2\% too much transiting
starlight in the reddest fitted columns of order~1, and depths 1.7 to 2.0\%
too shallow there. The profile is estimated in the same way today, so
this bias is not resolved. Its size and sign for the real visit have not been
established, and the benchmark of Section~\ref{sec:measured-benchmark} is
designed to measure them.

\subsection{The continuum, the background and the noise}
\label{sec:continuum}

The continuum of each group is linear in time,
$C_{tg}=\beta_{0g}+\beta_{1g}\tau_t$. The transit lies between the two
out-of-transit windows, so a slight bend in the baseline would pass into the
depth. I therefore measure one small bend, common to all columns, from the
out-of-transit integrations of each input and hold it fixed. For each order, I
divide a parabola by a straight line, both fitted in time to the
out-of-transit trace flux, and $\gamma_t$ is the geometric mean of the two
orders' ratios, normalised to unit mean out of transit. A free quadratic term
in each column would be fitted with the transit and could trade against the
depth of that column, whereas a single bend measured outside the transit
cannot. The correction $\gamma_t$ is used only if versions from the odd- and
even-numbered out-of-transit integrations correlate by at least 0.95 and agree
within 250~ppm in transit, and if it stays within 0.1\% of unity. Its
uncertainty is not propagated, and the white-light fit does not use it.

The background is a sum of eight fixed spatial maps $\Phi_{pk}$, each with a
constant amplitude $\alpha_{k0}$ and one amplitude, $\alpha_{k1}$, that
follows a temporal pattern $G_1(t)$,
\begin{equation}
 B_{tp}=\sum_{k=1}^{8}\Phi_{pk}
 \left[\alpha_{k0}+\alpha_{k1}\,G_1(t)\right].
 \label{eq:background-model}
\end{equation}
The maps and the 244,657 off-trace pixels were chosen once, from the
out-of-transit integrations of the WASP-17 visit, and are reused for every
input.
The first map is the zodiacal-light template that Stage~2 subtracted, plane~10
of the CRDS file \texttt{jwst\_niriss\_bkg\_0033}. The other seven are the
polynomials $1$, $y$, $x$, $xy$, $y^2$, $x^2$ and $xy^2$ in detector
coordinates, which follow smooth gradients. Eight maps predicted held-out,
trace-shaped strips of an off-trace image better than two or four. From each
input, a least-squares fit of the maps to the off-trace pixels of every
integration, weighted by the inverse squared pipeline uncertainty ERR, gives
eight amplitude series. The pattern $G_1$ is their first principal component
whose absolute correlation with the mean of the templates $\phi_o$ is at most
0.25, which limits how much transit-shaped variation the background can
absorb. On the real visit, the criterion keeps out the leading component,
whose correlation is 0.95. Whether that signal is starlight is the question of
Section~\ref{sec:which-light}. Finally, one weighted fit of all sixteen
amplitudes to all off-trace data gives their expected values
$\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{off}}$, and the normal matrix of this fit is the
precision matrix $\bm{N}_{\mathrm{off}}$. The anchor term thus acts as the
$\chi^2$ of the off-trace pixels. The background is refitted at every step,
but held close to what they show.

Each fitted sample has the uncertainty
$\sigma_{tp}=\mathrm{ERR}_{tp}\,N_{p}\,s_{p}$, where ERR is the pipeline
uncertainty. The scale $N_p$ mixes two fixed order scales, $N_1=3.07$ and
$N_2=4.19$, in proportion to the pixel's response to each order. These
empirical factors were derived from earlier reductions of the out-of-transit
data of the WASP-17 visit, and they include model mismatch as well as excess
noise. The pixel scale $s_p\geq1$, from each input, catches single pixels
noisier than the rest of their order. It is the root-mean-square error, in
units of $\mathrm{ERR}\,N_p$, with which a straight line in time fitted to
alternate out-of-transit integrations predicts the others, or 1 if smaller.

\subsection{The objective and its minimisation}

The standardised residual of each sample is
\begin{equation}
 r_{tp}=\frac{Y_{tp}-\widehat Y_{tp}
 (D,\bm{u},\bm{\beta},\bm{\alpha};\,\gamma,R,s,\Omega,\Phi,G_1)}{\sigma_{tp}},
 \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
with the fitted parameters before the semicolon and the fixed inputs after it.
The NOVA estimate is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
 &(\widehat D,\widehat{\bm{u}},\widehat{\bm{\beta}},
  \widehat{\bm{\alpha}})
 = \arg\min\Big\{ \\
 &\quad\sum_{(t,p)\in\mathcal{M}}\rho_{1.345}(r_{tp}) \\
 &\quad+\tfrac{1}{2}(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{off}})^{\mathsf T}
   \bm{N}_{\mathrm{off}}(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{off}}) \\
 &\quad+\tfrac{1}{2}\|\bm{r}_{\mathrm{LD,theory}}(\bm{u})\|_2^2
  +\tfrac{1}{2}\|\bm{r}_{\mathrm{LD,curv}}(\bm{u})\|_2^2
 \Big\},
\end{split}
 \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{M}$ is the set of fitted samples and $\rho_{1.345}$ is the
Huber loss, quadratic for $|r_{tp}|\leq1.345$ and linear beyond, so that
outliers do not dominate. The last two terms are the limb-darkening penalties.

The prediction is linear in the continuum and background coefficients. NOVA
therefore separates this linear block $(\bm{\beta},\bm{\alpha})$ from a
nonlinear block $\bm{\theta}$ of 166 numbers, the mean depth, 157 shape
coordinates and eight limb-darkening coefficients, and minimises in three
nested loops. In the innermost, with $\bm{\theta}$ and the Huber weights
$w_{tp}$ fixed, variable projection \citep{GolubPereyra1973} solves exactly
for the linear block, with weights $w_{tp}/\sigma_{tp}^2$ and the background
anchor. Each group has its own two continuum coefficients, so the groups are
coupled only through the sixteen background amplitudes. The exact derivatives with respect to $\bm{\theta}$ combine automatic
differentiation of the transit model with JAX and the analytic derivative of
the linear solution. In the middle loop, the trust-region reflective method
\citep{BranchColemanLi1999}, the default least-squares method of SciPy,
adjusts $\bm{\theta}$. It steps within a region where a quadratic
approximation of the objective is trusted, and keeps the mean depth positive.
In the outer loop, the weights $w_{tp}=\min(1,\,1.345/|r_{tp}|)$
\citep{Huber1964} are updated.

Each cycle uses the weights from the cycle before, whereas the Huber solution
reproduces its own weights. A start is therefore accepted only after two
successive cycles in each of which the weights change by less than 0.001, the
objective by a relative $10^{-8}$ and the spectrum, in 40 bins, by 0.01~ppm
RMS. For each parameter, the gradient of the objective, scaled and projected
onto the bounds, must also be at most $10^{-6}$ or at most 0.002 times the
norm of the standardised residuals. This is a numerical tolerance, not a proof
that no better minimum exists. NOVA runs three starts and keeps the accepted
one with the lowest objective. No check compares the three solutions. The first has the
white-light depth and the reference limb darkening. The second adds a 250~ppm
wave in log-wavelength and small limb-darkening shifts, fixed in advance for
every input, and the third lies halfway between.

\subsection{White-light geometry inference}
\label{sec:own-white}

The spectral fit holds the orbital geometry fixed at the result of a joint
white-light fit to the same input, which infers $t_0$, $a/R_\star$ and the
inclination $i$. Each order's white-light curve sums the light of its columns,
each estimated by optimal extraction with a fixed profile \citep{Horne1986}.
This profile is each pixel's median out-of-transit count rate in this input,
divided by the sum of these medians in its column. The two curves are fitted
jointly with the light-curve stage of Eureka! \citep{BellEtAl2022}. They share
$t_0$, $a/R_\star$ and $i$, whilst each order has its own radius ratio $k_o$,
quadratic limb darkening with uniform priors from 0 to 1, linear baseline and
white-noise multiplier. The geometry priors are flat and wide, so that these
data alone set the geometry, with $t_0$ anywhere in the observed window,
$a/R_\star$ from 1.01, just outside the star, to 100, and $i$ from $0^\circ$
to $90^\circ$. The posterior is sampled with dynesty \citep{Speagle2020}.

For a circular orbit, $b=(a/R_\star)\cos i$ is the distance between the
centres of planet and star at mid-transit, in stellar radii. In samples with
\begin{equation}
 b > 1-\max(k_1,\,k_2),
 \label{eq:grazing}
\end{equation}
the planet's disc is not wholly inside the stellar disc in at least one order,
and depth and geometry trade off. The geometry is accepted if the upper end of
the 95\% interval of the posterior weight of these samples is below 1\%, and
accepted with the weight reported if it lies between 1 and 5\%.

\subsection{The uncertainty ensemble}
\label{sec:ensemble}

I plan to estimate the effect of uncertain calibration products and orbital
geometry by repeating the analysis on an ensemble of simulated observations.
This uncertainty estimate has not yet been validated. The error bars shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:nova-real} remain conditional on the fitted calibration
products and geometry.

\subsection{A first spectrum of WASP-17~b}
\label{sec:nova-real}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/nova_vs_published_wasp17_preliminary.pdf}
  \caption{Preliminary \nova{} transmission spectrum of the real WASP-17~b
  visit (blue) and the published Ahsoka spectrum of \citet{LouieEtAl2025} at
  a resolving power of 100 (orange; orders 1 and 2 shown separately). The
  \nova{} error bars are conditional on the fitted calibration products and
  geometry, and do not yet include the uncertainty from the ensemble. All points of both spectra are shown.}
  \label{fig:nova-real}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:nova-real} compares a preliminary NOVA spectrum of the real
WASP-17~b visit, from a reduction of 18 September 2026, with the published
Ahsoka spectrum of \citet{LouieEtAl2025}. This reduction used NOVA's own joint
white-light geometry but was fitted before the criterion of
Section~\ref{sec:own-white} was written. Below about $1.8\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ the
two spectra show similar features, such as the water bands, but the NOVA
spectrum is deeper throughout. Interpolating the published spectrum to the
NOVA bins, the median difference is about 230~ppm below $1.6\,\mu\mathrm{m}$,
550~ppm between 1.8 and $2.3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, and 490~ppm above
$2.3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$.

Different geometry, limb-darkening and background treatments may all
contribute to this difference, and this comparison does not isolate them. The
larger difference in the red matters most, since it lies where the starlight
is faintest and the background is most important. Whether NOVA or the
published reduction is closer to the truth cannot be decided from the real
data. Separate injection tests also found nonzero biases of NOVA, but these
cannot be subtracted from the difference on real data. This motivates the
benchmark of the next two sections, which is designed to measure the errors of
the methods when the injected truth is known.
