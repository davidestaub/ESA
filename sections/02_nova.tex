\section{NOVA}
\label{sec:nova}

NOVA fits the transmission spectrum directly to the detector pixels. Its
model describes every fitted pixel as starlight, dimmed by the transit, plus
background, so the split between the two is decided in the same fit as the
transit. The fit estimates the spectrum, the limb darkening, the brightness of
the star in each column and the background together. Everything else is fixed.
Some of them are estimated beforehand from the same data: the spatial
profile, the stellar spectrum, the curvature of the baseline, the time pattern
of the background, a noise scale for each pixel and the orbital geometry. The
others are the same for every data set: the depth bins, the limb-darkening
reference, the apertures, the background maps and two noise scales. In this
section, I describe NOVA as it is currently implemented, and end with a first,
preliminary spectrum of WASP-17~b.

\subsection{Detector processing}
\label{sec:detector-processing}

NOVA's input is prepared from the raw ramps with the JWST calibration pipeline
\citep{JWSTPipeline2025} and exoTEDRF \citep{Radica2024}. After the
reference-pixel correction, exoTEDRF removes the $1/f$ noise from each group
by subtracting the median of each column outside the trace cores, leaving out
field stars found in the F277W exposure. For this step only, it subtracts a
scaled background model beforehand and adds it back afterwards. The remaining
steps of the JWST pipeline give count rates. Stage~2 of exoTEDRF then applies
the flat field, subtracts the scaled zodiacal background and corrects bad
pixels. I add no further mask.

\subsection{The detector model}
\label{sec:forward}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/nova_schematic.pdf}
  \caption{Schematic of the detector model of \nova{}. (a) The fitted pixels
  are grouped by order and column (a sketch, not data), and the two orders do
  not overlap. (b) The count rate of each pixel is modelled as starlight,
  dimmed by the transit, plus a background that is not dimmed. The spectrum is
  shared by both orders, whilst the limb darkening is fitted for each order as
  penalised deviations from a theoretical reference.}
  \label{fig:nova-schematic}
\end{figure}

Within one detector column, the pixels of a trace see nearly the same
wavelength. NOVA therefore groups the pixels by order and column: a group $g$
holds the pixels $P_g$ of one order in one column
(Figure~\ref{fig:nova-schematic}). The fitted pixels lie in two fixed
apertures along the traces. Four edge columns, the 51 reddest columns of
order~1 and flagged samples are left out. For the count rate $Y_{tp}$ of pixel
$p$ in integration $t$, NOVA predicts
\begin{equation}
 \widehat Y_{tp}
 = \gamma_t\, C_{tg}\, \bar{\mathcal{T}}_{tp}\, \tilde q_{pg} + B_{tp},
 \label{eq:nova-forward}
\end{equation}
where $C_{tg}$ is the brightness of the star in the group of pixel $p$,
$\tilde q_{pg}$ is the share of that light that falls on pixel $p$,
$\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{tp}$ is the dimming of pixel $p$ by the transit,
$\gamma_t$ is a small, fixed curvature of the baseline and $B_{tp}$ is the
background left after Stage~2. Only the starlight is dimmed.

To know which wavelengths reach which pixel, NOVA uses ATOCA, the SOSS
extraction code of the JWST pipeline
\citep{DarveauBernierEtAl2022,JWSTPipeline2025}, with the public trace and
wavelength calibration \citep{BainesEtAl2023Trace,BainesEtAl2023Wavelength},
line-spread kernel and throughput, and NOVA's own spatial profile
(Section~\ref{sec:response}). This gives $K_{p\lambda}$, the light of
wavelength $\lambda$ that reaches pixel $p$ per unit of profile. It includes
the line-spread kernel, which spreads each wavelength over a few neighbouring
columns, and each pixel receives light from its own order only. The transit
factor of a pixel is the transit model averaged over the light that reaches
it,
\begin{equation}
 \bar{\mathcal{T}}_{tp}
 = \frac{\sum_{\lambda} K_{p\lambda}\, s_\lambda\, \mathcal{T}_t(\lambda)}
        {\sum_{\lambda} K_{p\lambda}\, s_\lambda},
 \label{eq:pixel-transit}
\end{equation}
where $s_\lambda$ is the spectrum of the star, fitted to the out-of-transit
data of each input, and $\mathcal{T}_t(\lambda)$ is the transit at wavelength
$\lambda$. The line-spread kernel is therefore part of the model.

\subsection{The spectrum and the transit}

A planet has one transmission spectrum, so both orders share $D(\lambda)$. It
is represented by 160 top-hat bins from 0.62 to $2.84\,\mu\mathrm{m}$. Below
$2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ the bins are about $0.01\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ wide and above it
$0.038\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, wider in the red, where the signal-to-noise ratio is
lower. The bluest bin is twice as wide because the bin
next to it received no light. The two reddest bins also receive no light in
the model: order~2 does not reach them, and order~1 is cut at
$2.758\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, beyond which the public PASTASOSS calibration would
have to be extrapolated. They are held at the white-light depth and not
reported. I have not yet tested how the choice of bin widths affects the
spectrum. The other 158 depths are written as an overall level times a shape,
\begin{equation}
  D_j = \bar D\,
  \frac{\exp(h_j)}{\sum_{k} w_k \exp(h_k)}, \qquad \bm{h}=\bm{H}\bm{z},
  \label{eq:depth-basis}
\end{equation}
where $\bar D$ is the overall, achromatic depth and $w_k$ are the bin widths,
normalised to sum to one, so that $\bar D$ is the width-weighted mean of the
depths. The exponential keeps every depth positive. Adding the same constant to
every $h_j$ would not change the spectrum, so the fixed $158\times157$ matrix
$\bm{H}$ removes this direction. Its columns are orthonormal and each sums to
zero, and it is built by a QR decomposition of a vector of ones. The fit
varies $\bar D$ and the 157 shape coordinates $\bm{z}$.

The orbit is circular, with the period of \citet{LouieEtAl2025} and with the
mid-transit time $t_0$, scaled semi-major axis $a/R_\star$ and impact
parameter $b$ from NOVA's white-light fit (Section~\ref{sec:own-white}). The
transit is computed with jaxoplanet \citep{Jaxoplanet2025} and averaged over
each integration.

The limb darkening follows the quadratic law \citep{MandelAgol2002}, in the
coordinates $q_1,q_2\in[0,1]$ of \citet{Kipping2013},
\begin{equation}
 u_1=2\sqrt{q_1}\,q_2, \qquad
 u_2=\sqrt{q_1}\,(1-2q_2).
 \label{eq:kipping-coordinates}
\end{equation}
Limb darkening and depth are partly degenerate, so the spectral fit lets the
limb darkening deviate only smoothly from a reference computed from STAGGER
model intensities \citep{MagicEtAl2015}. For each order, the logit of each
$q$ may change by an offset and a slope in log-wavelength, and both are
penalised. The logit keeps $q$ between 0 and 1. A slope in log-wavelength
means the same change for each factor in wavelength, so it treats the blue and
red ends of order~1, a factor of 3.3 apart, alike. The white-light fit, by
contrast, leaves $u_1$ and $u_2$ free (Section~\ref{sec:own-white}).

\subsection{The spatial profile}
\label{sec:response}

The spatial profile $q^\star_{pg}$ is the share of a column's starlight that
falls on each of its pixels. NOVA measures it from the transit itself. The
starlight dims during the transit and the background does not, so the part of
a pixel's light curve that follows the transit is starlight. This assumes that
nothing else varies like the transit.

I first measure the transit shape of each order, $\phi_o(t)$, as the median
relative light curve of its brightest pixels, scaled so that its deepest point
is $-1$. Each pixel is then fitted as
\begin{equation}
 Y_{tp} = A_{pg}\,\phi_{o}(t) + \ell_p + \kappa_p\,\tau_t + \varepsilon_{tp},
 \label{eq:qstar-fit}
\end{equation}
where $A_{pg}$ is the part of the signal that follows the transit,
$\ell_p+\kappa_p\tau_t$ is a straight line in time and $\varepsilon_{tp}$ is
the residual. The profile is $q^\star_{pg}=A_{pg}/\sum_{p'\in P_g}A_{p'g}$, in
which the depth cancels. Each amplitude rests on a dip of only 1.5 to 2\%, so
I smooth the profile across neighbouring columns, with a strength chosen by
cross-validation between alternate integrations. How accurate the profile is
in the faintest, reddest columns is not yet known, and the benchmark of
Section~\ref{sec:measured-benchmark} is designed to measure it.

The share in equation~(\ref{eq:nova-forward}) weights the profile by the
starlight that reaches each pixel,
\begin{equation}
 \tilde q_{pg} = \frac{q^\star_{pg}\, S_p}{\sum_{p'\in P_g} q^\star_{p'g}\, S_{p'}},
 \qquad S_p=\sum_\lambda K_{p\lambda}\, s_\lambda .
 \label{eq:qtilde}
\end{equation}
The trace is slightly tilted, so the pixels of one column see slightly
different wavelengths and $S_p$ varies a little within a column. Without this
tilt, $\tilde q_{pg}$ would equal $q^\star_{pg}$.

\subsection{The continuum, the background and the noise}
\label{sec:continuum}

The brightness of the star in each group is a straight line in time,
$C_{tg}=\beta_{0g}+\beta_{1g}\tau_t$, fitted together with the transit. A
curvature of the baseline cannot be fitted in the same way. The transit lies
in the middle of the observation, where a parabola bulges most, so a free
curvature in each column would trade against that column's depth. Instead, one
curvature $\gamma_t$, shared by all columns, is measured beforehand from the
out-of-transit data and held fixed. For each order, I divide a parabola by a
straight line, both fitted in time to the background-subtracted out-of-transit
trace flux, and $\gamma_t$ is the geometric mean of the two orders' ratios,
normalised to one out of transit. It is used only if it agrees between
alternate out-of-transit integrations and stays within 0.1\% of one. Its
uncertainty is not yet propagated into the spectrum.

The background left after Stage~2 is modelled with eight fixed spatial maps:
the zodiacal-light model that Stage~2 subtracted, and seven smooth polynomials
in detector position ($1$, $y$, $x$, $xy$, $y^2$, $x^2$ and $xy^2$). Each map
$\Phi_{pk}$ has a constant amplitude and an amplitude that follows one time
pattern $G_1(t)$,
\begin{equation}
 B_{tp}=\sum_{k=1}^{8}\Phi_{pk}
 \left[\alpha_{k0}+\alpha_{k1}\,G_1(t)\right].
 \label{eq:background-model}
\end{equation}
The 244,657 off-trace pixels, away from the traces, field stars and bad
pixels, see only background, and the maps are made orthonormal over them. The
maps and these pixels were chosen once from the WASP-17 visit, where eight
maps predicted held-out strips of the off-trace image better than two or
four. Whether more maps, for example sixteen, would do better has not yet
been tested. The
time pattern is learned from each input. Fitting the maps to the off-trace
pixels of every integration gives eight amplitude series, and $G_1$ is their
strongest principal component that does not look like the transit, with a
correlation of at most 0.25. On the real visit, the strongest component does
look like the transit, with a correlation of 0.95, and is therefore left out.
Section~\ref{sec:which-light} asks whether that signal is starlight. Finally,
the fit to the off-trace pixels enters the objective as an anchor, which keeps
the background amplitudes close to what these pixels show.

The pipeline uncertainty ERR underestimates the scatter of the trace pixels,
so each sample has the uncertainty
$\sigma_{tp}=\mathrm{ERR}_{tp}\,N_{p}\,s_{p}$. The factor $N_p$ is fixed for
each order, 3.07 for order~1 and 4.19 for order~2. It was measured once on the
WASP-17 visit, from how well a simpler model predicted held-out out-of-transit
integrations, so it includes model mismatch as well as noise. The factor
$s_p\geq1$ is measured on each input in a similar way, with a straight line in
time, and inflates single pixels that are noisier than the rest of their
order. Measuring $N_p$ again for each input is future work.

\subsection{The fit}

Each sample has the residual $r_{tp}=(Y_{tp}-\widehat Y_{tp})/\sigma_{tp}$,
and NOVA minimises
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{(t,p)}\rho_{1.345}(r_{tp})
 +\tfrac{1}{2}(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{off}})^{\mathsf T}
   \bm{N}_{\mathrm{off}}(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{off}})
 +P_{\mathrm{LD}}(\bm{u}),
 \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_{1.345}$ is the Huber loss, quadratic for $|r_{tp}|\leq1.345$ and
linear beyond, so that outliers do not dominate. The second term is the
background anchor, with $\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{off}}$ and
$\bm{N}_{\mathrm{off}}$ the amplitudes and precision matrix of the fit to the
off-trace pixels, and $P_{\mathrm{LD}}$ is the limb-darkening penalty.

The model is linear in the continuum and background coefficients and
nonlinear in 166 numbers: $\bar D$, the 157 shape coordinates and eight
limb-darkening coefficients. At every step, NOVA solves for the linear
coefficients exactly, by variable projection \citep{GolubPereyra1973}, and
the trust-region reflective method \citep{BranchColemanLi1999} adjusts the
nonlinear ones, with exact derivatives that use JAX for the transit model. The Huber loss is minimised by
reweighting. The weights $w_{tp}=\min(1,\,1.345/|r_{tp}|)$ depend on the
residuals, so they are recomputed after each fit, and the cycle repeats until
the weights, the objective and the spectrum stop changing within fixed
tolerances.

\subsection{White-light geometry}
\label{sec:own-white}

The orbital geometry comes from a joint fit to the white-light curves of the
two orders of the same input. Each curve is the light of all fitted columns in
each integration, combined by optimal extraction \citep{Horne1986} with the
median out-of-transit image as the profile. Whether using $q^\star_{pg}$ here
instead would change the geometry has not been tested. The fit is made with Eureka!
\citep{BellEtAl2022} and sampled with dynesty \citep{Speagle2020}. The two
orders share $t_0$, $a/R_\star$ and the inclination $i$, and each has its own
radius ratio $k_o$, limb darkening, linear baseline and noise multiplier. The
priors are broad and uniform: $t_0$ within the observed window, $a/R_\star$
from 1.01 to 100, $i$ from $0^\circ$ to $90^\circ$ and $u_1,u_2$ from 0 to 1.

The geometry is not well determined if the planet may graze the stellar limb,
because depth and geometry then trade off. A sample grazes if
\begin{equation}
 b > 1-\max(k_1,\,k_2),
 \label{eq:grazing}
\end{equation}
with $b=(a/R_\star)\cos i$. The geometry is accepted only if grazing samples
carry little posterior weight: the upper end of the 95\% interval of their
weight must be below 1\%, or below 5\% with the weight reported. The sampler
must also have converged, and this interval must be known to within one
percentage point.

\subsection{Uncertainties}
\label{sec:ensemble}

I plan to estimate the uncertainty from the calibration products and the
geometry with an ensemble of simulated observations. Until then, the error
bars of NOVA are conditional on the fitted calibration products and geometry.

\subsection{A first spectrum of WASP-17~b}
\label{sec:nova-real}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/nova_vs_published_wasp17_preliminary.pdf}
  \caption{Preliminary \nova{} transmission spectrum of the real WASP-17~b
  visit (blue) and the published Ahsoka spectrum of \citet{LouieEtAl2025} at
  a resolving power of 100 (orange; orders 1 and 2 shown separately). The
  \nova{} error bars are conditional on the fitted calibration products and
  geometry. All points of both spectra are shown.}
  \label{fig:nova-real}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:nova-real} compares a preliminary NOVA spectrum of the real
WASP-17~b visit, from a reduction of 18 September 2026, with the published
Ahsoka spectrum of \citet{LouieEtAl2025}. This reduction used NOVA's own
white-light geometry, but it was fitted before the acceptance rule of
Section~\ref{sec:own-white} was written, and a refit under this rule is part
of the plan (Section~\ref{sec:plan}). Below about $1.8\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ the
two spectra show similar features, such as the water bands, but the NOVA
spectrum is deeper throughout. After interpolating the published spectrum to
the NOVA bins, NOVA is deeper by a median of about 230~ppm
below $1.6\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, 550~ppm between 1.8 and $2.3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, and
490~ppm above $2.3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$.

The two reductions treat the geometry, the limb darkening and the background
differently, and this comparison does not separate these effects. The larger
difference in the red matters most, because there the starlight is faintest
and the background matters most. The real data cannot tell which reduction is
closer to the truth, and the biases that NOVA showed in earlier injection
tests cannot simply be subtracted. This is why I build the benchmark of the
next two sections, which measures the errors of each method against a known,
injected truth.
