\section{NOVA}
\label{sec:nova}

In this section, I describe NOVA as it is currently implemented. It grew out
of an earlier release of August 2026, from which the pixel layout, the
off-trace pixel set, the background maps, the per-order uncertainty scales and
the form of the curvature correction are carried over. Every other fitted
product, including the transit geometry, is estimated again for each input.
The section ends with a first, preliminary spectrum of the real WASP-17~b
visit.

The aim of NOVA is to infer the transmission spectrum from the detector pixels
themselves, so that the split between starlight and background is decided in
the same fit as the transit. A single forward model maps the transmission
spectrum $D(\lambda)$, the limb darkening $\bm{u}(\lambda)$, the brightness of
the star and the additive background onto every retained detector sample. All
four are estimated by minimising one objective. Other ingredients are
estimated beforehand from the same data and then held fixed. These are the
spatial response, a curvature correction, the temporal pattern and expected
level of the background, the uncertainty scales and the transit geometry. The
geometry comes from a white-light fit, the one place where NOVA does extract
light curves (Section~\ref{sec:own-white}).

\subsection{Detector processing}
\label{sec:detector-processing}

NOVA's input is prepared from the raw ramps with the JWST calibration pipeline
\citep{JWSTPipeline2025} and exoTEDRF \citep{Radica2024}. Firstly, the group
steps of the JWST pipeline run up to and including the reference-pixel
correction. Secondly, exoTEDRF removes the $1/f$ noise in each group, after
temporarily subtracting a scaled model of the zodiacal background so that the
stripes can be estimated. The linearity, jump-detection, ramp-fitting and gain
steps then give count rates. Finally, Stage~2 of exoTEDRF subtracts the scaled
background model, applies the flat field and corrects bad pixels. The $1/f$
step masks the field stars identified in the F277W exposure, and I apply no
further mask to the scene.

\subsection{The detector forward model}

Within one detector column, the pixels of a trace see nearly the same
wavelength. NOVA therefore organises the samples into groups, where a group
$g$ contains the pixels $P_g$ of one order in one column. Denoting by $Y_{tp}$
the calibrated count rate of pixel $p$ in integration $t$, NOVA predicts
\begin{equation}
 \widehat Y_{tp}
 = \gamma_t\, C_{tg}\,
   \mathcal{T}_{tg}(D, \bm{u};\, \Omega)\,
   q^\star_{pg}
 + B_{tp},
 \label{eq:nova-forward}
\end{equation}
where $g$ is the group containing pixel $p$, $C_{tg}$ is the brightness of
the star in that group at time $t$ (the continuum), and $\mathcal{T}_{tg}$ is
the transit factor. The transit factor dims this light according to the depth
$D$ and limb darkening $\bm{u}$ at the wavelengths of the group and the
orbital state $\Omega$. Furthermore, $\gamma_t$ is a small correction for slow
curvature in the brightness of the star, $q^\star_{pg}$ is the spatial
response, which distributes the light of the group over its pixels with
$\sum_{p\in P_g} q^\star_{pg}=1$, and $B_{tp}$ is the additive background.
Only the stellar term is dimmed, so any light that the model assigns to the
background does not dim during the transit. In practice, the transit is
evaluated on a fine wavelength grid and projected onto the pixels through the
line-spread kernel and the spatial response. This follows the public trace
and wavelength calibration \citep{BainesEtAl2023Trace,BainesEtAl2023Wavelength}
and its kernel \citep{DarveauBernierEtAl2022,JWSTPipeline2025}. The pixels
fitted for the two orders do not overlap.

\subsection{The shared spectrum and transit model}

A planet has one transmission spectrum, whichever order records its light, and
so both orders share $D(\lambda)$. It is represented by $K=160$ top-hat basis
functions, about $0.01\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ wide below $2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ and about
$0.04\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ above, whose coefficients are
\begin{equation}
  D_j = D_{\mathrm{global}}\,
  \frac{\exp\!\left[(\bm{H}_v\bm{v})_j\right]}
       {\sum_k w_k \exp\!\left[(\bm{H}_v\bm{v})_k\right]} ,
  \label{eq:depth-basis}
\end{equation}
where $D_{\mathrm{global}}$ is the achromatic depth, $\bm{v}$ are the free
shape coordinates, $\bm{H}_v$ maps them onto the subspace orthogonal to a
constant, and $w_k$ are fixed basis-width weights. In this form,
$\sum_j w_j D_j = D_{\mathrm{global}}$ sets the overall depth, the shape is
carried by coordinates without a constant component, and every depth stays
positive. Two red coefficients have no response support, and I hold them
constant, which leaves 158 free depth coordinates. Otherwise the spectrum is
free. There is no atmospheric template, no smoothing between wavelengths, no
prior on its shape and no term that lets the depths of the two orders
differ.

The orbital state is
$\Omega=(P,\,a/R_\star,\,e,\,\omega_{\mathrm{peri}},\,b,\,t_0)$. The period is
that used by \citet{LouieEtAl2025} and the orbit is circular. The mid-transit
time $t_0$, the scaled semi-major axis $a/R_\star$ and the impact parameter
$b$ come from NOVA's own white-light fit. Each integration lasts about 44~s,
during which the planet moves, so $\mathcal{T}_{tg}$ is the transit averaged
over the exposure, computed with jaxoplanet \citep{Jaxoplanet2025}. The limb
darkening follows the quadratic law \citep{MandelAgol2002}, written in the
coordinates $q_1,q_2\in[0,1]$ of \citet{Kipping2013},
\begin{equation}
 u_1=2\sqrt{q_1}\,q_2, \qquad
 u_2=\sqrt{q_1}\,(1-2q_2).
 \label{eq:kipping-coordinates}
\end{equation}
Limb darkening and depth are partly degenerate. The spectral fit therefore
allows only a smooth deviation from a theoretical reference computed from
STAGGER intensities \citep{MagicEtAl2015}. The deviation has one offset and one
slope in normalised log-wavelength for each coordinate and order, eight in
total, and is penalised both for departing from the theory and for curvature
in wavelength. The white-light fit, by contrast, leaves the limb darkening
free.

\subsection{The empirical spatial response}

Because the starlight dims during the transit whilst the background does not,
the part of a pixel's time series that follows the transit measures how much
starlight falls on that pixel. This assumes that only starlight shares the
time dependence of the transit. For each order $o$, I therefore build a
template $\phi_o(t)$ of the shape of the transit from the median behaviour of
its bright, stable pixels. The template is scaled so that it carries no
information about the depth, and every pixel is fitted, with Huber weights, as
\begin{equation}
 Y_{tp} = A_{pg}\,\phi_{o}(t) + \ell_p + \kappa_p\,\tau_t + \varepsilon_{tp},
 \label{eq:qstar-fit}
\end{equation}
where $A_{pg}$ is the transit-correlated amplitude of the pixel, $\ell_p$ is
its level, $\kappa_p\tau_t$ is a linear drift in time $\tau_t$, and
$\varepsilon_{tp}$ is the residual. The response is the normalised amplitude,
$q^\star_{pg}=A_{pg}/\sum_{p'\in P_g}A_{p'g}$. Each amplitude comes from a dip
of only about 1.5\%, so the amplitudes are smoothed across neighbouring
columns at a fixed integer position within the trace. The strength of the
smoothing is chosen by cross-validation between two halves of the
integrations, which must also agree in how they distribute the flux and where
they place its centre. The fixed integer position makes the centre of the
response occasionally jump between columns. In audits of the earlier release,
this came with a response at the faintest red columns that placed roughly 1
to 2\% more amplitude inside the fitted pixels than the light delivered to
them, and so made the transit too shallow there. The effect has not yet been
measured again for each input.

\subsection{The continuum, the background and the noise}

The continuum of each group is linear in time,
$C_{tg}=\beta_{0g}+\beta_{1g}\tau_t$. The background is a sum of eight fixed
spatial maps $\Phi_{pk}$, each with a constant amplitude $\alpha_{k0}$ and one
amplitude, $\alpha_{k1}$, that follows a temporal pattern $G_1(t)$,
\begin{equation}
 B_{tp}=\sum_{k=1}^{8}\Phi_{pk}
 \left[\alpha_{k0}+\alpha_{k1}\,G_1(t)\right].
 \label{eq:background-model}
\end{equation}
Because a scaled zodiacal model has already been subtracted from the images,
$B_{tp}$ describes what remains, including any error in that scaling. The
first map is a standardised calibration background and the other seven are
smooth polynomial surfaces. Fitting the maps to the off-trace pixels of every
integration gives a time series of amplitudes. $G_1$ is the first principal
component of this series whose correlation with the shape of the transit is
at most 0.25 in absolute value, which limits how much transit-shaped
variation the background can absorb. On the real visit, the leading
off-trace component is strongly correlated with the transit. Whether this
signal is starlight is the question that Section~\ref{sec:which-light}
discusses. The criterion keeps
this component out of the background model, and the uncertainty ensemble puts
it back (Section~\ref{sec:ensemble}). Finally, a joint fit to all off-trace
pixels gives an expected value $\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{off}}$ of the sixteen
amplitudes, with precision matrix $\bm{N}_{\mathrm{off}}$. It acts as a soft
anchor, since the background is refitted at every step but cannot wander far
from what the off-trace pixels show.

A linear continuum cannot follow a gentle curvature in the brightness of the
star, and this curvature could leak into the depth. The factor $\gamma_t$ is
therefore the ratio of a quadratic fit to the out-of-transit flux of each
order's trace to its straight-line approximation, normalised to unit mean out
of transit, and one factor is shared by both orders. It is only accepted if
two alternating halves of the out-of-transit integrations predict nearly the
same, small correction during the transit. Each sample has the uncertainty
$\sigma_{tp}=\mathrm{ERR}_{tp}\,N_{o}\,s_{p}$, where $\mathrm{ERR}_{tp}$ is
the pipeline uncertainty, $N_o$ is a scale for order $o$ (3.07 and 4.19) and
$s_p\geq1$ is a scale for each pixel. The pixel scale is estimated by
predicting one half of the out-of-transit integrations from the other. These
scales only set the weights of the fit. The ensemble is intended to estimate
the uncertainty of the real spectrum.

\subsection{The objective and its minimisation}

Writing the standardised residual as
\begin{equation}
 r_{tp}=\frac{Y_{tp}-\widehat Y_{tp}
 (D,\bm{u},\bm{\beta},\bm{\alpha};\,q^\star,\Omega)}{\sigma_{tp}},
 \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
where the fitted parameters appear before the semicolon and the fixed inputs
after it, the NOVA estimate is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
 &(\widehat D,\widehat{\bm{u}},\widehat{\bm{\beta}},
  \widehat{\bm{\alpha}})
 = \arg\min\Big\{ \\
 &\quad\sum_{(t,p)\in\mathcal{M}}\rho_{1.345}(r_{tp}) \\
 &\quad+\tfrac{1}{2}(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{off}})^{\mathsf T}
   \bm{N}_{\mathrm{off}}(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{off}}) \\
 &\quad+\tfrac{1}{2}\|\bm{r}_{\mathrm{LD,theory}}(\bm{u})\|_2^2
  +\tfrac{1}{2}\|\bm{r}_{\mathrm{LD,curv}}(\bm{u})\|_2^2
 \Big\},
\end{split}
 \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{M}$ is the mask of retained samples and $\rho_{1.345}$ is the
Huber loss, which is quadratic for $|r_{tp}|\leq1.345$ and linear beyond, so
that outliers do not dominate the fit. The terms $\bm{r}_{\mathrm{LD,theory}}$
and $\bm{r}_{\mathrm{LD,curv}}$ are the scaled deviations of the limb
darkening from theory and its curvatures in wavelength. There is no
Gaussian-process term for correlated noise.

For given depths and limb darkening, the prediction is linear in the
thousands of continuum and background coefficients. NOVA therefore splits the
parameters into a nonlinear block $\bm{\theta}=(D,\bm{u})$ and a linear block
$(\bm{\beta},\bm{\alpha})$, and minimises in three nested loops. In the
innermost loop, with $\bm{\theta}$ and the Huber weights $w_{tp}$ held fixed,
variable projection \citep{GolubPereyra1973} solves exactly for the linear
block. This is the least-squares problem with weights $w_{tp}/\sigma_{tp}^2$,
together with the background anchor. It is tractable because each continuum
coefficient is coupled only to neighbouring columns through the line-spread
kernel, and the sixteen background coefficients are handled through a Schur
complement. The Jacobian with respect to $\bm{\theta}$ is obtained by
differentiating through this exact solution. In the middle loop, bounded
trust-region reflective least squares \citep{BranchColemanLi1999} minimises
this profiled objective over $\bm{\theta}$. In the outer loop, the Huber
weights $w_{tp}=\min(1,\,1.345/|r_{tp}|)$ \citep{Huber1964} are updated. A fit
is accepted only if the weights, the objective and the spectrum have all
settled, first-order optimality is reached, and a final cycle with recomputed
weights reproduces the solution. Every analysis is run from at least two
starts, one at the white-light depth with a flat shape and one with a fixed
perturbation of the shape and limb darkening, and the accepted start with the
lowest objective is selected. So far the starts agree to within about
0.01~ppm.

\subsection{White-light geometry inference}
\label{sec:own-white}

NOVA infers $t_0$, $a/R_\star$ and the inclination $i$, and hence
$b=(a/R_\star)\cos i$, from its own detector data, without using any fitted
value from another pipeline or from an injector. For each order, a
white-light curve is built by summing a profile-weighted (optimal) estimate of
the light in each column, using an out-of-transit profile from the same
input. The two curves are fitted jointly with the light-curve stage of
Eureka! \citep{BellEtAl2022}. They share $t_0$, $a/R_\star$ and $i$, whilst
each order has its own radius ratio $k_o$, quadratic limb darkening with
uniform priors between 0 and 1 on both coefficients, linear baseline and
white-noise multiplier. The geometry priors are deliberately
wide, with $t_0$ uniform over the observed window, $a/R_\star$ on
$[1.01,\,100]$ and $i$ on $[0^\circ,\,90^\circ]$. The other priors use no fit
to these data and are copied from the public Ahsoka configuration of
\citet{LouieEtAl2025}. The posterior is sampled with the nested sampler
dynesty \citep{Speagle2020}. On injected inputs, the ensemble sampler emcee
\citep{ForemanMackeyEtAl2013} did not reach 50 autocorrelation lengths, and a
later check found about 12\% of the samples of each failed chain on grazing
or non-transiting geometries.

Such geometries are the failure that matters here. For every posterior
sample, $b$ is computed from that sample's $a/R_\star$ and $i$, and the
sample counts as grazing or non-transiting if
\begin{equation}
 b > 1-\max(k_1,\,k_2),
 \label{eq:grazing}
\end{equation}
that is, if the planet's disc is not wholly inside the stellar disc at
mid-transit. A fit passes if the upper end of the 95\% interval of the
weighted fraction of such samples is below 1\%. It passes with the fraction
reported if that upper end lies between 1 and 5\%. Otherwise the fit is held
and not used, and it is not continued, reseeded, trimmed or replaced by
another geometry. Note that this rule is not blind to the real data. It is the fifth
version of the criterion, written after it was known that the real posterior
contains no grazing samples of positive weight. Before using it, however, I
tested it on saved posteriors, where it had to reject the three emcee chains
that had failed their length assessment and accept both those that had passed
and the nested posterior of the real visit. An accepted fit passes the
weighted medians of $t_0$, $a/R_\star$, $i$ and $k_o$ over the whole
posterior to the spectral fit.

\subsection{The uncertainty ensemble}
\label{sec:ensemble}

The covariance of a single fit is conditional on uncertain calibration
products, such as the response and the geometry. I therefore estimate the
uncertainty of NOVA's spectrum by repeating the whole analysis on simulated
realisations of the real visit,
\begin{equation}
 Y^{(n)}_{tp}=\widehat Y^{\mathrm{ref}}_{tp}+\bar r_p+a_p\,\psi_t
 +\mathrm{ERR}_{tp}\,z_{d_n(t),\,p},
 \label{eq:ensemble-draw}
\end{equation}
where $n$ labels the realisation, $\widehat Y^{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the accepted
prediction for the real visit, and $\bar r_p$ is the mean real out-of-transit
residual of each pixel, so that any static mismatch between model and data is
also present. The term $a_p\psi_t$ puts back the transit-shaped off-trace
component that the background model keeps out. Here $\psi_t$ is the transit
decrement of the accepted white-light fit, exactly zero outside the transit,
and $a_p$ is the projection of the component onto $\psi_t$ in each off-trace
pixel, extended under the trace by the background model. The last term adds
real noise, the real ERR array times a standardised out-of-transit residual
$z$ of a donor integration $d_n(t)$. Donors are drawn in blocks of 64
consecutive integrations within one out-of-transit interval, because real
noise is correlated in time.

The proposed ensemble has 352 realisations. 256 estimate the covariance, 32
are held out to test it, and two further sets of 32 test the block length. Each is
analysed from scratch, with its own calibration products, white-light fit and
two starts, and no failed realisation is replaced or dropped. The error of a
realisation, its spectrum minus that of the real visit, is expressed in
$N_{\mathrm{m}}=128$ modes, the rank of the map onto the 147 reporting bins.
Because the covariance $\bm{S}$ of the $N=256$ estimation errors is itself
estimated, a Gaussian likelihood would be too narrow. For a new error
$\bm{x}$ I therefore use the multivariate Student distribution of
\citet{SellentinHeavens2016}, centred on the sample mean $\bm{\mu}$, with
$N-N_{\mathrm{m}}$ degrees of freedom and scale matrix
\begin{equation}
 \bm{\Sigma}=\left(1+\frac{1}{N}\right)\frac{N-1}{N-N_{\mathrm{m}}}\,\bm{S},
 \label{eq:finite-ensemble}
\end{equation}
where the factor $1+1/N$ allows for the estimated mean. This distribution
still rests on assumptions about the shape of the errors, which the held-out
realisations test. The likelihood is only used for retrieval if the held-out
errors pass a prespecified test of coverage for its 95\% region. A first version of the ensemble failed this
test, with only 21 of 32 held-out errors inside the region. Its realisations
lacked the static mismatch and the transit-correlated off-trace light that
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ensemble-draw} now contains, although this difference has not
been shown to be the cause. The replacement ensemble was started, and 64 of
its realisations were completed before the remaining work was deferred. A
validated ensemble uncertainty is therefore not yet available.

\subsection{A first spectrum of WASP-17~b}
\label{sec:nova-real}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/nova_vs_published_wasp17_preliminary.pdf}
  \caption{Preliminary \nova{} transmission spectrum of the real WASP-17~b
  visit (blue) and the published Ahsoka spectrum of \citet{LouieEtAl2025} at
  a resolving power of 100 (orange; orders 1 and 2 shown separately). The
  \nova{} error bars are conditional on the fitted calibration products and
  geometry, and do not yet include the uncertainty from the ensemble. All
  points of both spectra are shown.}
  \label{fig:nova-real}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:nova-real} compares a preliminary NOVA spectrum of the real
WASP-17~b visit with the published Ahsoka spectrum of \citet{LouieEtAl2025}.
The NOVA spectrum comes from a reduction of 18 September 2026, which used
NOVA's own joint white-light geometry but was fitted before the criterion of
Section~\ref{sec:own-white} was written. The error bars of the NOVA spectrum
are conditional on its calibration products and geometry, since no validated
ensemble uncertainty is available yet. Below about $1.8\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ the
two spectra show similar features, such as the water bands, but the NOVA
spectrum is deeper throughout. Interpolating the published spectrum to the
bins of NOVA, the mean difference is about 250~ppm below
$1.6\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, 660~ppm between 1.8 and $2.3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, and
490~ppm above $2.3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$. Different geometry, limb-darkening and
background treatments may all contribute, and this comparison does not
isolate them. The larger difference in the red matters most, since it lies
where the starlight is faintest and the background is most important.
Separate injection tests also found nonzero biases of NOVA, but these cannot
be subtracted from the difference on real data. Whether NOVA or the published
reduction is closer to the truth cannot be decided from the real data. This
motivates the benchmark of the next two sections, which is designed to
measure the errors of the methods when the injected truth is known.
