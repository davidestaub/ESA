\section{NOVA}
\label{sec:nova}

NOVA fits the transmission spectrum directly to the detector pixels. Its
model describes every fitted pixel as starlight, dimmed by the transit, plus
background, so the split between the two is decided in the same fit as the
transit. The fit estimates the spectrum, the limb darkening, the brightness of the star in each column of each order and the background together. Everything else is held
fixed during the fit. Some of these fixed quantities are estimated beforehand
from each data set: the spatial profile, the stellar spectrum, the curvature
of the baseline, the time pattern of the background, a noise factor for each
pixel and the orbital geometry. The others are the same for every data set:
the depth bins, the limb-darkening reference, the apertures, the background
maps and a noise factor for each order. In this section, I describe NOVA as it is currently
implemented and compare its first, preliminary spectrum of WASP-17~b with the
published Ahsoka spectrum.

\subsection{Detector processing}
\label{sec:detector-processing}

NOVA's input is prepared from the raw ramps with the JWST calibration pipeline
\citep{JWSTPipeline2025} and exoTEDRF \citep{Radica2024}. After the
reference-pixel correction, exoTEDRF removes the $1/f$ noise from each group.
To estimate this noise, it subtracts from the group the median out-of-transit
image, scaled by a light curve measured from the data. It then takes the median
of what is left in each column outside the trace cores, leaving out field stars
found in a separate exposure through the F277W filter, in which the traces of WASP-17 almost vanish. This column offset is
subtracted from the original group. For this step only, a scaled background
model is subtracted beforehand and added back afterwards. The remaining
steps of the JWST pipeline give count rates. Stage~2 of exoTEDRF then applies
the flat field, subtracts the scaled zodiacal background and corrects bad
pixels.

\subsection{The detector model}
\label{sec:forward}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/nova_schematic.pdf}
  \caption{Schematic of the detector model of \nova{}. (a) The fitted pixels
  are grouped by order and column (a sketch, not data). The fitted apertures
  of the two orders do not overlap. (b) The count rate of each pixel is modelled as starlight,
  dimmed by the transit, plus a background that is not dimmed. The spectrum is
  shared by both orders, whilst the limb darkening is fitted for each order as
  penalised deviations from a theoretical reference.}
  \label{fig:nova-schematic}
\end{figure}

Within one detector column, the pixels of a trace see nearly the same range of wavelengths. NOVA therefore groups the pixels by order and column: a group $g$
holds the pixels $P_g$ of one order in one column
(Figure~\ref{fig:nova-schematic}). The fitted pixels lie in two fixed
apertures along the traces. I leave out four edge columns, the 51 reddest columns of order~1, beyond $2.758\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ (Section~\ref{sec:transit}), and flagged samples, where a sample is one
pixel in one integration. For the count rate $Y_{tp}$ of pixel
$p$ in integration $t$, NOVA predicts
\begin{equation}
 \widehat Y_{tp}
 = \gamma_t\, C_{tg}\, \bar{\mathcal{T}}_{tg}\, q^\star_{pg} + B_{tp},
 \label{eq:nova-forward}
\end{equation}
where $C_{tg}$ is the brightness of the star in the group $g$ that contains pixel $p$,
$q^\star_{pg}$ is the share of that light that falls on pixel $p$
(Section~\ref{sec:response}), $\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{tg}$ is the fraction of
the group's light that remains during the transit (Section~\ref{sec:transit}), $\gamma_t$ is a fixed factor,
close to one, that describes a slight curvature of the baseline and $B_{tp}$ is the background left after
Stage~2 (Section~\ref{sec:continuum}).

\subsection{The spatial profile}
\label{sec:response}

NOVA measures the spatial profile $q^\star_{pg}$ from the transit itself. The
starlight dims during the transit and the background does not, so the part of
a pixel's light curve that follows the transit is starlight. This assumes that
nothing else varies like the transit.

I first measure the transit shape of each order, $\phi_o(t)$, as the median
relative light curve of its brightest pixels, scaled so that its deepest point
is $-1$. Each pixel is then fitted as
\begin{equation}
 Y_{tp} = A_{pg}\,\phi_{o}(t) + \ell_p + \kappa_p\,\tau_t + \varepsilon_{tp},
 \label{eq:qstar-fit}
\end{equation}
where $A_{pg}$ is the part of the signal that follows the transit,
$\ell_p+\kappa_p\tau_t$ is a straight line in the time $\tau_t$ of integration
$t$, and $\varepsilon_{tp}$ is the residual. The profile is $q^\star_{pg}=A_{pg}/\sum_{p'\in P_g}A_{p'g}$, in
which the depth cancels.

The planet blocks only about 1.5 to 2\% of the starlight. A pixel that
receives, for example, 100~DN\,s$^{-1}$ of starlight dims by only about 1.5
to 2~DN\,s$^{-1}$ during the transit, which is comparable to its noise in a single
integration. The measured $A_{pg}$, and so $q^\star_{pg}$, is therefore
noisy. Because the profile changes only slowly from one column to the next, I
smooth it across neighbouring columns with a Whittaker smoother \citep{Eilers2003}, which follows the measured values but is penalised for bending, and gives less weight to outlying values. The strength of
the smoothing is chosen so that the profile smoothed from one half of the
integrations best predicts the unsmoothed profile from the other half.

\subsection{The transit and the spectrum}
\label{sec:transit}

Each column records light from a range of wavelengths. For each group $g$ and
wavelength $\lambda$, $K_{g\lambda}$ is how much of the star's light at
$\lambda$ is recorded in the column. It combines three public calibration
products. The PASTASOSS trace and wavelength calibration says where
each wavelength falls on the detector
\citep{BainesEtAl2023Trace,BainesEtAl2023Wavelength}. The line-spread kernel
describes how the light of one wavelength spreads over a few neighbouring
columns, and the throughput says how much of the light at each wavelength is
detected. NOVA builds $K_{g\lambda}$ from these products with ATOCA, the SOSS extraction code of the JWST
pipeline \citep{DarveauBernierEtAl2022,JWSTPipeline2025}. In this
calibration, all pixels of a column see the same wavelengths, and only the
light of each group's own order is modelled. The fraction of a group's light that remains during the transit is therefore
the mean of the transit light curve over the wavelengths of its column,
weighted by the light that each wavelength contributes,
\begin{equation}
 \bar{\mathcal{T}}_{tg}
 = \frac{\sum_{\lambda} K_{g\lambda}\, s_\lambda\, \mathcal{T}_t(\lambda)}
        {\sum_{\lambda} K_{g\lambda}\, s_\lambda},
 \label{eq:group-transit}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{T}_t(\lambda)$ is the transit light curve at wavelength
$\lambda$, whose depth is $D(\lambda)$, and $s_\lambda$ is the spectrum of the
star, fitted to the out-of-transit data of each data set and then held
fixed.

A planet has one transmission spectrum, so both orders share $D(\lambda)$. The spectrum
is represented by 160 top-hat bins from 0.62 to $2.84\,\mu\mathrm{m}$. The bins
are about $0.01\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ wide below $2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ and
$0.038\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ wide above it. They are wider in the red, where the
signal-to-noise ratio is lower. The bluest bin is twice as wide, because no fitted column reaches its
bluer half. The two reddest bins receive no light in the model, because order~2 does
not reach them and order~1 is cut at $2.758\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, beyond which the PASTASOSS calibration would have to be extrapolated. They are held at
the white-light depth and not reported. The other 158 depths are written as an
overall level times a shape,
\begin{equation}
  D_j = \bar D\,
  \frac{\exp(h_j)}{\sum_{k} w_k \exp(h_k)}, \qquad \bm{h}=\bm{H}\bm{z},
  \label{eq:depth-basis}
\end{equation}
where $D_j$ is the depth in bin $j$, $\bar D$ is the overall, achromatic depth and $w_k$ are the bin widths,
normalised to sum to one, so that $\bar D$ is the width-weighted mean of the
depths. The exponential keeps every depth positive. Adding the same constant to
every $h_j$ would not change the spectrum, so the fixed $158\times157$ matrix
$\bm{H}$ removes this direction. Its columns are orthonormal and each sums to
zero, and it is built by a QR decomposition of a vector of ones. The fit
varies $\bar D$ and the 157 shape coordinates $\bm{z}$. For reporting and for
comparison with other pipelines, the fitted spectrum is mapped onto a fixed
grid of 147 bins between 0.63 and $2.74\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, at a resolving power
of about 100. Each reporting bin takes the mean of the fitted depths that
overlap it, weighted by the overlap in wavelength.

The orbit is circular, with the period of \citet{LouieEtAl2025}. The
mid-transit time $t_0$, scaled semi-major axis $a/R_\star$ and impact
parameter $b$ are all computed from NOVA's white-light fit
(Section~\ref{sec:own-white}). The
transit light curve is evaluated with jaxoplanet \citep{Jaxoplanet2025} and averaged over
each integration.

The limb darkening follows the quadratic law \citep{MandelAgol2002}, in the
coordinates $q_1,q_2\in[0,1]$ of \citet{Kipping2013},
\begin{equation}
 u_1=2\sqrt{q_1}\,q_2, \qquad
 u_2=\sqrt{q_1}\,(1-2q_2),
 \label{eq:kipping-coordinates}
\end{equation}
where $u_1$ and $u_2$ are the coefficients of the quadratic law. Limb darkening and depth are partly degenerate, so the spectral fit lets the
limb darkening deviate only smoothly from a reference computed from STAGGER
model intensities \citep{MagicEtAl2015}. In order $o$, the logit of each
coordinate $q_c$ is its reference value plus a deviation
$\delta_{oc}(\lambda)=a_{oc}+b_{oc}\,\ell_o(\lambda)$, with an offset $a_{oc}$
and a slope $b_{oc}$. Here $\ell_o$ is the logarithm of wavelength, centred on
the order and scaled so that its largest magnitude is one.

\subsection{The continuum, the background and the noise}
\label{sec:continuum}

The continuum, the brightness of the star in each group, is a straight line in time,
$C_{tg}=\beta_{0g}+\beta_{1g}\tau_t$. Its level differs between columns
because each column sees a different wavelength. It is fitted together with
the transit, but in practice it is set by the integrations before and after
the transit. The baseline may also curve slightly. From these integrations, a curvature is harder
to measure than a straight line, and in a single column it would trade against the depth. I therefore
measure one curvature from all columns together, using the out-of-transit data
only, and hold it fixed. For each order, I sum the background-subtracted light
of all trace pixels, fit it with a parabola and with a straight line in time,
and divide the parabola by the line. This leaves only the curvature of that
order, as a factor close to one. In the detector model, $\gamma_t$ is the geometric mean of the two
orders' curvature factors, normalised to one out of transit. This assumes that the curvature is the same, as a fraction of
the light, in every column.

The background left after Stage~2 is modelled with eight fixed spatial maps:
the zodiacal-light model that Stage~2 subtracted, and seven smooth polynomials
in detector position ($1$, $y$, $x$, $xy$, $y^2$, $x^2$ and $xy^2$). Each map
$\Phi_{pk}$ has a constant amplitude and an amplitude that follows one time
pattern $G(t)$, shared by all maps,
\begin{equation}
 B_{tp}=\sum_{k=1}^{8}\Phi_{pk}
 \left[\alpha_{k0}+\alpha_{k1}\,G(t)\right].
 \label{eq:background-model}
\end{equation}
The 244,657 off-trace pixels lie away from the traces, field stars and bad pixels. They are assumed to see only background, although some starlight from the faint wings of the traces may reach them (Section~\ref{sec:which-light}). The
maps are made orthonormal over these pixels. The maps and these pixels were
chosen once from the WASP-17~b visit, where eight maps predicted held-out strips
of the off-trace image better than two or four. I have not yet tested how well
the maps describe the background under the traces, or how much an error in the
maps there would change the depths.

Field stars are left out of the off-trace pixels and of the $1/f$ correction
(Section~\ref{sec:detector-processing}), but NOVA does not yet treat
field-star light that falls on the traces, which it cannot tell apart from the
light of the target. A treatment of such light, for example with the positions
of field-star spectra that Gaia predicts (Section~\ref{sec:measuring-star}),
is future work, needed for both the benchmark and the WASP-17~b visit.

The time pattern is learned from each data set. Fitting the maps to the
off-trace pixels of every integration gives eight amplitude series, and $G$
is the strongest of their principal components whose absolute correlation
with the transit is at most 0.25. On the real WASP-17~b visit, the strongest component
does look like the transit, with a correlation of 0.95, and is therefore left
out. Whether that signal is starlight is still open
(Section~\ref{sec:real-data-test}).

The pipeline uncertainty ERR underestimates the scatter of the trace pixels,
so each sample has the uncertainty
$\sigma_{tp}=\mathrm{ERR}_{tp}\,N_{p}\,s_{p}$. The factor $N_p$ is 3.07 for
order~1 and 4.19 for order~2. It was measured once on the WASP-17~b visit, from
how well the model predicted out-of-transit integrations held out of the fit,
so it also covers part of the model mismatch, treated as random noise. I still
need to measure it again with the current model, and for each data set. The factor
$s_p\geq1$ is measured on each data set in a similar way, with a straight line
in time. It inflates the uncertainty of single pixels that are noisier than the
rest of their order.

\subsection{The fit}
\label{sec:fit}

NOVA fits the spectrum, the limb-darkening offsets and slopes, the continuum
coefficients $\bm{\beta}$ and the background amplitudes $\bm{\alpha}$ by
minimising
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
 \mathcal{L} ={}& \sum_{t,p}\rho\!\left(\frac{Y_{tp}-\widehat Y_{tp}}{\sigma_{tp}}\right)\\
 &+\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{off}})^{\mathsf T}
   \bm{N}_{\mathrm{off}}(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{off}}) \\
 &+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{o,c}\left[\left\langle\left(\frac{\delta_{oc}}{0.5}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{o}
 +\left\langle\left(\frac{\delta''_{oc}}{2\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{-2}}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{o}\right].
\end{split}
 \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
The first term compares the model with the data. For every fitted sample, the
bracket is the standardised residual $r_{tp}$, the difference between data and
model in units of the uncertainty. The Huber loss \citep{Huber1964} is $\rho(r)=r^2/2$ for
$|r|\leq1.345$ and $1.345\,|r|-1.345^2/2$ beyond, so it is quadratic for small
residuals and grows only linearly for outliers, which therefore do not
dominate the fit. The second term ties the background to the off-trace pixels. Here $\bm{\alpha}$ holds the
sixteen background amplitudes, and $\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{off}}$ and
$\bm{N}_{\mathrm{off}}$ are the amplitudes and precision matrix of a fit of
the background model to the off-trace pixels alone. The term is half the
increase in the $\chi^2$ of those pixels above their own best fit, so it keeps
the background close to what they show.
The last term penalises the limb-darkening deviations $\delta_{oc}$ of the two
coordinates $c$ in the two orders $o$, where $\langle\cdot\rangle_o$ is the
average over the wavelengths of order $o$. It acts as a Gaussian prior with
two parts. The first keeps the deviation close to the reference, with a
standard deviation of 0.5 in the logit. The second limits the curvature $\delta''_{oc}$ of the
deviation in wavelength, with a standard deviation of $2\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{-2}$.

The model is linear in the continuum and background coefficients and
nonlinear in 166 parameters: $\bar D$, the 157 shape coordinates and the eight
limb-darkening offsets and slopes. The trust-region reflective method \citep{BranchColemanLi1999} adjusts the
nonlinear parameters, using exact derivatives that JAX computes for the transit
model. At every step of this method, NOVA solves for the linear coefficients
exactly, by variable projection \citep{GolubPereyra1973}. The
Huber loss is minimised by repeated reweighting. Each sample is given the weight
$w_{tp}=\min(1,\,1.345/|r_{tp}|)$. The weights depend on the residuals, so they are
recomputed after each fit, and the cycle repeats until the weights, the
objective and the spectrum stop changing within fixed tolerances.

\subsection{White-light geometry}
\label{sec:own-white}

The orbital geometry comes from a joint fit to the white-light curves of the
two orders, from the same data set as the spectral fit. Each curve is the light of all fitted columns
in each integration, combined by optimal extraction \citep{Horne1986} with
the median out-of-transit image as the profile. I have not tested whether using
$q^\star_{pg}$ as the profile instead would change the geometry. The
fit is made with Eureka! \citep{BellEtAl2022} and sampled with dynesty
\citep{Speagle2020}. The two orders share $t_0$, $a/R_\star$ and the
inclination $i$, and each has its own radius ratio, limb darkening,
linear baseline and noise multiplier. The priors are broad and uniform: $t_0$
within the observed window, $a/R_\star$ from 1.01 to 100, $i$ from $0^\circ$
to $90^\circ$ and $u_1,u_2$ from 0 to 1.

NOVA currently works only for transits that are not grazing. A white-light
fit is therefore accepted only if its posterior gives little weight to orbits that graze
the stellar limb, for which depth and geometry are hard to separate.

\subsection{Uncertainties}
\label{sec:ensemble}

I plan to use an ensemble of simulated observations to estimate the uncertainty
that the calibration products and the geometry contribute to the spectrum. Until then, the error
bars of NOVA are conditional on the quantities held fixed in the fit,
including the geometry.

\subsection{A first spectrum of WASP-17~b}
\label{sec:nova-real}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/nova_vs_published_wasp17_preliminary.pdf}
  \caption{Preliminary \nova{} transmission spectrum of the real WASP-17~b
  visit (blue) and the published Ahsoka spectrum of \citet{LouieEtAl2025} at
  a resolving power of 100 (orange; orders 1 and 2 shown separately). The
  \nova{} error bars are conditional on the quantities held fixed in the fit,
  including the geometry. All points of both spectra are shown.}
  \label{fig:nova-real}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:nova-real} compares a preliminary NOVA spectrum of the real
WASP-17~b visit with the published
Ahsoka spectrum of \citet{LouieEtAl2025}. Below about $1.8\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ the
two spectra show similar features, such as the water bands, but the NOVA
spectrum is deeper at every wavelength. With the published spectrum interpolated
to the NOVA bins, the NOVA spectrum is deeper by a median of about 250~ppm below $1.8\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, 550~ppm between 1.8 and $2.3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, and
490~ppm above $2.3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$.

The two reductions treat the geometry, the limb darkening and the background
differently, and this comparison does not separate these effects. The larger difference in the red matters most for deciding which reduction is right, because there the starlight is faintest and the recovered depth depends most on how much of the faint light is taken to be starlight (Section~\ref{sec:which-light}). The real data cannot tell which
reduction is closer to the truth. This is why I am building the benchmark of the
next two sections, which is designed to measure the errors of each method
against a known, injected truth.
