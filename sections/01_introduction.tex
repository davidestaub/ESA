\section{Introduction and Literature Review}
\label{sec:introduction}

% Question: What is the central problem of this project, in miniature?
% Answer: A transmission spectrum is inferred through a chain of processing
% steps, a real planet provides no known spectrum to score it against, and
% the chain fixes the background early and compresses pixels to one
% dimension, so this project builds a truth-known injector and a joint
% detector-level inference method that makes neither choice.
% Evidence: The argument developed across this introduction.
In conventional analyses, the transmission spectrum of an exoplanet is
inferred from a detector-level time series through a chain of processing
steps: firstly the detector is calibrated and the background is treated,
then a spectrum is extracted from each image, light curves are built from
these spectra, and finally a transit model is fitted to each light curve.
Every atmospheric conclusion drawn from the spectrum depends on how well
this chain works. Independent analyses of the same observation can
produce different spectra \citep{ConstantinouEtAl2023,KirkEtAl2024},
whilst close agreement has also been reported \citep{LouieEtAl2025}.
However, neither outcome on its own establishes absolute accuracy, because
for a real planet there is no independently known spectrum against which
the recovered one can be scored.

The chain also makes two structural choices early on, about the order in
which quantities are estimated and about what is passed from one stage to
the next. Firstly, the background is
estimated before or during extraction, separately from the transit signal.
Secondly, each detector image is compressed into a one-dimensional
spectrum, which can discard information that the later fitting stages are
unable to recover. Whether either choice biases the recovered spectrum can
only be answered with data for which the true spectrum is known. In this project I develop two connected tools. The first is a
detector-level injector, which writes a known transmission spectrum into the
raw data of a real JWST/NIRISS SOSS observation. The second is NOVA, a forward model of the detector data. It propagates the
stellar spectrum and the transit through a model of the instrument response
and fits them, together with an additive background, in a single fit to the
detector pixels, without extracting one-dimensional spectra for the spectral
fit. The background which remains after detector processing is therefore
fitted together with the wavelength-dependent transit, instead of being
fixed beforehand.

% Question: How is the argument of this introduction organised?
% Answer: Five sections lead from the scientific motivation, through the
% case study and the conventional workflow, to the structural hypotheses and
% the research question.
% Evidence: The section structure below.
The rest of this introduction is organised as follows.
Sections~\ref{sec:atmospheres-transmission} and~\ref{sec:jwst-soss-wasp17}
explain why exoplanet atmospheres are primary scientific targets, and why a
JWST/NIRISS SOSS observation of WASP-17~b is used as the first case study.
Section~\ref{sec:detector-to-validation} follows the data through the
conventional workflow, which I will call extraction-first, and explains why
the spectra it produces are difficult to validate.
Section~\ref{sec:structural-limitations-inference} states two structural
hypotheses, one about background treatment and one about spectral
compression, and describes the response of this project to them. Finally,
Section~\ref{sec:research-question} states the research question and
objectives.

\subsection{Exoplanet atmospheres and transmission spectroscopy}
\label{sec:atmospheres-transmission}
\label{sec:exoplanets-targets}
\label{sec:observing-atmospheres}

% Question: What does exoplanet diversity require us to determine?
% Answer: The physical and chemical properties of individual planets, and
% how reliably those properties can be inferred from observations.
% Evidence: Winn and Fabrycky (2015).
The discovery of planets outside the Solar System has shown that planetary
systems can look very different from our own. Exoplanets span a wide range
of sizes, temperatures and orbits, and many of them have no close
counterpart in the Solar System \citep{WinnFabrycky2015}. Understanding this
diversity means determining the physical and chemical properties of
individual planets, and knowing how reliably those properties can be
inferred from observations.

% Question: Why can bulk measurements not determine a planet's composition?
% Answer: Mass and radius constrain bulk density but do not uniquely determine
% a planet's mixture of rock, ice and gas.
% Evidence: Rogers and Seager (2010) and Madhusudhan (2019).
For a well-characterised transiting planet, the mass and radius are usually
among the first physical properties to be inferred. Together they constrain
the bulk density, but planets with similar masses and radii can be made of
different mixtures of rock, ice and gas, which leaves their exact
compositions underconstrained \citep{RogersSeager2010}. Distinguishing
between these possibilities requires measurements which are sensitive to
the composition and thermal structure of the outer layers of the planet, and
these are provided by its atmosphere \citep{Madhusudhan2019}.

% Question: What additional information is carried by an atmosphere?
% Answer: Atmospheric spectra probe composition, energy transport, clouds,
% escape, and possible signatures of formation and evolution.
% Evidence: Oberg et al. (2011), Vidal-Madjar et al. (2003), and Madhusudhan
% (2019).
The composition and structure of an atmosphere reflect the present
conditions on a planet, and may also retain information about how it formed
and evolved. Atomic and molecular abundances constrain the chemical
inventory, elemental ratios and metallicity of the atmosphere. These
quantities may carry information about the gas and solids accreted during
formation, although they do not determine a unique formation history
\citep{ObergEtAl2011}. Temperature structure, clouds and hazes govern how
radiation is absorbed, emitted and transported, and signatures of escape
trace atmospheric loss driven by high-energy irradiation from the host star
\citep{VidalMadjarEtAl2003}. When interpreted together with the mass,
radius, age and irradiation of the planet, atmospheric measurements can
therefore be used to test hypotheses about formation, migration, enrichment
and long-term mass loss \citep{Madhusudhan2019}. To make such tests,
however, the atmospheric signal must first be separated from the light of the
host star.

% Question: What observing method turns the atmospheric signal into a
% measurement, and why does this work begin there?
% Answer: Transmission spectroscopy measures starlight filtered through the
% atmospheric limb during transit. Its well-defined, repeating geometry
% makes it a convenient setting in which to test how detector modelling and
% reduction choices affect a recovered spectrum.
% Evidence: Current project scope.
The atmospheric signal can be observed when a planet passes in front of its
host star and part of the starlight passes through the planet's atmospheric
limb, a technique known as transmission spectroscopy. I start with
transmission spectroscopy because the geometry of a transit is well defined
and repeats with every orbit, which makes it a convenient setting in which to
test how detector modelling and data reduction choices affect the recovered
atmospheric spectrum.

% Question: How does transmission spectroscopy probe an atmosphere?
% Answer: During transit, wavelength-dependent atmospheric opacity changes the
% apparent planetary radius and therefore the fraction of blocked starlight.
% Evidence: Seager and Sasselov (2000) and Brown (2001).

Transmission spectroscopy measures how the depth of a transit depends on
wavelength. Ignoring limb darkening for the moment, the transit depth is
approximately
\begin{equation}
  \delta(\lambda) \simeq
  \left[\frac{R_{\mathrm p}(\lambda)}{R_\star}\right]^2 ,
  \label{eq:transit-depth}
\end{equation}
where $\delta(\lambda)$ is the transit depth, i.e.\ the fractional drop in
stellar flux during transit, $R_{\mathrm p}(\lambda)$ is the effective
radius of the planet at wavelength $\lambda$ and $R_\star$ is the radius of
the star. The effective radius is larger at wavelengths where the atmosphere
is more opaque, and smaller where it is more transparent. Absorption by
atoms, molecules, clouds and aerosols can therefore imprint
wavelength-dependent structure on the measured transit depth
\citep{SeagerSasselov2000,Brown2001}. This geometry, and how it gives rise to
a transmission spectrum, is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:transmission-spectroscopy}.

% Question: Why are atmospheric transmission signals difficult to measure?
% Answer: Only the starlight passing through a thin annulus around the planet
% probes the atmosphere, so the atmospheric signal is a small fraction of the
% total stellar flux.
% Evidence: Seager and Sasselov (2000) and Brown (2001).
Only the starlight which passes through a thin annulus around the planet
probes its atmosphere, and so the wavelength-dependent atmospheric signal is
a small fraction of the total stellar flux. The signal is stronger when the
planet is large compared to its host star, and when its atmosphere extends
over a large range of altitudes. High temperatures, atmospheres dominated by
light gases such as hydrogen and helium, and weak gravity all make an
atmosphere more extended, whilst clouds and hazes can hide spectral
features. How easily a transmission signal can be measured therefore depends
on both the atmosphere and the geometry of the planet and star
\citep{SeagerSasselov2000,Brown2001}.

% Question: Why begin method development with hot Jupiters?
% Answer: Their large radii, high temperatures, hydrogen-dominated atmospheres
% and often low gravities make their atmospheric signals comparatively strong,
% so reduction errors are easier to diagnose.
% Evidence: Charbonneau et al. (2002), Brown (2001), and Madhusudhan (2019).
Hot Jupiters are especially favourable targets for transmission
spectroscopy. They are gas giants which orbit close to their host stars and
are therefore strongly irradiated. Their large radii, high temperatures,
hydrogen-dominated atmospheres and, in some cases, low gravities make their
transmission signals comparatively strong \citep{Brown2001,Madhusudhan2019}.
Indeed, the first detection of an exoplanet atmosphere was the sodium
absorption found in the hot Jupiter HD~209458~b \citep{CharbonneauEtAl2002}.
Their strong signals also make hot Jupiters useful for developing and
testing methods which recover transmission spectra from spectroscopic time
series, since errors in data reduction and light-curve fitting are easier to
diagnose when the signal is large, before moving on to planets whose
atmospheric signals are intrinsically weaker. They are only a starting
point, however: once a method has been validated on these favourable
targets, it can be tested, and if necessary revalidated, on smaller and
cooler planets, including those most relevant to questions of
habitability.

% Question: What complementary information comes from emission and phase
% curves?
% Answer: Secondary eclipses constrain dayside emission, while phase curves
% track the changing planetary flux through the orbit and probe longitudinal
% temperature and composition.
% Evidence: Deming et al. (2005), Knutson et al. (2007), Coulombe et al.
% (2023), Mikal-Evans et al. (2023), Kempton et al. (2023), Gressier et al.
% (2024), and Splinter et al. (2025).

Transmission is one of three main time-series geometries used for
atmospheric spectroscopy. Secondary-eclipse measurements isolate the
emission from the dayside of the planet
\citep{DemingEtAl2005,CoulombeEtAl2023,GressierEtAl2024}, whilst phase
curves follow the changing contribution of the planet over its orbit and
constrain the day--night temperature contrast, heat redistribution and
longitudinal structure
\citep{KnutsonEtAl2007,MikalEvansEtAl2023,KemptonEtAl2023,
SplinterEtAl2025}. Each geometry probes the atmosphere in a different way, i.e.\ the limb in transmission, the dayside in emission and
the longitudinal structure in a phase curve. This project begins with
transmission spectroscopy, and emission and phase-curve analyses are planned
as future applications of the same detector-level framework.

% Question: Why is atmospheric characterisation an inverse problem?
% Answer: Atmospheric signals are mixed with stellar, instrumental and
% geometric effects, and different physical states can produce similar spectra.
% Evidence: Rackham et al. (2018), Madhusudhan (2019), and Barstow and Heng
% (2020).
In all three geometries, the atmosphere is not observed in isolation. Its
signal is embedded in the light of the host star, and it is modified by the
telescope, the detector, the observing geometry and the data analysis.
Furthermore, different combinations of abundances, temperature, reference
radius, clouds, hazes and stellar heterogeneity can produce similar spectral
signatures \citep{RackhamEtAl2018,Madhusudhan2019}. Atmospheric
characterisation is therefore an inverse problem, in which the data
constrain a family of possible physical explanations instead of directly
measuring a unique atmospheric state \citep{BarstowHeng2020}, and so any
atmospheric interpretation can only be as reliable as the transmission
spectrum on which it is based.

% Question: Is a transmission spectrum the final atmospheric result?
% Answer: No. Detector data are first used to infer a transmission spectrum,
% which is then interpreted with forward models or atmospheric retrievals.
% Evidence: Brown (2001) and Madhusudhan (2019).

The transmission spectrum is itself an inferred data product: the
detector measurements are first calibrated and modelled to estimate the
transit depth as a function of wavelength, and a separate atmospheric
forward model or retrieval then maps this spectrum to composition,
temperature, clouds and other physical quantities
\citep{Brown2001,Madhusudhan2019}. Errors can enter at either stage: a
retrieval can be statistically precise whilst inheriting a bias from the
recovered spectrum, and a well-recovered spectrum can still allow several
atmospheric interpretations. This work is mainly concerned with the first
stage, i.e.\ with how accurately the transmission spectrum itself is
recovered, although the last of its objectives
(Section~\ref{sec:research-question}) also asks how errors in this stage
propagate into the second.

\subsection{JWST, NIRISS/SOSS, and the WASP-17~b case study}
\label{sec:jwst-soss-wasp17}
\label{sec:arecibo-to-jwst}
\label{sec:soss-wasp17}

% Question: Where did the targets for atmospheric measurements come from?
% Answer: Three decades of discovery, from pulsar timing and radial velocities
% through the Kepler and TESS surveys, produced a census of thousands of
% planets and the bright targets suited to atmospheric follow-up.
% Evidence: Wolszczan and Frail (1992), Mayor and Queloz (1995), Borucki et
% al. (2010), Winn and Fabrycky (2015), Ricker et al. (2015), and the NASA
% Exoplanet Archive (2026).

The planets which are now targeted for atmospheric measurements, and the
facilities used to observe them, are the result of three decades of
discovery and instrument development. Pulsar timing at Arecibo revealed the
first confirmed exoplanets, around PSR~B1257+12 \citep{WolszczanFrail1992},
and radial-velocity measurements of 51~Pegasi~b with ELODIE established a
class of close-in giant planet which had not been anticipated from the
architecture of the Solar System \citep{MayorQueloz1995}. Kepler then turned
these isolated detections into a population, by monitoring approximately
150,000 stars for transits, which made it possible to measure planet
occurrence, sizes, periods and system architectures
\citep{BoruckiEtAl2010,WinnFabrycky2015}. TESS surveyed nearly the whole sky
and prioritised bright, nearby host stars, which are the most favourable for
mass measurements and atmospheric follow-up \citep{RickerEtAl2015}. Together
with discoveries from other facilities, these surveys have taken the number
of confirmed exoplanets beyond 6,000 \citep{NASAExoplanetArchive2026}.

% Question: What could atmospheric observations measure before JWST, and what
% limited those measurements?
% Answer: HST and Spitzer established the transmission, emission and
% phase-curve techniques, but broad spectra were commonly assembled from
% restricted bandpasses observed with different instruments at different
% epochs.
% Evidence: Charbonneau et al. (2002), Deming et al. (2005), Charbonneau et al.
% (2005), Knutson et al. (2007), and Sing et al. (2016).

The Hubble Space Telescope (HST) and Spitzer showed that the atmospheres of
transiting exoplanets could be characterised. The sodium detection in
HD~209458~b mentioned above was the first spectroscopic detection of an
exoplanet atmosphere \citep{CharbonneauEtAl2002}, and it was followed by
broadband Spitzer measurements of the thermal emission of HD~209458~b and
TrES-1~b \citep{DemingEtAl2005,CharbonneauEtAl2005}, a Spitzer phase curve of
HD~189733~b \citep{KnutsonEtAl2007}, and comparative programmes such as the
ten-planet survey of \citet{SingEtAl2016}, which found a continuum from
clear to cloudy atmospheres. These studies established the transmission,
emission and phase-curve techniques which are still used today. However,
when broad wavelength coverage was required, it was commonly assembled from
the restricted bandpasses of different instruments, observed at different
epochs.

% Question: What changed with JWST?
% Answer: JWST combines a large cold aperture with stable time-series modes and
% broad near- to mid-infrared coverage, so several absorbers and continuum
% regions can be measured within a single visit, reducing the reliance on
% observations stitched across instruments and epochs.
% Evidence: Gardner et al. (2006) and Rigby et al. (2023).

JWST was designed as a 6.5-metre, cryogenic, infrared-optimised observatory,
with instruments covering the near- to mid-infrared \citep{GardnerEtAl2006}.
Unlike Kepler and TESS, JWST is not a wide-field transit survey, and for the
time-series spectroscopy considered here it is used to follow up planets
whose transit times are already known, in order to characterise their
atmospheres. Its collecting area, cold optics and suite of instruments give
access to several molecular bands over a much broader wavelength range than
a single HST or Spitzer mode, and commissioning showed that the sensitivity,
stability and spectral performance of the observatory met or exceeded the
mission requirements in most areas \citep{RigbyEtAl2023}. JWST can therefore
measure smaller wavelength-dependent changes in transit depth, and it can
cover several atmospheric absorbers and continuum regions within a single
observing mode and visit. This reduces the need to combine measurements
taken with different instruments or at different epochs, although it does
not remove it.

% Question: Why does this project begin with NIRISS/SOSS, and what makes the
% mode useful and difficult?
% Answer: The project starts from a single SOSS transit observation, and the
% curved, overlapping SOSS orders make spectral extraction particularly
% difficult.
% Evidence: Gardner et al. (2006), Albert et al. (2023), and Louie et al.
% (2025).

JWST carries four science instruments, NIRCam, NIRSpec, MIRI and NIRISS
\citep{RigbyEtAl2023,AlbertEtAl2023}, several of which support time-series
spectroscopy of exoplanets. This project starts with the Single Object Slitless Spectroscopy (SOSS) mode
of NIRISS, and with a single observation: a complete transit of WASP-17~b
\citep{LouieEtAl2025}.

SOSS uses the GR700XD disperser, which combines a grism with a
cross-dispersing prism and spreads the light into several spectral orders,
displaced from each other in the perpendicular (cross-dispersion)
direction. Together they cover approximately $0.6$--$2.8\,\mu\mathrm{m}$,
with a resolving power of about $R=650$ at $1.25\,\mu\mathrm{m}$
\citep{AlbertEtAl2023}. The prism curves the orders on the detector, and
because limited mechanical clearance restricted the angles of the prism and
grism, the first two orders partly overlap \citep{AlbertEtAl2023}, which
makes spectral extraction particularly difficult. A weak cylindrical lens
spreads the light over roughly 23 detector rows in the cross-dispersion
direction. Because the light is spread over more pixels, no single pixel
saturates as quickly, and this allows bright host stars to be observed
\citep{AlbertEtAl2023}. The bandpass contains several water bands, the
potassium doublet, and regions which can constrain other absorbers and
optical scattering by clouds and hazes, although the exact sensitivity
depends on the atmosphere of the target
\citep{AlbertEtAl2023,HolmbergMadhusudhan2023,LouieEtAl2025}. The curved
footprints of the first and second orders, which I refer to as the traces,
are shown on a median SOSS detector image in
Figure~\ref{fig:soss-detector-orders}.

% Question: How precise is SOSS in practice?
% Answer: A broad-band SOSS light curve reached about 20 ppm scatter when
% averaged over 40 minutes during commissioning, but independent reductions of
% the same WASP-17 b observation report median spectroscopic uncertainties
% spanning 46--73 ppm.
% Evidence: Albert et al. (2023) and Louie et al. (2025).

JWST time-series spectroscopy must distinguish changes in transit depth of
tens to hundreds of parts per million (ppm). During commissioning, an
in-flight observation of the standard star BD+$60^{\circ}1753$ reached a
scatter of approximately 20~ppm, after a long-term trend had been removed
and the data averaged over 40-minute intervals \citep{AlbertEtAl2023}. This
on-sky test demonstrated the stability of SOSS over time, but it was not a
transit observation, and it did not measure the precision of
wavelength-dependent transit depths.

The 20~ppm figure refers to a white-light time series, which combines the
photons from the whole SOSS bandpass. A transmission spectrum instead
measures the transit depth separately in narrower wavelength channels. Each
channel contains only part of the total photon count, and so normally has a
larger fractional photon noise than the white-light measurement
\citep{AlbertEtAl2023,LouieEtAl2025}. For the WASP-17~b transit, three separate reductions of the same
observation reported median
uncertainties of 46--73~ppm per spectral channel across orders 1 and 2, the
two orders which carry useful signal \citep{LouieEtAl2025}. This range does
not identify which reduction is the most accurate, but it does show that
the precision assigned to a transmission spectrum depends on the analysis,
on a scale which is relevant to the atmospheric signal.

% Question: What has SOSS already delivered, and what analysis difficulties
% have those observations exposed?
% Answer: SOSS has recovered molecular, atomic and cloud signatures, but the
% analyses must treat background, readout noise, overlapping orders, field-star
% contamination and wavelength-correlated residuals.
% Evidence: Feinstein et al. (2023), Radica et al. (2023), and Holmberg and
% Madhusudhan (2023).

The first SOSS analyses of exoplanets showed that the mode can recover
detailed atmospheric structure; for example, the Early Release Science
spectrum of WASP-39~b contained multiple water bands, potassium absorption
and evidence of clouds \citep{FeinsteinEtAl2023}. Every JWST
transit-spectroscopy analysis has to deal with detector calibration, bad
pixels and cosmic rays, background emission, readout structure, wavelength
calibration, spectral assignment, limb darkening and time-dependent noise.
SOSS adds its own combination of difficulties: curved orders which
physically overlap, contamination by the spectra of field stars (because the
mode is slitless), and a zodiacal background with a sharp step near detector
column 700 \citep{AlbertEtAl2023,RadicaEtAl2023,HolmbergMadhusudhan2023}. Recovering the atmospheric information requires choices about detector
calibration, extraction and light-curve fitting. For hot Jupiters the
atmospheric signals are strong enough for the effect of these choices to be
diagnosed, whilst the curved and overlapping traces expose the assumptions
made in the detector model, and together this makes SOSS observations of
hot Jupiters a good test case for comparing reduction methods.

% Question: Why begin method development with WASP-17 b?
% Answer: WASP-17 b is a low-density hot Jupiter with an extended atmosphere, a
% complete SOSS transit, earlier Hubble and Spitzer constraints, and three
% published reductions of the same NIRISS observation.
% Evidence: Anderson et al. (2010), Alderson et al. (2022), Louie et al.
% (2025), Espinoza et al. (2019), Espinoza (2022), Radica et al. (2023),
% and Radica (2024).

WASP-17~b is the first case study of this project. Its low density and low
gravity give it an extended atmosphere \citep{AndersonEtAl2010}.
\citet{AldersonEtAl2022} combined HST STIS and WFC3 spectra with Spitzer
IRAC photometry to build a $0.3$--$5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ transmission spectrum,
but their atmospheric interpretation still allowed both a high- and a
low-metallicity solution. \citet{LouieEtAl2025} then analysed a complete
NIRISS/SOSS transit with three separate reductions: Ahsoka, which they
introduced; \texttt{transitspectroscopy}
\citep{Espinoza2022TransitSpectroscopy} with \texttt{juliet}
\citep{EspinozaEtAl2019}; and supreme-SPOON \citep{RadicaEtAl2023}, which is
now distributed as exoTEDRF \citep{Radica2024}. The three spectra agreed
closely over most of the bandpass, and the wider SOSS coverage constrained
the water abundance more tightly than the earlier HST and Spitzer data had
\citep{LouieEtAl2025}. The combination of earlier observations, a complete
SOSS transit and several reductions of the same data makes WASP-17~b a
strong case for a controlled comparison of methods. Later stages of the
project will include other targets and further injected atmospheric
scenarios.

\subsection{From detector integrations to the validation problem}
\label{sec:detector-to-validation}
\label{sec:detector-to-spectrum}
\label{sec:validation-problem}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \input{figures/conventional_reduction_workflow}
  \caption{Conceptual structure shared by extraction-first JWST/NIRISS SOSS
  reductions. (a) Within an integration, the detector is read
  nondestructively several times, and each read (group) samples the
  accumulated charge. A cosmic ray adds a step to the accumulated counts.
  Because there are several groups, this step can be seen, and the count rate
  of the pixel can be fitted from the unaffected differences between reads
  instead of from the shifted absolute counts after the jump. (b) Each
  integration produces a two-dimensional count-rate image. After the
  background and noise have been treated, spectral extraction compresses the
  detector pixels into a one-dimensional spectrum. Binning in wavelength
  gives one light curve per channel, and fitting a transit model to each
  light curve gives the wavelength-dependent depths which make up the
  transmission spectrum. The exact implementation and order of these steps,
  including whether an operation is applied to groups or to count rates,
  differ between pipelines.}
  \label{fig:extraction-first-workflow}
\end{figure*}

% Question: Does JWST measure a transmission spectrum directly?
% Answer: No. It records nondestructive detector reads that are converted into
% count rates, calibrated images, extracted spectra, spectral light curves and
% fitted transit depths.
% Evidence: Bell et al. (2022), Albert et al. (2023), and Radica et al. (2023).

To see where the structural choices above enter, it helps to follow the data
from the detector. In a NIRISS/SOSS time-series observation, the detector is
read nondestructively several times during each integration, and in the
readout pattern used for these observations each read is recorded as one
group. Charge accumulates from one group to the
next, and a fit to this ramp gives a count rate for each pixel in each
integration. Repeating this throughout a visit produces a time series of
two-dimensional count-rate images \citep{AlbertEtAl2023}.

% Question: What is the shared extraction-first chain?
% Answer: Detector corrections and ramp fitting, background and noise
% treatment, compression of each image to a one-dimensional spectrum,
% per-channel light curves, and transit fitting produce the spectrum.
% Evidence: Bell et al. (2022), Radica et al. (2023), and Louie et al. (2025).

The conventional analyses considered here follow the extraction-first
workflow summarised in Figure~\ref{fig:extraction-first-workflow}. First, the official JWST calibration pipeline, or a custom variant of it,
identifies unusable data, flags saturated groups, subtracts the bias and dark
current, applies the reference-pixel and nonlinearity corrections, detects
jumps and fits the ramps; some pipelines also correct the low-frequency
($1/f$) readout noise at this group level, before the ramps are fitted.
Subsequent processing assigns a wavelength and trace position to each pixel,
and deals with the flat-field response, background, field-star
contamination, bad pixels and, in the other pipelines, the $1/f$ noise
\citep{BellEtAl2022,RadicaEtAl2023,LouieEtAl2025}. A box, optimal or
forward-modelled extraction then converts each two-dimensional image into a
one-dimensional spectrum \citep{Horne1986,DarveauBernierEtAl2022}. These
spectra are divided into wavelength channels, giving one light curve per
channel, and each light curve is fitted with a transit model, with any
remaining time dependence treated in a way that is specific to each
pipeline. Finally, the fitted planet-to-star radius ratio in each channel is
converted into a transit depth, and the depths are assembled as a function
of wavelength to form the transmission spectrum.

% Question: Where can analysis pipelines differ?
% Answer: At detector calibration, jump detection, 1/f correction, background
% scaling, trace estimation, extraction, outlier treatment, limb darkening,
% systematics models and posterior inference; the WASP-17 b branches made
% different time-systematics choices.
% Evidence: Bell et al. (2022), Radica et al. (2023), Holmberg and Madhusudhan
% (2023), and Louie et al. (2025).

Implementations of this chain differ, both in the order of the operations
and in the choices made at each step. Examples include jump-detection
thresholds, the handling of partially saturated ramps, bad-pixel masks, the
order and location of the $1/f$ correction, the scaling of the
zodiacal-background model, the treatment of dispersed field stars, trace
profiles, extraction widths, wavelength bins, limb-darkening priors and
time-domain systematics models
\citep{RadicaEtAl2023,HolmbergMadhusudhan2023,LouieEtAl2025}. The published
WASP-17~b comparison illustrates the last of these. Ahsoka used the Eureka!
light-curve framework \citep{BellEtAl2022} with polynomial time trends and a
multiplicative noise term. The \texttt{transitspectroscopy}/\texttt{juliet}
branch used an offset, an additional white-noise term and a
Mat\'ern-$3/2$ Gaussian process, which allows residuals that are close
together in time to be correlated. Finally, supreme-SPOON used no separate
time-systematics function, but included an additive uncertainty term
\citep{LouieEtAl2025}.

% Question: What does pipeline disagreement establish?
% Answer: Sensitivity to analysis choices, but not which result is closer to
% the unknown true spectrum.
% Evidence: Constantinou et al. (2023), Holmberg and Madhusudhan (2023), and
% Kirk et al. (2024).

Each step of this chain therefore has defensible alternatives, and different
combinations of them are in active use, so it is worth asking what a
comparison of the resulting spectra can establish. Firstly, when
independent reductions disagree, this shows that the spectrum is sensitive
to methodological choices, and it may reveal an uncertainty which has not
been modelled. For the 3--5~$\mu\mathrm{m}$ NIRSpec spectrum of WASP-39~b, for example,
differences between reductions changed the retrieved abundances by up to
about an order of magnitude and affected the detectability of weaker
species
\citep{ConstantinouEtAl2023}. For the sub-Earth GJ~341~b, three reductions
all supported the broad conclusion that no atmosphere was detected, yet two
of the pipelines produced weak candidate features at different wavelengths,
and consequently favoured different molecular explanations
\citep{KirkEtAl2024}. Comparisons like these show how sensitive a spectrum
is to the analysis, but a difference between two spectra does not by itself
identify which one is more accurate.

% Question: What does pipeline agreement establish?
% Answer: Reproducibility across the tested analyses, but shared data,
% reference files, code and assumptions can preserve a common bias.
% Evidence: Louie et al. (2025), Bell et al. (2022), and the shared components
% documented by the pipeline papers.

Secondly, when reductions agree, as the three WASP-17~b reductions do over
most of the SOSS bandpass \citep{LouieEtAl2025}, this is strong evidence that
the result is reproducible across those implementations. The analyses do,
however, start from the same observation, use the same calibration
reference files and deliberately standardise several choices, and some of
them share software components, so they are not fully independent at every
stage \citep{BellEtAl2022,RadicaEtAl2023,LouieEtAl2025}. Where the analyses
made different choices and still recovered similar spectra, this suggests
that the net effect of those choices was small compared with the quoted
uncertainties in this case, although their individual effects cannot be
separated and could partly cancel. Agreement cannot,
however, reveal an assumption which all three share, since such an
assumption could bias every spectrum in a similar way and still leave them
in agreement.

% Question: Why can neither agreement nor disagreement establish absolute
% accuracy on real observations?
% Answer: The exact planetary transmission spectrum is not independently known.
% Evidence: The distinction between reproducibility and accuracy follows from
% the absence of an external truth spectrum for a real planet.

Both cases come back to the problem raised at the start of this section,
which is that a real planet has no independently known transmission
spectrum against which a recovered spectrum can be scored. Agreement between
reductions can establish reproducibility, and disagreement can expose
sensitivity, but neither directly measures the absolute error of a
recovery. The residuals of a model fit do not solve this problem
either, because an incorrect split of the observed light into source,
background and instrument terms can still fit the pixels well. Absolute
validation therefore requires data for which the spectrum that generated
them is known. Simulated SOSS observations with known input spectra have
already been used to validate individual steps; ATOCA, for example, recovers
the input spectrum to within 10~ppm when its calibration inputs are correct
\citep{DarveauBernierEtAl2022}. Such simulations, however, only contain the
noise and background that were modelled.

\subsection{Two structural hypotheses and the response of this project}
\label{sec:structural-limitations-inference}
\label{sec:structural-limitations}
\label{sec:benchmark-and-nova}

% Question: What separates the epistemic problem from the modelling
% hypotheses?
% Answer: Real data lack ground truth whatever the method, whilst background
% treatment and pixel-to-spectrum compression are concrete, testable choices
% of the conventional chain.
% Evidence: The validation problem of Section 1.3 and the benchmark design in
% Methods.

Whilst the lack of a ground truth affects every method, the two structural
choices introduced at the start of this section, i.e.\ how the background is
treated and how the detector pixels are compressed into one-dimensional
spectra, are specific to the conventional chain. I treat each of them as a
hypothesis about where recovery error could enter, without presuming that
either one causes a bias, and a benchmark with a known truth can then test
whether either affects the recovered spectra at a relevant level.

% Question: Why does background treatment require particular attention?
% Answer: Published methods either subtract the background before extraction
% or refine it during extraction, but do not infer it jointly with the
% transit.
% Evidence: Radica et al. (2023), Holmberg and Madhusudhan (2023), and Louie et
% al. (2025).

The first hypothesis concerns the treatment of the background, and
published methods do not all treat it in the same way. In the Ahsoka and
supreme-SPOON implementations used for WASP-17~b, scaled versions of the
STScI zodiacal-background model were subtracted before the extracted light
curves were fitted, and the adopted scaling was not left free in the later
transit fit. The \texttt{transitspectroscopy}/\texttt{juliet} branch
followed the same general order \citep{LouieEtAl2025}. JExoRES is an
important exception. It fits a residual background level at the same time as
the fluxes of the overlapping orders during its multi-order extraction
\citep{HolmbergMadhusudhan2023}, and so it models the background at the
extraction stage instead of simply fixing it. Even in JExoRES, however, the
background is fitted as part of the extraction of a stellar spectrum, whilst
the time-dependent transit is only fitted in the next stage.

What matters is therefore whether the background and the
wavelength-dependent transit are inferred together; a pipeline can model its
background carefully and still fix it before the transit is fitted. Because
the transit dims the stellar light but not the additive background, the
difference between in-transit and out-of-transit images shows where the
stellar light falls. In a single pixel, however, the fractional depth and a
constant additive term cannot be separated, since the data only fix the
out-of-transit level and the absolute size of the dip. The split becomes possible because the same depth applies to many pixels
whose ratios of starlight to background differ, provided that the
background is described by far fewer parameters than there are pixels and
does not itself vary in time like a transit. This spatial information is
lost once the pixels are summed into a light curve. If the split between them is fixed before the transit
model is introduced, this information can no longer revise it. None of the
published implementations considered here infers the background jointly
with the transit signal in the same likelihood over detector pixels: the
background is either subtracted before extraction or refined together with
it \citep{HolmbergMadhusudhan2023,LouieEtAl2025}.

Once the spectrum has been extracted, the transit fit only sees flux
estimates, and the background is no longer an explicit part of that fit;
all that remains of it is any residual error and its propagated
uncertainty. The transit fit can only account for this uncertainty if the
extraction passes it on, including its correlations across wavelength. In
the published WASP-17~b comparison, the three branches report per-channel
flux uncertainties and downstream noise terms, but they do not describe
passing on a wavelength-correlated covariance associated specifically with
the background subtraction. The transit fit therefore cannot correct an
error in how the detector counts were divided between the stellar source and
the additive background.

% Question: How can background error affect a transmission spectrum?
% Answer: Additive light allocated incorrectly between source and background
% changes the fractional transit depth, and can introduce wavelength-dependent
% offsets or slopes.
% Evidence: Kelson (2003), Radica et al. (2023), and Holmberg and Madhusudhan
% (2023).
Background and transit enter the data in different ways: the background is
added to the stellar light, whereas the transit multiplies it. To be
explicit, if a constant amount of flux is wrongly assigned to the
background, the inferred out-of-transit stellar flux is wrong by the same
amount, and since the transit depth is measured as a fraction of this flux,
the depth is biased; assigning too much flux to the background, for
example, makes the transit appear deeper. If the error varies across the
detector, it can change spectral offsets, slopes or local features.
Classical two-dimensional spectroscopy has already shown the value of
modelling the background in the native detector geometry, before any
resampling \citep{Kelson2003}. For slitless SOSS data, the problem also
includes structured zodiacal light, field-star spectra, masked pixels and
$1/f$ readout noise \citep{RadicaEtAl2023,HolmbergMadhusudhan2023}.

% Question: Is one-dimensional extraction itself a mistake?
% Answer: No. Optimal extraction can be statistically efficient when its
% spatial profile and noise model are correct.
% Evidence: Horne (1986).

The second hypothesis concerns the compression of detector pixels into
one-dimensional spectra. A simple sum over pixels can lose the information
needed to distinguish stellar light from background, defective pixels or
overlapping wavelengths. Extraction does not have to lose information,
however. If the spatial profile, background, detector response, bad-pixel
mask and noise model are all correct, the optimal extraction of
\citet{Horne1986} combines the pixels of each column with weights
proportional to the spatial profile divided by the variance, and can give an
efficient summary of the two-dimensional data. Problems arise when
uncertain parts of the detector model, such as the spatial profile or the
background, are fixed during the compression, or when their covariance and
the description of the instrument response are not passed on, since a later
light-curve fit can then no longer revisit how the light was divided
between source and background, or how individual pixels were treated.

% Question: When can extraction lose information relevant to later inference?
% Answer: When the compression fixes an imperfect detector model, or omits the
% covariance and resolution description needed to reconstruct the pixel
% likelihood; overlapping SOSS orders make this concrete.
% Evidence: Bolton and Schlegel (2010), Bolton et al. (2012), Darveau-Bernier
% et al. (2022), and Radica et al. (2022).

The spectro-perfectionism method of \citet{BoltonSchlegel2010} makes precise
when such a compression loses no information. It models the detector image
with the full two-dimensional point-spread function, and shows that a
one-dimensional product can preserve the pixel-level likelihood if the
extracted spectrum is delivered together with a suitable covariance and
resolution description \citep{BoltonEtAl2012}. Conversely, if the trace, the
blending of neighbouring wavelengths by the point-spread function, the
background, the bad-pixel treatment or the decomposition into orders is
fixed incorrectly, and the resulting covariance is not carried forward, the
later light-curve fit cannot recover the discarded information.

For SOSS, this condition is harder to meet where the first and second
spectral orders overlap on the detector, since some pixels there contain
light from both. The ATOCA extraction algorithm addresses this by building a
linear model for every detector pixel, in which both orders are produced by
one underlying incident spectrum, which is solved for directly; for a typical box extraction, ignoring the contamination between the orders
would bias transit spectra by about 1\% of the strength of their spectral
features \citep{DarveauBernierEtAl2022}. Its accuracy depends on the trace profiles,
wavelength maps, throughputs and spectral-resolution kernels, and the
APPLESOSS package \citep{RadicaEtAl2022} estimates the spatial profiles that
ATOCA needs directly from the observation.

% Question: What limitation remains even after a sophisticated extraction?
% Answer: Existing extraction models estimate the stellar spectrum before
% fitting the time-dependent transit, so the transit model cannot inform the
% detector-pixel decomposition into source, background and overlapping
% orders under the adopted response.
% Evidence: Darveau-Bernier et al. (2022), Bell et al. (2022), and Radica et al.
% (2023).

Even sophisticated extraction models keep this two-stage structure. ATOCA,
for example, treats the incident spectrum in each integration as the unknown
quantity, and the time-dependent transit parameters are only inferred
afterwards \citep{DarveauBernierEtAl2022,BellEtAl2022,RadicaEtAl2023}. This
ordering is practical, and it may be entirely adequate. However, it means
that the transit model cannot be used, within a single likelihood, to help
separate the stellar light, the additive background and the contributions
of the overlapping orders under the adopted detector response. Whether this
causes a meaningful bias in SOSS transmission spectra is an empirical
question, and one which the benchmark described below is designed to answer.

% Question: How can absolute recovery accuracy be measured?
% Answer: Write a known transmission spectrum into real detector data, give
% identical data to independent recovery methods, and reveal the injected
% spectrum only after each prediction is fixed.
% Evidence: This is the controlled benchmark design developed in this project.

The validation problem and these two hypotheses together motivate a
benchmark at the detector level. An injector writes a known transmission
spectrum into the raw data of the integrations taken before the real transit
of WASP-17~b, so that the additive background, noise, detector flags and
field sources come from a real observation, whilst the injected transit, and
hence the spectrum of the planet, is known. The injected transit dims only
the light which the injector assigns to the target, and since it is not
known how much of the faint light far from the traces belongs to the star,
this assignment is bracketed (Section~\ref{sec:injector}). Every recovery method receives the same injected raw frames, and all methods
share the priors on the transit geometry, which are fixed in advance. The
injected spectrum is only revealed once each method has fixed its
prediction, so that it cannot influence the analysis. The recovery error can then be measured directly, as a function of
wavelength, spectral shape, order, signal level, background structure and
detector condition.

% Question: How does NOVA address the two structural hypotheses?
% Answer: NOVA fits calibrated detector pixels directly, propagates the stellar
% source and transit through a specified instrument response, and fits the
% transmission spectrum, additive background and nuisance components jointly,
% before any spectral compression.
% Evidence: The NOVA forward model described in the Methods section.

NOVA is the recovery method developed alongside this benchmark. Instead of
extracting a stellar spectrum and fitting spectral light curves in separate
stages, it evaluates a forward model directly in detector space. A single
wavelength-dependent transmission spectrum is shared between orders 1 and
2, propagated through the instrument response, and fitted together with the
limb darkening, the stellar continuum and the additive background. Only the
stellar light is multiplied by the transit model. This keeps the physical
distinction between source and background, and it allows information from
many detector pixels, and from both orders, to constrain the same
transmission spectrum. NOVA still depends on steps taken before the fit, namely the detector
processing, which subtracts a scaled model of the background, a spatial
response estimated from the same data, and a transit geometry from a
white-light fit to optimally extracted light curves (Section~\ref{sec:nova}),
but the background which remains is refitted together with the transit.

% Question: Why are both the benchmark and NOVA necessary?
% Answer: NOVA proposes a different recovery model, whilst the benchmark
% determines whether that model is accurate and under which conditions it
% improves on extraction-first analyses.
% Evidence: The separation of injector, recovery and scoring in the benchmark
% protocol.

The benchmark and NOVA address different parts of the problem. As noted in
Section~\ref{sec:detector-to-validation}, a good fit to the pixels does not
prove that the stellar, background and transit components were separated
correctly, and so NOVA cannot validate itself. The benchmark supplies the
missing truth, and it allows a controlled comparison with extraction-based
methods. It can show whether the established pipelines already recover the injected
spectra without a detectable bias, and whether NOVA improves on them; where
every method tested fails, it points instead to an inadequate instrument
model or injector.

\subsection{Research question and objectives}
\label{sec:research-question}

% Question: What is the central research question?
% Answer: How accurately and robustly can JWST/NIRISS SOSS transmission spectra
% be recovered from detector-level time-series observations, and can joint
% detector-level inference reduce biases associated with background treatment
% and extraction-first analysis?
% Evidence: The validation problem and structural limitations established in
% the preceding sections.

The central research question of this project is:
\begin{quote}
\raggedright\itshape
How accurately and robustly can JWST/NIRISS SOSS transmission spectra
be recovered from detector-level time-series observations, and can joint
detector-level inference reduce biases associated with background treatment and
extraction-first analysis?
\end{quote}

% Question: Which objectives make the research question testable?
% Answer: Build a truth-known benchmark, develop NOVA, compare recovery methods
% under controlled conditions, and identify the assumptions that limit
% accuracy.
% Evidence: The Methods and future-work programme of this assessment.

To make this question testable, I divide it into four objectives:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.,leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item develop an independent detector-level benchmark with known injected
  transmission spectra;
  \item develop NOVA as a joint detector-level inference method for
  NIRISS/SOSS transmission spectroscopy;
  \item compare NOVA with extraction-based analyses under controlled
  observing conditions and atmospheric scenarios; and
  \item identify the detector, calibration and modelling assumptions which
  limit recovery accuracy, and determine how these limitations propagate into
  atmospheric interpretation.
\end{enumerate}

% Question: What is the scope of the present assessment?
% Answer: The assessment defines the problem, documents the current NOVA
% formulation and the injector, and sets out the benchmark programme, without
% claiming results that have not yet passed their checks.
% Evidence: The Methods, Results and Current Status, and Discussion and Future
% Work sections.

This assessment defines the problem, documents the current method and sets
out the experiments needed to answer the research question. As described in Section~\ref{sec:jwst-soss-wasp17}, WASP-17~b is the first
case study. The production stage of the benchmark
(Section~\ref{sec:envelope}) extends the injections to five further SOSS
visits, of HAT-P-26, WASP-39, HAT-P-18, WASP-96 and WASP-80, and later
experiments will vary the background, spectral shape, noise, masking,
instrument response and target properties before the method is applied to
further observations. In this assessment I only report results for
which the injector and recovery checks described in
Section~\ref{sec:methods} have been completed.
