\section{Introduction and Literature Review}
\label{sec:introduction}

The transmission spectrum of an exoplanet is the depth of its transit, the
fraction of starlight that the planet blocks, at each wavelength. It is
obtained from a time series of detector images through a chain of processing
steps. Errors could enter at two of these steps. Firstly, the background light
is subtracted before the transit is fitted. An error in that subtraction can bias the fitted transit depth, even when the transit model fits the light curve well. Secondly, each image is compressed into a one-dimensional spectrum, which can lose
information that the later steps cannot recover. I have therefore developed
Nonlinear Order-coupled Variable-projection Analysis (NOVA). NOVA does not
compress the images into spectra. It fits the transit to the detector pixels
themselves, and in the same fit it fits whatever background the earlier
subtraction has left behind. Whether NOVA or the established methods give the
more accurate spectrum cannot be decided from real data, because the true
spectrum of a real planet is unknown. I am therefore building a benchmark in
which a known transit is added to the raw data of a real observation from the
James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), so that the error of each method, NOVA
included, can be measured against a known truth.

\subsection{Exoplanet atmospheres and transmission spectroscopy}
\label{sec:atmospheres-transmission}

Exoplanets span a wide range of sizes, temperatures and orbits, many with no
counterpart in the Solar System \citep{WinnFabrycky2015}. Their mass and
radius give their mean density, but planets with similar masses and radii can
be made of different mixtures of rock, ice and gas \citep{RogersSeager2010}.
The atmosphere helps to distinguish between these possible compositions. The composition of the atmosphere
can also hint at where and how the planet formed \citep{Madhusudhan2019}, and some planets are observed to lose gas from their upper atmosphere \citep{VidalMadjarEtAl2003}.

The atmosphere can be studied when the planet transits, that is, passes in front of its star, because part of the starlight then passes through the atmosphere. Measuring the transit at many wavelengths is called
transmission spectroscopy. If the stellar disc were uniformly bright (in reality it dims towards its edge, an effect called limb darkening), the depth of a transit would be approximately
\begin{equation}
  \delta(\lambda) \simeq
  \left[\frac{R_{\mathrm p}(\lambda)}{R_\star}\right]^2 ,
  \label{eq:transit-depth}
\end{equation}
where $\delta(\lambda)$ is the fractional drop in stellar flux during transit,
$R_{\mathrm p}(\lambda)$ the effective radius of the planet at wavelength
$\lambda$ and $R_\star$ the radius of the star. The effective radius is larger
at wavelengths where the atmosphere absorbs more strongly, because the
atmosphere then becomes opaque at a higher altitude. Absorption and scattering by atoms, molecules, clouds and aerosols therefore makes the transit depth vary with
wavelength \citep{SeagerSasselov2000,Brown2001}, as
Figure~\ref{fig:transmission-spectroscopy} illustrates.

\begin{figure*}[t]

  \caption{Principle of transmission spectroscopy. (a) During transit, the
  opaque body of the planet blocks starlight, whilst a small fraction of the
  starlight passes through its atmospheric annulus. The altitude at which
  the atmosphere becomes opaque, and hence the apparent radius of the
  planet, depends on wavelength. (b) Where the atmosphere is more opaque,
  $R_{\mathrm p}(\lambda)$ is larger and the transit is deeper.}
  \label{fig:transmission-spectroscopy}
\end{figure*}

Only a thin ring of atmosphere around the planet filters the starlight, so the atmospheric signal, the part of the transit depth that changes with wavelength, is a small fraction of the stellar flux. The signal is
largest for planets that are large relative to their star and whose
atmospheres are hot, rich in hydrogen and held by weak gravity, because such
atmospheres extend further above the planet
\citep{SeagerSasselov2000,Brown2001}. A class of planets that satisfies these
conditions is the so-called hot Jupiters, gas giants that orbit close to their
stars \citep{Brown2001,Madhusudhan2019}. It is no coincidence that the first
exoplanet atmosphere to be detected, through the sodium absorption of
HD~209458~b \citep{CharbonneauEtAl2002}, belonged to a hot Jupiter. Because the signal of a hot Jupiter is strong, an error introduced by the processing is easier to see against it. I therefore develop my methods on hot Jupiters before they are applied to smaller and cooler planets.

Transmission spectroscopy is one of three ways to observe an atmosphere as the
planet moves along its orbit. In a secondary eclipse, the planet passes behind
the star, and the drop in light isolates the light emitted or reflected by the dayside of the planet \citep{DemingEtAl2005,CoulombeEtAl2023,GressierEtAl2024}. A phase
curve follows the brightness of the planet around its orbit. It constrains the
temperature contrast between the day and night sides, how heat is carried
around the planet and how the atmosphere varies with longitude
\citep{KnutsonEtAl2007,MikalEvansEtAl2023,KemptonEtAl2023,SplinterEtAl2025}.
I start with transmission because a transit has a well-defined geometry that
repeats with every orbit. A transit is therefore a convenient case in which to
test how the processing of the data affects the recovered spectrum. Secondary
eclipses are a planned later application of my methods
(Section~\ref{sec:plan}).

Whichever technique is used, the properties of the atmosphere are then
inferred from the measured spectrum by a statistical fit called a retrieval
\citep{BarstowHeng2020}. A retrieval does not always give a unique answer,
because different combinations of abundances, temperature, clouds, hazes and
uneven brightness of the stellar surface can produce similar spectra
\citep{RackhamEtAl2018,Madhusudhan2019}. The retrieval also takes the measured
spectrum as given, so a bias in the spectrum can propagate into the retrieved atmosphere, however precise the retrieval. This project therefore mainly
concerns the step before the retrieval, namely how accurately the spectrum
itself is recovered from the detector data. Objective~4
(Section~\ref{sec:research-question}) also asks how errors in the spectrum
propagate into the retrieval. Testing how accurately the spectrum is recovered first requires an
instrument that can measure small changes in transit depth, and a planet whose
signal is strong. The next subsection introduces the telescope, the instrument
and the planet that I use.

\subsection{JWST, NIRISS/SOSS and WASP-17~b}
\label{sec:jwst-soss-wasp17}

For exoplanets, the main task of JWST, as of the Hubble Space Telescope (HST), is to measure the properties of planets that are already known, which is called characterisation.
HST and the Spitzer Space Telescope established the techniques for studying
the atmospheres of such planets
\citep{CharbonneauEtAl2002,DemingEtAl2005,CharbonneauEtAl2005,KnutsonEtAl2007}.
However, a broad spectrum often had to be combined from observations with
several instruments at different times. JWST characterises atmospheres better, because
its 6.5-metre mirror and infrared instruments \citep{GardnerEtAl2006} let it
measure smaller changes in transit depth. A single observation with one
instrument mode can also cover the absorption bands of several atoms and
molecules. Many of the planets characterised with JWST were first identified by transit surveys, which build on more than three decades of detection work. Detection began with planets around a pulsar
\citep{WolszczanFrail1992} and a planet around a Sun-like star
\citep{MayorQueloz1995}. Surveys such as Kepler and the Transiting Exoplanet
Survey Satellite (TESS) \citep{BoruckiEtAl2010,RickerEtAl2015} have since
raised the number of confirmed exoplanets to more than 6,000
\citep{NASAExoplanetArchive2026}. Many of these planets orbit bright nearby
stars, the most favourable targets for follow-up observations.

In this project, I use one of the four science instruments of JWST, the Near
Infrared Imager and Slitless Spectrograph (NIRISS), in its Single Object
Slitless Spectroscopy (SOSS) mode \citep{AlbertEtAl2023}.
Figure~\ref{fig:soss-detector-orders} shows how SOSS spreads the light of a
star over the detector. An optical element called GR700XD, a grism combined with a prism, spreads the light into several copies of the spectrum, called orders
\citep{AlbertEtAl2023}. Orders 1 and 2 together cover about
$0.6$-$2.8\,\mu\mathrm{m}$. The resolving power $\lambda/\Delta\lambda$ of the first order is about 650 at $1.25\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, where $\Delta\lambda$ is the smallest
difference in wavelength that SOSS can separate. On the detector, each order
forms a curved stripe of light, called its trace. A weak cylindrical lens
spreads the light over about 23 detector rows, so that bright stars can be
observed without saturating the pixels. The wavelength range contains several
water bands, the two potassium lines and regions sensitive to other absorbers
and to clouds and hazes
\citep{AlbertEtAl2023,HolmbergMadhusudhan2023,LouieEtAl2025}.

  hyperbolic sine (\(\operatorname{asinh}\)) stretch keeps both the bright

SOSS measures the total light of a star very precisely. This precision was
tested during commissioning, the test period before science operations, on the A-type standard star BD$+$60$^{\circ}$1753 \citep{AlbertEtAl2023}. The light
of all wavelengths was summed into a white-light curve, which gives the
brightness of the star as a function of time. A slow trend was subtracted
from this curve, and the remaining points were averaged in 40-minute bins.
The binned points then scattered by only about 20 parts per million (ppm), with the scatter decreasing under binning as expected for photon noise \citep{AlbertEtAl2023}. Photon noise
is the random variation in the number of detected photons, and its relative
size falls as more photons are collected. The spectrum, by contrast, is measured in channels, each a narrow range of wavelengths. A channel receives far fewer photons than the white-light curve, so the transit depth in each channel is less precise than the white-light depth.

However, SOSS data are difficult to analyse for three reasons. Firstly,
orders 1 and 2 partly overlap on the detector, because limited mechanical
clearance restricted the angles of the grism and the prism
\citep{AlbertEtAl2023}. Where the two orders overlap, their light must be
separated before either spectrum can be measured. Secondly, the mode has no
slit to block the light of other stars. The spectra of other stars in the
field of view, called field stars, therefore also fall on the detector, and
some of them land on the traces of the target (Figure~\ref{fig:soss-detector-orders}). Finally, the zodiacal
background, which is sunlight scattered by dust in the Solar System, is not
smooth across the detector. Its level changes abruptly near detector column 700 (Figure~\ref{fig:soss-detector-orders}), so the background model must account for this step
\citep{AlbertEtAl2023,RadicaEtAl2023,HolmbergMadhusudhan2023,LouieEtAl2025}.
Despite these difficulties, SOSS has delivered detailed spectra. For example,
one of the first SOSS analyses recovered the water bands, potassium absorption
and clouds of the hot Jupiter WASP-39~b \citep{FeinsteinEtAl2023}.

In this project, I focus on another hot Jupiter, WASP-17~b. Its low density and low gravity give it an extended atmosphere \citep{AndersonEtAl2010}. HST and Spitzer had already measured
the transmission spectrum of WASP-17~b over $0.3$-$5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, by
combining observations with several instruments \citep{AldersonEtAl2022}.
However, that spectrum could not constrain how much water the atmosphere
contains. Depending on the model, the data were consistent with a water abundance below the solar value, with one above it, or with both
\citep{AldersonEtAl2022,LouieEtAl2025}. \citet{LouieEtAl2025} then analysed
one SOSS transit of WASP-17~b with three independent data reductions. A data
reduction is the chain of software that turns the detector data into a
spectrum. The three reductions were Ahsoka, which Louie et al.\ introduced,
\texttt{transitspectroscopy} \citep{Espinoza2022TransitSpectroscopy} with the
transit-fitting code \texttt{juliet} \citep{EspinozaEtAl2019}, and
supreme-SPOON \citep{RadicaEtAl2023}, now distributed as exoTEDRF
\citep{Radica2024}. The three spectra agreed within their uncertainties over
a large part of the wavelength range. The SOSS spectrum also constrained the
water abundance more tightly than the HST and Spitzer data had, and placed it
above the solar value \citep{LouieEtAl2025}.

Each reduction gave its own uncertainty for the transit depth in every spectral
channel. For spectra binned to a resolving power of 100, the median of these uncertainties ranged from 46 to 73~ppm across
the three reductions and orders 1 and 2 \citep{LouieEtAl2025}. In order 1,
for example, the median was 46~ppm for supreme-SPOON, 55~ppm for Ahsoka and
69~ppm for \texttt{transitspectroscopy}. These numbers are the uncertainties
that each reduction assigned to its own spectrum, not differences between the
three spectra. So the precision assigned to a spectrum depends on the analysis
as well as on the data. WASP-17~b is therefore a good test case for
comparing analysis methods. Its transmission signal is large, earlier HST and
Spitzer spectra exist, and three published reductions of the same SOSS
transit can be compared with each other.

\subsection{How a transmission spectrum is obtained}
\label{sec:detector-to-spectrum}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \input{figures/conventional_reduction_workflow}
  \caption{Chain of steps shared by extraction-first reductions, which extract a spectrum from each image before fitting the transit. (a) Within an integration, the detector is read
  nondestructively several times, and each read (group) samples the
  accumulated charge. A cosmic ray adds a jump, which the several groups make
  visible, so the count rate can be fitted from the unaffected differences
  between reads. (b) After the background has been subtracted and the $1/f$
  noise removed, extraction compresses each count-rate image into a
  one-dimensional spectrum. Binning in wavelength gives one light curve per
  channel, and a transit fit to each gives the depths that make up the
  transmission spectrum.}
  \label{fig:extraction-first-workflow}
\end{figure*}

All three reductions turn the raw detector data into a transmission spectrum
through the same basic chain of steps. The SOSS detector records a transit observation as a long sequence of short
exposures, called integrations. During each integration, the detector is read
several times without clearing the charge collected in the pixels, and each
read is called a group. In the WASP-17~b observation, for example, each
integration contained eight groups. The charge in a pixel therefore rises from
group to group in a ramp, and the slope of the ramp gives the count rate of
the pixel \citep{AlbertEtAl2023}. Figure~\ref{fig:extraction-first-workflow}a
shows such a ramp. Fitting the ramp of every pixel turns each integration into
a two-dimensional image of count rates, so an observation produces a time
series of count-rate images.

Before the ramps are fitted, the groups are corrected for the known behaviour
of the detector, usually with the JWST calibration pipeline
\citep{JWSTPipeline2025} or a variant of it. These corrections flag pixels
that saturate, subtract the electronic offset of each pixel and remove slow drifts in the readings, which are measured with reference pixels that receive no light. They also correct
the nonlinear response of the pixels and subtract the dark current, the charge
that collects without light. A cosmic ray that hits a pixel adds a sudden jump
to the ramp of that pixel. Jumps larger than a chosen threshold are flagged as
cosmic-ray hits, and the count rate is fitted from the unaffected differences
between groups. The readout also adds $1/f$ noise, a noise whose strength is
largest at low frequencies. The $1/f$ noise appears as stripes along the
detector columns. Depending on the pipeline, it is removed from each group
before the ramp fit or from the count-rate images afterwards.

Figure~\ref{fig:extraction-first-workflow}b shows the steps that turn the
count-rate images into a transmission spectrum. The images are corrected for
bad pixels and for differences in sensitivity between pixels, a correction
called the flat field. The background is removed by subtracting a model of the zodiacal background, scaled to match the observed images \citep{LouieEtAl2025}. The light of field
stars is masked or corrected, before or after extraction. Extraction then
combines the pixels across the width of each trace, compressing each
count-rate image into a one-dimensional spectrum
\citep{Horne1986,DarveauBernierEtAl2022}. Each detector column, or a group of columns, gives a light curve. A transit model is fitted to each light curve, and the resulting depths can be binned to form the transmission spectrum \citep{BellEtAl2022,RadicaEtAl2023,LouieEtAl2025}.

Pipelines differ in the order of these steps and in the choices made at each
one \citep{RadicaEtAl2023,HolmbergMadhusudhan2023,LouieEtAl2025}. Examples are
the threshold for flagging cosmic-ray hits, the stage at which the $1/f$ noise
is removed, how the background model is scaled and how field stars are
handled. Others are the trace positions, the width of the strip of pixels
combined in extraction and the treatment of limb darkening. A further choice
is the model of slow instrumental trends in the light curves, the slow changes
in brightness caused by the instrument rather than by the planet. The three
reductions of WASP-17~b differ in several of these choices
\citep{LouieEtAl2025}. Ahsoka and supreme-SPOON removed the $1/f$ noise in each group, temporarily subtracting the background during this step. Background subtraction was then applied to the count-rate images. They scaled the background model
separately on the two sides of the step in the background near column 700.
The \texttt{transitspectroscopy} reduction instead used a single scale factor
for the background and removed the $1/f$ noise from the count-rate images.
Ahsoka and \texttt{transitspectroscopy} did not correct for the light of field
stars and excluded the affected wavelengths, whilst supreme-SPOON corrected
the extracted spectra for several of these stars.

The three reductions also modelled the slow instrumental trends in their light
curves differently \citep{LouieEtAl2025}. Ahsoka, built on the Eureka!
framework \citep{BellEtAl2022}, fitted a linear trend in time alongside the transit model for each light curve and scaled the expected noise by a fitted factor. The
\texttt{transitspectroscopy} reduction used a Mat\'ern-$3/2$ Gaussian process,
a flexible statistical model of noise that is correlated in time. The
supreme-SPOON reduction fitted no trend function, only a term added to the
uncertainties. These models describe trends and noise in the extracted light curves. They do not refit the division of the detector light into starlight and background, which all three reductions had already subtracted from the images.

Whatever their differences, the three reductions follow the same order. Each
subtracts the background from the detector images, extracts a one-dimensional
spectrum from each image, and only then fits the transit. I call analyses that
follow this order extraction-first. The background and the extracted spectra
are therefore both fixed before the transit fit begins, and these two steps
are where errors could enter.

\subsection{Two steps where errors could enter}
\label{sec:structural-limitations}

Each of the two steps could bias the spectrum. For each, I say what existing methods do about it, what is still missing, and what NOVA does differently.

The background could bias the depth because it adds light, whereas the transit dims the starlight. Suppose that some steady background is left in the data after the subtraction. I call this left-over light the
residual background. If the residual background is counted as starlight, the
star appears brighter than it is. The light blocked by the planet is then a
smaller fraction of the total, and the transit comes out too shallow.
Removing too much light as background has the opposite effect and makes the
transit too deep. An error that varies across the detector changes the depth
by different amounts at different wavelengths, and so changes the shape of
the spectrum. In all three reductions of WASP-17~b, the background was
subtracted before the light curves were fitted, and the transit fit held the
subtracted background fixed \citep{LouieEtAl2025}. The transit fit therefore
could not correct an error in the subtraction. The reductions also report one
uncertainty per wavelength channel. A single number per channel cannot
describe how one background error moves neighbouring channels up or down
together.

A suitable extraction can preserve the information in the detector pixels. The extraction needs the profile of the
trace, which describes how the light of each column is spread across the
pixels, as well as the background and the noise of each pixel. The optimal
extraction of \citet{Horne1986} weights each pixel by the expected profile
divided by the noise variance of that pixel. When these inputs are correct, the optimal extraction gives the flux in each column with as little noise as the pixels allow, under its model assumptions. For a known linear detector response and Gaussian noise, \citet{BoltonSchlegel2010} showed that a suitably extracted one-dimensional spectrum keeps the information that the pixels carry about the spectrum, provided it comes with two things \citep{BoltonEtAl2012}. These are a covariance matrix, which records how the errors at different wavelengths are related, and a description of the spectral resolution.

The compression could bias the spectrum when an input is wrong. The error is
then built into the extracted spectrum, and the transit fit that follows
cannot remove it. For SOSS, the hardest case is the part of the detector
where orders 1 and 2 overlap. A simple box extraction sums the pixels within a
fixed width around the trace. Ignoring the overlap in such an extraction
typically biases transit spectra by about 1\% of the strength of their
features \citep{DarveauBernierEtAl2022}.

Two SOSS methods already improve these steps. JExoRES addresses the background. During the extraction, JExoRES fits the level of the residual background together with the fluxes of the overlapping orders \citep{HolmbergMadhusudhan2023}. ATOCA addresses the overlap. It models every pixel as the sum of the light of both orders. Because the two orders are copies of the same stellar spectrum, one spectrum has to explain both, and this lets ATOCA separate them during the extraction \citep{DarveauBernierEtAl2022}. ATOCA needs
the profile of each order across the detector, and the APPLESOSS package can
estimate these profiles from the observation itself \citep{RadicaEtAl2022}.

Modelling the sky on the original detector pixels, before resampling, has a precedent outside SOSS. \citet{Kelson2003} showed that the sky background of a spectrograph can be modelled
on the original detector pixels, before the images are resampled onto a straight grid, which avoids
the errors that resampling introduces into the subtraction. That result concerns sky subtraction in slit spectroscopy. Whether fitting the background and transit together improves SOSS spectra remains to be tested.

What is still missing is a fit in which the transit helps to separate the
starlight from the background. JExoRES and ATOCA, like the three reductions
of WASP-17~b, extract the spectrum first and fit the transit afterwards
\citep{DarveauBernierEtAl2022,RadicaEtAl2023,HolmbergMadhusudhan2023,LouieEtAl2025}.
By the time the transit is fitted, the split between starlight and
background has already been decided. None of these SOSS methods fits the
background and the transit together, in one fit to the detector pixels.

A joint fit could help to separate the starlight from the background,
because only the starlight dims during the transit. In a single pixel, the
dimming alone cannot decide the split. The data fix only the level before
the transit and the size of the dip. Both can be matched by a deeper transit
of less starlight plus more background, or by a shallower transit of more
starlight plus less background. Pixels that record the same wavelength,
however, share one transit depth, whilst their mix of starlight and
background differs from the bright centre of a trace to its faint wings. The
joint fit uses these differences across the image together with the dimming
in time. Whether the fit can separate starlight from background depends on
the model assumed for how the background varies across the detector and in
time. Any non-stellar variation sufficiently similar to the transit in time, such as a slow electronic drift, could also be mistaken for starlight.
Section~\ref{sec:which-light} describes a test on the real WASP-17~b data that tried to decide how the observed light divides between star and background, and could not.

NOVA fits the transit and the background together, directly on the detector
pixels (Section~\ref{sec:nova}). NOVA predicts the count rate of every pixel
it uses, in every integration, from one transmission spectrum shared by
orders 1 and 2, the brightness of the star, the limb darkening and the
residual background. All of these quantities are adjusted together until the prediction matches the
pixels, and no one-dimensional spectrum is extracted for this fit.

NOVA still depends on three steps taken before its spectral fit, and each
could bias the NOVA spectrum. Firstly, the detector processing subtracts a
scaled model of the zodiacal background before the fit, as in the other
pipelines. NOVA then fits only what that subtraction has left behind. If the scaling was wrong, that error is part of what NOVA has to fit, and it could bias the result. Secondly, NOVA, like an extraction, needs the profile of the trace, that is, how the light of each wavelength is spread over the pixels of a column. In NOVA I call this profile the spatial response. NOVA estimates the spatial response from the
observation being analysed, before the spectral fit, by measuring how
strongly each pixel dims during the transit. The estimate therefore also
assumes that only the starlight follows the transit. In an earlier version
of NOVA, an error in the spatial response made the transit too shallow in the faintest columns, at the red end of the spectrum (Section~\ref{sec:nova}). Finally, NOVA takes the
transit geometry, meaning the timing of the transit and the path of the
planet across the star, from a separate white-light fit to the light of each
order summed over wavelength. This white-light fit is the only place where
NOVA extracts light curves.

If any of these three steps goes wrong, NOVA could still fit the pixels well whilst giving a wrong spectrum. A good fit therefore cannot show that NOVA separated the starlight from the background correctly, and the next subsection explains why the same holds for the other methods.

\subsection{Which spectrum is right?}
\label{sec:validation-problem}

For a real planet, the true spectrum is unknown, so no recovered spectrum can
be scored against it. A common check is therefore to compare independent
reductions of the same observation. When the reductions disagree, the
spectrum is shown to depend on the analysis. For example, in the
3-5~$\mu\mathrm{m}$ NIRSpec spectrum of WASP-39~b, differences between
reductions changed the retrieved abundances by up to about an order of
magnitude \citep{ConstantinouEtAl2023}. For the sub-Earth GJ~341~b, none of
the three reductions found evidence of an atmosphere. Yet two of them showed weak candidate absorption features at different wavelengths, pointing to different molecules \citep{KirkEtAl2024}. A disagreement of this kind does not say which spectrum
is right. When the reductions agree, as the three reductions of WASP-17~b do
over most of the bandpass \citep{LouieEtAl2025}, the spectrum is shown to be
reproducible. However, neither outcome establishes absolute accuracy.

Although they differ in many details, the three reductions of WASP-17~b follow the same extraction-first order (Section~\ref{sec:detector-to-spectrum}), and they also share reference files and part of the software \citep{BellEtAl2022,RadicaEtAl2023,LouieEtAl2025}. Their agreement therefore cannot reveal an error common to all of them. Subtracting the background before the transit fit could cause exactly such a shared error. Crucially, a good fit to the extracted light curves does not show that the background was subtracted correctly. Steady background left in a light curve makes the transit shallower (Section~\ref{sec:structural-limitations}), but the light curve still has the shape of a transit, so it can still be fitted well. The same holds for NOVA's fit to the pixels. To measure the error in a recovered transit depth, I therefore
need a test signal whose depth is known.

Known test signals have been supplied in two ways. The first is simulation,
in which the whole observation is computed from a model. ATOCA, introduced above, was tested on simulated SOSS images \citep{DarveauBernierEtAl2022}. When it
was given correct calibration inputs, such as the shape of each trace and the
wavelength of each pixel, the spectrum it extracted matched the spectrum used
to make the images to within 10~ppm. A simulation, however, contains only the
noise and background that its authors put in. \citet{CarterEtAl2025} studied
how the steps that flag cosmic-ray hits and fit the ramps affect SOSS spectra.
They used the raw files of a real WASP-39~b observation, but replaced the reads inside them with simulated reads of a transit, so the noise was simulated as well and no background was included.

The second way adds a known signal to real data, so that the sky, the other
stars and the noise are those of an actual observation.
\citet{ChristiansenEtAl2013} added modelled transits to the calibrated pixels
of real stars observed by Kepler. They then compared the signal-to-noise ratio
that the Kepler pipeline recovered for each injected transit with the expected
value. \citet{GirmeniaMetchev2026} added model images of moving Solar System
objects to the raw reads of real JWST NIRCam images, before any calibration,
to test how well their method detects such objects. Each study tested one
pipeline, and neither recovered a spectrum. In both, the injected light came
from a model of how a source appears on the detector. The injected light can also be measured rather than modelled, for example the light of a laboratory lamp. Before launch, NIRSpec was tested in the laboratory with
a lamp in place of a star. \citet{RustamkulovEtAl2022} added transits to a
time series of these lamp-lit images and recovered the injected spectra with
one analysis. The lamp light and the detector noise were real, but there was
no sky. The transits were also added after the ramps had been fitted, so the
steps that turn the reads into count rates never saw them. Measured light has also been injected into real observations in a different field, the direct imaging of exoplanets. There, a planet appears as a faint point of light beside its much brighter star, and the analysis must remove the light of the star to reveal it. To test whether an analysis
recovers a planet, a fake planet is added to the real images. The fake planet is a faint, scaled copy
of the star's own image \citep{LafreniereEtAl2007}. The injected light is then a measured image of the star, as in the benchmark described below, but the signal is a point of light and not a transit.

Data challenges go one step further and score the methods of several teams
against the same known signal. The Spitzer data challenge compared seven
methods of analysing eclipse observations of the planet XO-3~b
\citep{IngallsEtAl2016}. The accuracy of the methods was measured on ten simulated eclipse observations,
whose eclipse depth the organisers had set. Ten real eclipses were analysed as
well, but without a known depth they could only show whether each method gave
repeatable results. In direct imaging, the Exoplanet Imaging Data Challenge
added fake planets to eight real data sets from two instruments
\citep{CantalloubeEtAl2024}. The participants estimated the positions and
spectra of these planets, and their estimates were scored against the
injected values.

A transit dims only the starlight. To inject one into real SOSS data, I must therefore know which part of the recorded light belongs to the star, so I need an image of the star on the detector. My first injectors, the tools that add the transit to the data, took this image from the WASP-17~b data themselves. These data, however, cannot
show how much of the faint light in the wings of the traces comes from the
star (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}). The commissioning study of SOSS met a
related limit. It could not determine how much of a star's light falls
outside the extraction aperture, the band of pixels that is combined into the
spectrum. Neither the on-orbit commissioning data nor WebbPSF, the software
that models how JWST images a star, could provide this fraction
\citep{AlbertEtAl2023}. Calibration programme 4476 was designed to measure
this light \citep{VolkEspinoza2023}. In a SOSS time series, only a strip of the detector is read out. Programme 4476 observed a star at its usual position on this strip, and then again with the star moved off it. On this strip, the difference between the two exposures
measures the light of the star. This greatly reduces the need to split the
observed light into starlight and background with a fit.

None of the studies above combines three things. The first is an image of the
star estimated from exposures of that same star, as in programme 4476, and
then cleaned for injection. The second is a known transit added to the raw
reads of a real SOSS exposure, so that each pipeline runs its own detector
steps on it. The third is recovery by several complete pipelines, each of which learns the injected truth only after it has delivered its final result. The benchmark that I
am building from programme 4476 is designed to combine all three
(Section~\ref{sec:measured-benchmark}). From the difference of the two
exposures, I estimate the image of the star, remove the field sources from it
and smooth its faint outer wings. I will then dim this image with a known
transit and add it to the raw reads of the moved exposure. Because I will apply the transit to this constructed image myself, the injected signal will be known exactly for that image. The benchmark is designed to show whether the established pipelines
recover a known transit without a detectable bias, and whether NOVA improves
on them.

\subsection{Research question and objectives}
\label{sec:research-question}

The central research question of this project is:
\begin{quote}
\raggedright\itshape
How accurately can a transmission spectrum be recovered from the detector
images of a JWST NIRISS/SOSS transit observation, and does fitting the
transit and the background together, directly on the pixels, reduce the
biases that subtracting the background first and extracting a spectrum first
can cause?
\end{quote}

To make this question testable, I divide it into four objectives.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.,leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item Develop NOVA, a method that fits the transmission spectrum and the
  background together, directly to the detector pixels of NIRISS/SOSS
  observations.
  \item Develop a benchmark that adds a known transit to the detector reads of
  real NIRISS/SOSS exposures and is built independently of the methods it
  tests.
  \item Use the benchmark to compare the accuracy of NOVA with that of the
  established extraction-based pipelines, for several injected transmission
  spectra.
  \item Identify which assumptions limit the accuracy of the recovered
  spectrum, and determine how the resulting errors carry over into the
  atmospheric properties that a retrieval infers. Some of these assumptions
  concern the instrument, for example the corrections applied to the detector
  reads. Others concern the analysis, for example how the starlight and the
  background are modelled.
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:nova} describes NOVA as it is currently implemented and ends
with a first, preliminary spectrum of WASP-17~b. Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}
sets out what a benchmark needs and what I learned from my first attempts to
build one from the WASP-17~b data. Section~\ref{sec:measured-benchmark}
describes the benchmark that I am now building from calibration programme
4476, and the tests it must pass before any pipeline is scored.
Section~\ref{sec:research-plan} discusses what the first year has shown and
sets out the plan for the rest of the project.
