\section{Introduction and Literature Review}
\label{sec:introduction}

The transmission spectrum of an exoplanet is the depth of its transit, the
fraction of starlight that the planet blocks, at each wavelength. It is
obtained from a time series of detector images through a chain of processing
steps. Errors could enter at many points in this chain, and I focus on two of them. The first is the subtraction of the background light. Any background that remains is not modelled when the transit is fitted. An error in that subtraction can bias the fitted transit depth, even when the transit model fits the data well. The second is the compression of each image into a one-dimensional spectrum. This compression can lose information that the later steps cannot recover.

I have therefore developed a method called Nonlinear Order-coupled Variable-projection Analysis (NOVA). NOVA does not
compress the images into spectra. It fits the transit to the detector pixels
themselves, and the same fit includes whatever background the earlier
subtraction has left behind. Real data cannot decide whether NOVA or the established methods give the more accurate spectrum, because the true spectrum of a real planet is unknown. I am therefore building a benchmark in
which a known transit is added to the raw data of a real observation from the
James Webb Space Telescope (JWST). The error of each method, NOVA included, can then be measured against a known truth.

\subsection{Exoplanet atmospheres and transmission spectroscopy}
\label{sec:atmospheres-transmission}

Before the atmosphere of a planet can be studied, the planet has to be found.
The first exoplanets were detected around a pulsar in 1992
\citep{WolszczanFrail1992}, and the first planet around a Sun-like star
followed in 1995 \citep{MayorQueloz1995}. Since then, transit surveys such as
Kepler and the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS)
\citep{BoruckiEtAl2010,RickerEtAl2015} have helped to raise the number of
confirmed exoplanets to more than 6,000 \citep{NASAExoplanetArchive2026}.
Many of these planets orbit bright nearby stars, the most favourable targets
for follow-up observations. A detection alone, however, reveals little about
what a planet is like. It gives the orbit of the planet and its radius or its
mass, depending on the method. Measuring the properties of a
known planet and its atmosphere is called characterisation.

The planets found so far span a wide range of sizes, temperatures and orbits, and many have no
counterpart in the Solar System \citep{WinnFabrycky2015}. Their mass and
radius give their mean density, but planets with similar masses and radii can
be made of different mixtures of rock, ice and gas \citep{RogersSeager2010}.
The atmosphere helps to distinguish between these possibilities, and its composition can also hint at where and how the planet formed \citep{Madhusudhan2019}.

The atmosphere can be studied when the planet transits, that is, passes in front of its star, because part of the starlight then passes through the atmosphere. Measuring the transit at many wavelengths is called
transmission spectroscopy. The stellar disc dims towards its edge, an effect called limb darkening. If the disc were instead uniformly bright, the depth of a transit would be approximately
\begin{equation}
  \delta(\lambda) \simeq
  \left[\frac{R_{\mathrm p}(\lambda)}{R_\star}\right]^2 ,
  \label{eq:transit-depth}
\end{equation}
where $\delta(\lambda)$ is the fractional drop in stellar flux during transit,
$R_{\mathrm p}(\lambda)$ the apparent radius of the planet at wavelength
$\lambda$ and $R_\star$ the radius of the star. The apparent radius is larger
at wavelengths where the atmosphere absorbs more strongly, because the
atmosphere then becomes opaque at a higher altitude. Absorption and scattering by atoms, molecules, clouds and aerosols therefore make the transit depth vary with
wavelength \citep{SeagerSasselov2000,Brown2001}, as
Figure~\ref{fig:transmission-spectroscopy} illustrates.

  opaque body of the planet blocks starlight, whilst a small fraction of the
  starlight passes through its atmospheric annulus. The altitude at which
  the atmosphere becomes opaque, and hence the apparent radius of the

  \label{fig:transmission-spectroscopy}

The atmospheric signal is the part of the transit depth that changes with wavelength. Only a thin ring of atmosphere around the planet filters the starlight, so this signal is a small fraction of the stellar flux. The signal is
largest for planets that are large relative to their star and whose
atmospheres are hot, rich in hydrogen and held by weak gravity, because such
atmospheres extend further above the planet
\citep{SeagerSasselov2000,Brown2001}. A class of planets that satisfies these
conditions is the so-called hot Jupiters, gas giants that orbit close to their
stars \citep{Brown2001,Madhusudhan2019}. It is no coincidence that the first
exoplanet atmosphere to be detected, through the sodium absorption of
HD~209458~b \citep{CharbonneauEtAl2002}, belonged to a hot Jupiter. I therefore develop my methods on hot Jupiters, whose atmospheric signals are easier to measure, before applying them to smaller and cooler planets.

Transmission spectroscopy is one of three ways to observe an atmosphere as the
planet moves along its orbit. In a secondary eclipse, the planet passes behind
the star, and the drop in brightness isolates the light emitted or reflected by the dayside of the planet \citep{DemingEtAl2005,CoulombeEtAl2023,GressierEtAl2024}. A phase
curve follows the brightness of the planet around its orbit. It constrains the
temperature contrast between the day and night sides, how heat is carried
around the planet and how the atmosphere varies with longitude
\citep{KnutsonEtAl2007,MikalEvansEtAl2023,KemptonEtAl2023,SplinterEtAl2025}.
I start with transmission spectroscopy, whose transits have a well-defined geometry that repeats with every orbit, and for which three independent reductions of the same observation of WASP-17~b can be compared (Section~\ref{sec:jwst-soss-wasp17}). This makes a transit a convenient way to test how the processing of the data affects the recovered spectrum. Secondary
eclipses are a possible later application of my methods
(Section~\ref{sec:plan}).

Whichever technique is used, the properties of the atmosphere are then
inferred from the measured spectrum by a statistical fit called a retrieval
\citep{BarstowHeng2020}. Inferring the atmosphere from its spectrum is an inverse problem that does not always have a unique answer. Different combinations of abundances, temperature, clouds, hazes and uneven brightness of the stellar surface can produce similar spectra \citep{RackhamEtAl2018,Madhusudhan2019}. The retrieval also takes the measured
spectrum as given, so a bias in the spectrum can propagate into the retrieved atmosphere, however precise the retrieval. This project therefore mainly
concerns the step before the retrieval, namely how accurately the spectrum
itself is recovered from the detector data. Objective~4
(Section~\ref{sec:research-question}) also asks how errors in the spectrum
carry over into the retrieved atmosphere. To test how accurately the spectrum is recovered, I first need an instrument that can measure small changes in transit depth, and a planet whose
signal is strong. 

\subsection{JWST, NIRISS/SOSS and WASP-17~b}
\label{sec:jwst-soss-wasp17}

The first characterisations of exoplanet atmospheres were made with the Hubble
Space Telescope (HST) and the Spitzer Space Telescope, several years before
Kepler was launched in 2009 \citep{BoruckiEtAl2010}. In 2002, HST found
sodium in the atmosphere of HD~209458~b \citep{CharbonneauEtAl2002}. In 2005, Spitzer detected the infrared light emitted by two planets
\citep{DemingEtAl2005,CharbonneauEtAl2005}. The two telescopes went on to
establish the techniques for studying exoplanet atmospheres
\citep{KnutsonEtAl2007,SingEtAl2016}. For transmission spectroscopy, however,
each HST instrument recorded a spectrum over only a narrow range of
wavelengths, such as 1.1 to $1.7\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ \citep{SingEtAl2016}. A broad
spectrum, such as one over 0.3 to $5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, therefore had to be
combined from observations made at different times with several instruments on HST and Spitzer \citep{SingEtAl2016,AldersonEtAl2022}.

JWST was launched in December 2021 \citep{RigbyEtAl2023}. Like HST, it is used in exoplanet science mainly to characterise planets that are already known. For transmission spectroscopy,
JWST has two main advantages over HST. Firstly, it collects more light. Its
primary mirror is about 6.6~m across \citep{GardnerEtAl2006,RigbyEtAl2023},
compared with 2.4~m for HST \citep{NASAHubbleDesign2026}. With more light, the
brightness of the star is measured with less noise, so smaller changes in
transit depth can be detected. Secondly, a single instrument mode of JWST can
cover a wide range of wavelengths. For example, the Near Infrared Imager and
Slitless Spectrograph (NIRISS), one of the four science instruments of JWST,
has a mode that covers 0.6 to $2.8\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ in one observation
\citep{AlbertEtAl2023}. This range contains several water bands, the two
potassium lines and regions sensitive to other absorbers and to clouds and
hazes \citep{AlbertEtAl2023,HolmbergMadhusudhan2023,LouieEtAl2025}. A spectrum covering several absorption bands can therefore come from one transit observed with one instrument,
and it no longer has to be combined from observations made at different
times.

In this project, I use a mode of NIRISS, called Single Object Slitless
Spectroscopy (SOSS) \citep{AlbertEtAl2023}.
Figure~\ref{fig:soss-detector-orders} shows how the light of a star falls on
the SOSS detector. An optical element called GR700XD spreads the light into several copies of the spectrum, called orders \citep{AlbertEtAl2023}. It combines a grism, a prism carrying a diffraction grating, with a second prism. Orders 1 and 2 together give the
range of 0.6 to $2.8\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ mentioned above. The resolving power
$\lambda/\Delta\lambda$ of the first order is about 650 at
$1.25\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ \citep{AlbertEtAl2023}, where $\Delta\lambda$ is the
smallest difference in wavelength that SOSS can separate. On the detector,
each order forms a curved stripe of light, called its trace. A weak
cylindrical lens spreads the light over about 23 detector rows. Bright stars can then be observed without saturating the pixels, and small pointing jitter and errors in the flat field, the map of each pixel's sensitivity, matter less \citep{AlbertEtAl2023,JDoxNIRISSSOSS2026}.

SOSS measures the total light of a star very precisely. This precision was
measured during commissioning, the test period before science operations, on
the A-type standard star BD$+$60$^{\circ}$1753 \citep{AlbertEtAl2023}. The
light of all wavelengths was summed into a white-light curve, which gives the
brightness of the star as a function of time. A slow trend was subtracted
from this curve, and the remaining points were averaged in 40-minute bins.
The binned points then scattered by only about 20 parts per million (ppm)
\citep{AlbertEtAl2023}. Unlike the white-light curve, the spectrum
is measured in channels, each a narrow range of wavelengths. A channel
receives far fewer photons than the white-light curve, so the transit depth in
each channel is less precise than the white-light depth.

Although SOSS measures light precisely, its data are difficult to
analyse for three reasons. Firstly, orders 1 and 2 partly overlap on the
detector, because limited mechanical clearance restricted the angles of the
grism and the prism \citep{AlbertEtAl2023}. Where the two orders overlap,
their light must be separated before either spectrum can be measured.
Secondly, the mode has no slit to block the light of other stars. Other stars in the field of view, called field stars, therefore also form spectra on the detector, and some of these spectra land on the traces of the target (Figure~\ref{fig:soss-detector-orders}). SOSS is slitless out of necessity. A slit must sit where the optics form an image of the sky before the light is dispersed, and when SOSS was developed, NIRISS had no optics that form such an image \citep{AlbertEtAl2023}. Being slitless also helps transit observations, because a narrow slit would clip a varying fraction of the starlight as the pointing jitters. For the same reason, the Near Infrared Spectrograph (NIRSpec) of JWST observes transits through a wide aperture \citep{JDoxNIRSpecBOTS2026}.

Finally, the zodiacal background, which is sunlight scattered by dust in the
Solar System, is not smooth across the detector. As Figure~\ref{fig:soss-detector-orders} shows, its level changes abruptly near detector column 700 \citep{AlbertEtAl2023,LouieEtAl2025}, at about $2.15\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ in order 1 \citep{AlbertEtAl2023}.
\citet{AlbertEtAl2023} traced this step to the pick-off mirror, the mirror
that directs the light from the telescope into NIRISS. Because there is no
slit, zodiacal light from the whole patch of sky seen by this mirror reaches
GR700XD, which spreads it into orders in the same way as the light of the
star. The step lies where the zeroth order of the background, the light that
GR700XD leaves almost undispersed, is cut off by the edge of the pick-off
mirror. To the left of the step, the background is therefore almost entirely
first-order light. To the right, zeroth-order and first-order light add, and
the background is about twice as bright \citep{AlbertEtAl2023}.

Because of this structure, subtracting one constant level everywhere would leave a background that differs from column to column, and would bias the transit depths by an amount that changes with wavelength \citep{RadicaEtAl2023}. Pipelines therefore subtract a model of the background, scaled to the observed level. The brightness of the background, however, changes from one pointing to another, and the two sides of the step do not change by the same factor, so the model should be scaled separately on each side \citep{AlbertEtAl2023,LouieEtAl2025}. 

Despite these difficulties, SOSS has delivered detailed spectra. For example,
one of the first SOSS analyses recovered the water bands, potassium absorption
and clouds of the Saturn-mass gas giant WASP-39~b \citep{FeinsteinEtAl2023}.

I focus on the hot Jupiter WASP-17~b. Its low density and low gravity give it
an extended atmosphere \citep{AndersonEtAl2010}. HST and Spitzer had already
measured its transmission spectrum over 0.3 to $5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, by combining
observations made with several instruments between 2012 and 2017
\citep{AldersonEtAl2022}. However, that spectrum did not settle how much water the atmosphere contains \citep{LouieEtAl2025}. Depending on the
atmospheric model assumed in the retrieval, the HST and Spitzer data were
consistent with a water abundance below the solar value, with one above it, or
with two separate solutions, one below and one above
\citep{AldersonEtAl2022,LouieEtAl2025}.

\citet{LouieEtAl2025} then analysed one SOSS visit of WASP-17~b, that is, one observation of one transit, with three independent data reductions. A data reduction is the processing that turns the detector data into a spectrum, and the software that performs it is called a pipeline. One reduction used Ahsoka, which Louie et al.\ introduced. Another used \texttt{transitspectroscopy} \citep{Espinoza2022TransitSpectroscopy} with the transit-fitting code \texttt{juliet} \citep{EspinozaEtAl2019}. The third used supreme-SPOON \citep{RadicaEtAl2023}, now distributed as exoTEDRF \citep{Radica2024}. The
three spectra agreed within their uncertainties over a large part of the
wavelength range. The SOSS data also constrained the water abundance more tightly than the HST and Spitzer data, and placed it above the solar value \citep{LouieEtAl2025}.

Each reduction gave its own uncertainty for the transit depth in every
spectral channel. For spectra binned to a resolving power of 100, the median
of these uncertainties ranged from 46 to 73~ppm across the three reductions
and orders 1 and 2 \citep{LouieEtAl2025}. In order 1, for example, the median
was 46~ppm for supreme-SPOON, 55~ppm for Ahsoka and 69~ppm for
\texttt{transitspectroscopy}. These differences show that the precision
assigned to a spectrum depends on the analysis as well as on the data.
WASP-17~b is therefore a good test case for comparing analysis methods. Its
transmission signal is large, earlier HST and Spitzer spectra exist, and three
published reductions of the same SOSS transit can be compared with each other.

\subsection{How a transmission spectrum is obtained}
\label{sec:detector-to-spectrum}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \input{figures/conventional_reduction_workflow}
  \caption{Chain of steps shared by extraction-first reductions (schematic), which extract
  a spectrum from each image before fitting the transit. (a) Within an
  integration, the detector is read nondestructively several times, and each
  read (group) samples the accumulated charge. Because there are several groups, a cosmic ray shows up as a jump, and the count rate can be fitted from the unaffected differences between reads. (b) After the background has been
  subtracted and the $1/f$ noise removed, extraction compresses each
  count-rate image into a one-dimensional spectrum. Each spectral channel, a
  single detector column in the three reductions of WASP-17~b, gives one light
  curve. A transit fit to each light curve gives one depth per channel, and
  these depths, binned in wavelength, make up the transmission spectrum.}
  \label{fig:extraction-first-workflow}
\end{figure*}

All three reductions of WASP-17~b turned the raw detector data into a
transmission spectrum through the same basic chain of steps
\citep{LouieEtAl2025}. The SOSS detector records a transit observation as a
long sequence of short exposures, called integrations. During each
integration, the detector is read several times without clearing the charge
collected in the pixels, and each read is called a group. In the WASP-17~b
observation, for example, each integration contained eight groups. The charge
in a pixel therefore rises from group to group in a ramp, and the slope of the
ramp gives the count rate of the pixel \citep{AlbertEtAl2023}.
Figure~\ref{fig:extraction-first-workflow}a shows such a ramp. Fitting the
ramp of every pixel turns each integration into a two-dimensional image of
count rates, so an observation produces a time series of count-rate images.

Before the ramps are fitted, the groups are corrected for the known behaviour
of the detector, usually with the JWST calibration pipeline
\citep{JWSTPipeline2025} or a variant of it. These corrections flag pixels
that saturate and subtract the electronic offset of each pixel. They also remove slow drifts in the readings. These drifts are measured with reference pixels, which receive no light. Further corrections account for the nonlinear response
of the pixels and subtract the dark current, the charge that collects without
light. A cosmic ray that hits a pixel adds a sudden jump to the ramp of that
pixel. Jumps larger than a chosen threshold are flagged as cosmic-ray hits,
and the count rate is fitted from the unaffected differences between groups.

The readout also adds correlated noise called $1/f$ noise. The detector
reads its pixels one at a time, at about $10\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ per pixel, and measures
each pixel against a reference voltage \citep{AlbertEtAl2023}. This reference
voltage fluctuates in time, and the fluctuations are strongest at low
frequencies $f$, with a power proportional to $1/f$. Most of the fluctuation
is therefore slow, so pixels read one after another receive almost the same
offset. In SOSS, the pixels of each detector column are read one after
another, so the offsets appear as stripes along the columns
\citep{AlbertEtAl2023,RadicaEtAl2023}. In each read, a stripe adds a few to a
few tens of electrons to the pixels of its column. For comparison, the read
noise of a single read is about 17 electrons \citep{AlbertEtAl2023}. The
stripes change with time and across the detector, so a calibration image such
as a dark cannot remove them. The reference pixels also do not sample them
often enough to correct them \citep{AlbertEtAl2023,RadicaEtAl2023}.

The pipelines therefore estimate the stripes from the science images
themselves, either in each group before the ramp fit or in the count-rate
images afterwards \citep{LouieEtAl2025}. Because the stripes are faint, a
median image of the star, made from many integrations, is first subtracted to
remove most of the starlight. The traces and the brighter field stars are
masked. The level of the stripe in each column is then estimated from the
remaining pixels and subtracted from that column
\citep{AlbertEtAl2023,RadicaEtAl2023,LouieEtAl2025}. This estimate is correct
only if the remaining pixels contain no starlight after the median image has
been subtracted. However, the faint wings of the SOSS traces cover almost the
whole detector, so these pixels also receive some starlight. Any starlight
that the subtraction leaves in them is mistaken for $1/f$ noise and removed
with it. The transit depth is then biased by an amount that varies with
wavelength \citep{RadicaEtAl2023}. Light from field stars that are not masked
biases the estimate in a similar way.

Figure~\ref{fig:extraction-first-workflow}b shows the steps that turn the
count-rate images into a transmission spectrum. In Ahsoka and supreme-SPOON, the count-rate images were first flat-field corrected with the JWST pipeline \citep{RadicaEtAl2023,LouieEtAl2025}. \citet{LouieEtAl2025} do not mention a flat-field correction for \texttt{transitspectroscopy}. The
background is removed by subtracting a model of the zodiacal background,
scaled to match the observed images \citep{LouieEtAl2025}. Bad pixels are
then flagged and replaced \citep{LouieEtAl2025}. The light of
field stars is masked or corrected, before or after extraction.

Extraction then compresses each count-rate image into a one-dimensional
spectrum by combining the pixels across the width of each trace. Ahsoka and
\texttt{transitspectroscopy} used a box extraction, which sums the pixels in a
band of fixed width centred on the trace. The band was 35 or 36 pixels wide in
Ahsoka and 30 pixels wide in \texttt{transitspectroscopy}
\citep{LouieEtAl2025}. The supreme-SPOON reduction instead used ATOCA (an
Algorithm to Treat Order Contamination), an extraction method that models the
overlap of orders 1 and 2 \citep{DarveauBernierEtAl2022,LouieEtAl2025}. All
three reductions of WASP-17~b then treated each detector column as one
spectral channel. The extracted flux in a column, followed over all
integrations, gives the light curve of that channel. Ahsoka, for example,
fitted 2,021 light curves in order 1 and 705 in order 2. Fitting a transit
model to each light curve gave one transit depth per column. Only after these
fits were the depths of neighbouring columns binned to a resolving power of
100, to form the published spectra \citep{LouieEtAl2025}.

Pipelines differ in the order of these steps and in the choices made at each
one \citep{RadicaEtAl2023,HolmbergMadhusudhan2023,LouieEtAl2025}. Examples are
the threshold for flagging cosmic-ray hits, the stage at which the $1/f$ noise
is removed, how the background model is scaled and how field stars are
handled. Other choices are the trace positions, the width of the band of
pixels combined in extraction and the treatment of limb darkening. A further
choice is the model of slow instrumental trends in the light curves, meaning
changes in brightness caused by the instrument rather than by the planet. The
three reductions of WASP-17~b differ in several of these choices
\citep{LouieEtAl2025}. Ahsoka and supreme-SPOON removed the $1/f$ noise from
each group. To estimate the stripes, they subtracted the background model from each group and added it back once the stripes had been removed. The background itself was subtracted later, from the count-rate images. Both reductions scaled the background model separately on the two sides of the
step in the background near column 700. The \texttt{transitspectroscopy}
reduction instead scaled the whole background model by a single factor, and removed
the $1/f$ noise from the count-rate images. Ahsoka and
\texttt{transitspectroscopy} did not correct for the light of field stars and
excluded the affected wavelengths, whilst supreme-SPOON corrected the
extracted spectra for several of these stars.

The three reductions also modelled the slow instrumental trends in their light
curves differently \citep{LouieEtAl2025}. Ahsoka used Eureka! \citep{BellEtAl2022} to fit each light curve with the transit model and a linear trend in time, and scaled the expected noise by a fitted factor. The \texttt{transitspectroscopy} reduction used a
Mat\'ern-$3/2$ Gaussian process, a flexible statistical model of noise that is
correlated in time. The supreme-SPOON reduction fitted no trend, only an extra
term added to the uncertainties. These models describe trends and noise in
the extracted light curves. They do not revisit how the light on the detector
was divided into starlight and background, because all three reductions had
already subtracted the background from the images.

Whatever their differences, the three reductions follow the same order. Each
subtracts the background from the detector images, extracts a one-dimensional
spectrum from each image, and only then fits the transit. I call analyses that
follow this order extraction-first. The subtracted background and the extracted spectra are therefore both fixed before the transit fit begins. The treatment of the background and the extraction are the two steps on which this project focuses.

\subsection{Two steps where errors could enter}
\label{sec:structural-limitations}

The first step is the treatment of the background. Any background that remains after the subtraction is not modelled in the transit fit, so it could bias the transit depth. The background adds light to the image, whereas the
transit dims only the starlight. Suppose that some steady residual background
remains in the data. If this background is
counted as starlight, the star appears brighter than it is. The light blocked
by the planet is then a smaller fraction of the total, and the transit comes
out too shallow. Removing too much light as background has the opposite
effect and makes the transit too deep. An error that varies across the
detector changes the depth by different amounts at different wavelengths, and
so changes the shape of the spectrum. In all three reductions of WASP-17~b, the transit fit held the subtracted background fixed \citep{LouieEtAl2025}, so it could not correct an error in the subtraction. The
reductions also report one uncertainty per wavelength channel. A single
number per channel cannot describe how one background error moves
neighbouring channels up or down together.

The second step, compressing each image into a one-dimensional spectrum, can
lose information that the transit fit cannot recover. However, a suitable
extraction can preserve the information in the detector pixels, provided that
its inputs are correct. The extraction needs the profile of the trace, which
describes how the light of each column is spread across the pixels. The extraction also needs the level of any background left under the trace, and the noise of each pixel. The optimal
extraction of \citet{Horne1986} weights each pixel by the expected profile
divided by the noise variance of that pixel. Under its model assumptions and
with correct inputs, Horne's extraction gives the flux in each column with as
little noise as the pixels allow. For a known linear detector response and
Gaussian noise, \citet{BoltonSchlegel2010} showed that a suitably extracted
one-dimensional spectrum keeps the information that the pixels carry about
the spectrum. To keep this information, the extracted spectrum must come with
a covariance matrix and a description of the spectral resolution
\citep{BoltonSchlegel2010,BoltonEtAl2012}. The covariance matrix records how
the errors at different wavelengths are related.

If an input to the extraction is wrong, the error is built into the extracted
spectrum, and the transit fit that follows cannot remove it. In SOSS,
extraction is hardest where orders 1 and 2 overlap, because the light of the
two orders must be separated there. Ignoring the overlap in a box extraction
typically biases transit spectra by about 1\% of the strength of their
features \citep{DarveauBernierEtAl2022}.

Two SOSS methods already improve these steps, each in a different way. The
first is the JExoRES pipeline \citep{HolmbergMadhusudhan2023}, which
addresses the background. During the extraction, JExoRES fits the level of
the residual background together with the fluxes of the overlapping orders.
However, the extraction still comes before the transit fit.

The second method, ATOCA, addresses the overlap of the orders. It models
every pixel as the sum of the light of both orders. Because the two orders
are copies of the same stellar spectrum, one spectrum has to explain both.
ATOCA can therefore separate the two orders during the extraction
\citep{DarveauBernierEtAl2022}. It needs the profile of each order across
the detector, and the APPLESOSS package can estimate these profiles from the
observation itself \citep{RadicaEtAl2022}. Like JExoRES, however, ATOCA still
extracts the spectrum before the transit is fitted. In the supreme-SPOON
reduction of WASP-17~b, for example, the background was subtracted before the
ATOCA extraction, and the transit was fitted to the extracted light curves
afterwards \citep{LouieEtAl2025}.

Neither JExoRES nor ATOCA, nor any of the three reductions of WASP-17~b, fits
the background and the transit together in one fit to the detector pixels \citep{DarveauBernierEtAl2022,RadicaEtAl2023,HolmbergMadhusudhan2023,LouieEtAl2025}. In all of them, the transit therefore cannot help to separate the starlight from the background.
Modelling the background on the original detector pixels has a precedent
outside SOSS. \citet{Kelson2003} showed that the sky background of a
spectrograph can be modelled on the original pixels, before the images are
resampled onto a straight grid. Working on the original pixels avoids the
errors that resampling introduces into the sky subtraction. Kelson's result
concerns sky subtraction in slit spectroscopy, so it does not show whether
the same approach improves SOSS transit spectra.

A transit observation offers a further way to separate the starlight from the
background, because only the starlight dims during the transit. In a single
pixel, the dimming alone cannot decide the split, because the data fix only
the level before the transit and the size of the dip. The same two numbers
can be matched by a deeper transit of less starlight plus more background, or
by a shallower transit of more starlight plus less background. Pixels that
record the same wavelength, however, share one transit depth, whilst their
mix of starlight and background differs from the bright centre of a trace to
its faint wings. A joint fit of the background and the transit can use these
differences across the image together with the dimming in time. Whether such
a fit can separate the starlight from the background depends on the model
assumed for how the background varies across the detector and in time. Any
non-stellar variation sufficiently similar to the transit in time, such as a
slow electronic drift, could also be mistaken for starlight. 

NOVA makes such a joint fit of the transit and the background, directly on
the detector pixels (Section~\ref{sec:nova}). In every integration, NOVA
predicts the count rate of every pixel that it uses. The prediction depends
on one transmission spectrum shared by orders 1 and 2, on the brightness of
the star, on the limb darkening and on the residual background. All of these
quantities are adjusted together until the prediction matches the pixels. No
one-dimensional spectrum is extracted for this fit.

Three of the steps taken before NOVA's spectral fit could each bias the NOVA spectrum. Firstly, the detector processing subtracts a
scaled model of the zodiacal background before the fit, as in the other
pipelines. NOVA then fits only the residual background that the subtraction
leaves behind. If the model is scaled wrongly, the scaling error becomes part
of the residual background that NOVA must fit, and could bias the spectrum. Secondly, NOVA needs a spatial profile, which describes how the
starlight of each order in a column is spread over the pixels of that column.
The spatial profile plays the same role as the profile of the trace in an
extraction. NOVA estimates the spatial profile from the observation being analysed,
before the spectral fit, by measuring how strongly each pixel dims during the
transit. The estimate therefore also assumes that only the starlight follows
the transit. Finally, NOVA takes the transit geometry, meaning the timing of
the transit and the path of the planet across the star, from a separate
white-light fit. The white-light fit uses the light of each order summed over
wavelength, and it is the only step in which NOVA fits a transit to extracted light curves. The spectral fit holds this geometry fixed, so an error in it would change the shape of the transit model at every wavelength and could bias the whole spectrum.

If any of these three steps goes wrong, NOVA could still fit the pixels well
whilst giving a wrong spectrum. A good fit therefore cannot show that NOVA
separated the starlight from the background correctly. 

\subsection{Which spectrum is right?}
\label{sec:validation-problem}

For a real planet, the true spectrum is unknown, so no recovered spectrum can
be scored against it. A common check is therefore to compare independent
reductions of the same observation. When the reductions disagree, the
spectrum is shown to depend on the analysis. For example, NIRSpec measured the 3 to $5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ spectrum of
WASP-39~b. Differences between reductions of these data changed the
retrieved abundances by up to about an order of magnitude
\citep{ConstantinouEtAl2023}.

Small differences between reductions can also change what is concluded about
a planet. For the sub-Earth GJ~341~b, \citet{KirkEtAl2024} reduced the same data from the Near Infrared Camera (NIRCam) of JWST with three
independent pipelines. None found evidence of an atmosphere. Two, however, showed weak candidate absorption features at different wavelengths, which retrievals assigned to different gases. The authors concluded that these features were not real astrophysical signals. A
disagreement of this kind shows that a result depends on the reduction. It
does not show which reduction, if any, recovered the true spectrum.

When the reductions agree, as the three reductions of WASP-17~b do over a
large part of the wavelength range \citep{LouieEtAl2025}, the spectrum is
shown to be reproducible. However, neither outcome establishes absolute
accuracy. Although they differ in many details, the three reductions of WASP-17~b follow the same extraction-first order
(Section~\ref{sec:detector-to-spectrum}). They also
share part of their software. All three used steps of the JWST calibration
pipeline for the detector corrections
\citep{JWSTPipeline2025,RadicaEtAl2023,LouieEtAl2025}. Ahsoka and supreme-SPOON ran these steps themselves, whilst \texttt{transitspectroscopy} started from count-rate files that
the pipeline had already produced for the JWST archive. All three subtracted
the same model of the zodiacal background, provided by the Space Telescope
Science Institute (STScI), although each reduction scaled the model in its own
way. Ahsoka also used the $1/f$ removal and the bad-pixel step of
supreme-SPOON, and a slightly modified version of the supreme-SPOON background
subtraction. The extraction, in contrast, was done with different code in
each reduction. For the transit fits, Ahsoka used Eureka!, whilst
\texttt{transitspectroscopy} and supreme-SPOON both used \texttt{juliet}
\citep{LouieEtAl2025}. Because the three reductions share their order of steps and some of their software, they could also share an error, and their agreement could not reveal it. Leaving any remaining background out of the transit fit could cause such a shared error.

Crucially, a good fit to the extracted light curves does not show that the
background was subtracted correctly. Steady background left in a light curve
makes the transit shallower (Section~\ref{sec:structural-limitations}). The
light curve, however, keeps the shape of a transit, so it can still be
fitted well. The same holds for NOVA's fit to the pixels. To measure the error
in a recovered transit depth, I therefore need a test signal whose depth is
known.

Earlier studies have supplied known test signals in two ways. The first is
simulation, in which the whole observation is computed from a model. For
example, ATOCA, introduced above, was tested on simulated SOSS images
\citep{DarveauBernierEtAl2022}. In this test, ATOCA was given correct
calibration inputs, such as the shape of each trace and the wavelength of each
pixel. With these inputs, the spectrum that ATOCA extracted matched the
spectrum used to make the images to within 10~ppm. A simulation, however,
contains only the noise and background that its authors put in.
\citet{CarterEtAl2025} also used simulated data to study how the steps that
flag cosmic-ray hits and fit the ramps affect SOSS spectra. The raw data files
of a real WASP-39~b observation served only as a template. Carter et al.\
replaced the reads in these files with simulated reads of a transit. The
simulated data therefore had the standard format, so the JWST calibration
pipeline could process them. All the pixel values, including the noise, were
simulated, and no background was included.

The second way adds a known signal to real data, so that the sky, the other
stars and the noise are those of an actual observation.
\citet{ChristiansenEtAl2013} added modelled transits to the calibrated pixels
of real stars observed by Kepler. They then compared the signal-to-noise ratio
that the Kepler pipeline recovered for each injected transit with the expected
value. \citet{GirmeniaMetchev2026} added model images of moving Solar System
objects to the raw reads of real JWST NIRCam images, before any calibration.
They compared their method, which uses differences between successive reads, with the standard reduction based on ramp fitting. Neither study recovered a transmission spectrum. In both, the injected
light came from a model of how a source appears on the detector.

The injected light can also be measured rather than modelled, for example the
light of a laboratory lamp. Before launch, NIRSpec was tested in the
laboratory with a lamp in place of a star. \citet{RustamkulovEtAl2022} added
transits to a time series of these lamp-lit images and recovered the injected
spectra with one analysis. The lamp light and the detector noise were real,
but there was no sky. The transits were also added after the ramps had been
fitted, so they did not pass through the steps that turn the reads into count
rates.

Measured light has also been injected into real observations in a
different field, the direct imaging of exoplanets. There, a planet appears as
a faint point of light beside its much brighter star, and the analysis must
remove the light of the star to reveal the planet. To test whether an
analysis recovers a planet, a fake planet is added to the real images. The
fake planet is a faint, scaled copy of the star's own image
\citep{LafreniereEtAl2007}. The injected light is then a measured image of the
star, as in the benchmark described below. The signal, however, is a point of
light beside the star, whereas a transit dims the star itself.

Data challenges score the methods of several teams against the same known signal. The Spitzer data challenge compared seven
methods of analysing eclipse observations of the planet XO-3~b
\citep{IngallsEtAl2016}. The accuracy of the methods was measured on ten
simulated eclipse observations, whose eclipse depth the organisers had set.
Ten real eclipses were analysed as well. Because their depth was not known,
the real eclipses could only show whether each method gave repeatable
results. In direct imaging, the Exoplanet Imaging Data Challenge added fake
planets to eight real data sets from two instruments
\citep{CantalloubeEtAl2024}. The participants, who did not know the injected
values, estimated the positions and spectra of these planets. Their estimates
were then scored against the injected values.

Each of these studies supplies part of what a SOSS benchmark needs. Firstly,
the data should come from a real SOSS exposure, so that the noise, the
background and the field stars are real. Secondly, the transit should dim a
measured image of the star on the detector, including the faint wings of the
traces. Thirdly, the transit should be added to the raw reads, so that each
pipeline runs its own detector steps on the injected data. Finally, several
complete pipelines should recover the spectrum, each without knowing the injected truth until it has delivered its final result. None of the
studies above combines all four. The simulations lack real noise and
background. The Kepler and NIRCam injections used modelled light, the NIRSpec
lamp test had no sky, and the fake planets of direct imaging do not transit.
The two data challenges scored several methods, but neither scored a known
transit or eclipse added to real data. The benchmark that I am building is
designed to meet all four needs.

The second need is difficult to meet. Because a transit dims only the starlight, an injector, the tool that adds the transit to the data, must know
which part of the recorded light belongs to the star. For this, the injector
needs an image of the star on the detector. My first injectors took this image
from the WASP-17~b data themselves. These data, however, cannot show how much
of the faint light in the wings of the traces comes from the star
(Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}). The commissioning study of SOSS met a related
limit. That study could not determine how much of the light of a star falls
outside the extraction box, the band of pixels that is combined into the spectrum. Neither the commissioning data nor WebbPSF, the software that models
how JWST images a star, could provide this fraction \citep{AlbertEtAl2023}.

Calibration programme 4476 was designed to measure the light outside the
extraction box \citep{VolkEspinoza2023}. In a SOSS time series, only a strip of the
detector is read out. Programme 4476 observed a star at its usual position on
this strip, and then again with the star moved off the strip. On the strip,
the difference between the two exposures measures the light of the star. This measurement greatly reduces the need to split the observed light into
starlight and background with a fit. I am building the benchmark from this difference (Section~\ref{sec:measured-benchmark}). I first turn the difference into a cleaned image of the star, by removing the field sources (field stars and any other objects in the field) and smoothing the faint far wings. I then dim this image with a known
transit and add it to the raw reads of the exposure in which the star was
moved off the strip. The noise, the background and the field stars of that
exposure are real. Because I apply the transit to the cleaned image
myself, the injected signal will be known exactly for that image. The
benchmark is designed to show whether the established pipelines recover a
known transit without a detectable bias, and whether NOVA improves on them.

\subsection{Research question and objectives}
\label{sec:research-question}

The central research question of this project is:
\begin{quote}
\raggedright\itshape
How accurately can a transmission spectrum be recovered from the detector
images of a JWST NIRISS/SOSS transit observation, and does fitting the
transit and the background together, directly on the pixels, reduce the
biases that can arise from fitting the transit without a background term and from extracting a spectrum first?
\end{quote}

To make this question testable, I divide it into four objectives.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.,leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item Develop NOVA, a method that fits the transmission spectrum and the
  background together, directly to the detector pixels of NIRISS/SOSS
  observations.
  \item Develop a benchmark that adds a known transit to the raw reads of
  real NIRISS/SOSS exposures and is built independently of the methods it
  tests.
  \item Use the benchmark to compare the accuracy of NOVA with that of the
  established extraction-first pipelines, for several injected transmission
  spectra.
  \item Identify which assumptions limit the accuracy of the recovered
  spectrum, and determine how the resulting errors carry over into the
  atmospheric properties that a retrieval infers. If NOVA recovers spectra
  more accurately, I will find out which of its parts are needed for an accurate spectrum, by switching them off one at a time and measuring how the recovery changes.
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:nova} describes NOVA as it is currently implemented and ends
with a first, preliminary spectrum of WASP-17~b. Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}
sets out what a benchmark needs and what I learned from my first attempts to
build one from the WASP-17~b data. Section~\ref{sec:measured-benchmark}
describes the benchmark that I am now building from calibration programme
4476, and the tests it must pass before any pipeline is scored.
Section~\ref{sec:research-plan} discusses what the first year has shown and
sets out the plan for the rest of the project.
