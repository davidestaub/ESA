\section{Introduction and Literature Review}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Atmospheres as observational targets}
\label{sec:exoplanets-targets}

% Question: What does exoplanet diversity require us to determine?
% Answer: We need to determine the physical and chemical properties of planets
% and establish how reliably those properties can be inferred from observations.
% Evidence: Winn and Fabrycky (2015).
The discovery of planets beyond the Solar System has shown that planetary
systems can look very different from our own. These exoplanets span a wide
range of sizes, temperatures, and orbital configurations, including many with
no close Solar System counterpart \citep{WinnFabrycky2015}. Understanding this
diversity requires determining the physical and chemical properties of
individual planets and assessing how reliably those properties can be inferred
from observations.

% Question: How are exoplanets commonly grouped?
% Answer: Four broad observational categories provide useful orientation, but
% they do not uniquely determine a planet's physical composition.
% Evidence: NASA Science (2026).
For orientation, exoplanets are often described using four broad observational
categories: terrestrial planets, super-Earths, Neptune-like planets, and gas
giants \citep{NASAPlanetTypes2026}. These categories provide useful descriptive
shorthand, but their boundaries are not fundamental, and a category does not
uniquely determine a planet's interior or atmospheric composition.

% Question: Why can bulk measurements not determine a planet's composition?
% Answer: Mass and radius constrain bulk density but do not uniquely determine
% its interior structure or atmospheric mass fraction.
% Evidence: Rogers and Seager (2010) and Madhusudhan (2019).
For well-characterised transiting planets, mass and radius are usually among
the first physical properties inferred. Together they constrain bulk density,
but planets with similar masses and radii can have different interior
structures and atmospheric mass fractions, leaving their exact compositions
underconstrained
\citep{RogersSeager2010}. Distinguishing between these possibilities requires
observables that respond directly to the material and energy in a planet's
outer layers. Atmospheres provide those observables
\citep{Madhusudhan2019}.

% Question: What additional information is carried by an atmosphere?
% Answer: Atmospheric spectra probe composition, energy transport, clouds,
% escape, and possible signatures of formation and evolution.
% Evidence: Oberg et al. (2011), Vidal-Madjar et al. (2003), and Madhusudhan
% (2019).
Atmospheric composition and structure reflect present conditions and may
retain information about a planet's formation and evolution. Atomic and
molecular abundances constrain chemical inventories, elemental ratios, and
atmospheric metallicity. These quantities may carry information about the gas
and solids accreted during formation, although they do not determine a unique
formation history \citep{ObergEtAl2011}. Temperature structures, clouds, and
hazes govern how radiation is absorbed, emitted, and transported, while escape
signatures trace atmospheric loss driven by the host star's high-energy
irradiation
\citep{VidalMadjarEtAl2003}. When interpreted alongside mass, radius, age, and
irradiation, atmospheric measurements can therefore test hypotheses about
formation, migration, enrichment, and long-term mass loss
\citep{Madhusudhan2019}. Atmospheric characterisation provides a route from
detecting a planet to testing physical explanations for its present state. To
do so reliably, however, the atmospheric signal must first be recovered from
the light of the host star.

Transmission spectroscopy measures the wavelength dependence of a transit.
Ignoring limb darkening for the moment, the transit depth is approximately
\begin{equation}
  \delta(\lambda) \simeq
  \left[\frac{R_{\mathrm p}(\lambda)}{R_\star}\right]^2 .
  \label{eq:transit-depth}
\end{equation}
The effective planetary radius $R_{\mathrm p}(\lambda)$ increases where the
atmosphere is more opaque and decreases where it is more transparent.
Absorption by atoms, molecules, clouds, and aerosols can therefore imprint
wavelength-dependent structure on the measured transit depth
\citep{SeagerSasselov2000,Brown2001}.

Hot Jupiters are a subgroup of gas giants that orbit close to their host stars
and are consequently strongly irradiated. Their large radii, high temperatures,
hydrogen-dominated atmospheres, and sometimes low gravities make their
transmission signatures comparatively strong. The first detection of an
exoplanet atmosphere identified sodium absorption in the hot Jupiter
HD~209458~b \citep{CharbonneauEtAl2002}. Hot Jupiters are therefore
useful targets for developing and testing methods that recover transmission
spectra from spectroscopic time series. A stronger signal makes errors in
reduction and light-curve fitting easier to diagnose before moving to planets
with intrinsically weaker atmospheric signatures. Hot Jupiters are a starting
point rather than an end point. Once a method has been validated on these
targets, it can be applied to smaller and cooler planets, including the worlds
most relevant to questions of planetary habitability.

dayside emission by comparing the combined star and planet flux with the stellar
flux measured while the planet is hidden \citep{DemingEtAl2005}.

% Question: Why is atmospheric characterisation an inverse problem?
% Answer: Atmospheric signals are mixed with stellar, instrumental, and
% geometric effects, and different physical states can produce similar spectra.
% Evidence: Rackham et al. (2018), Madhusudhan (2019), and Barstow and Heng

Across all three geometries, the atmosphere is not observed in isolation. Its
signal is embedded in light from the host star and modified by the telescope,
detector, observing geometry, and data analysis. Different combinations of
abundance, temperature, reference radius, clouds, hazes, and stellar
heterogeneity can also produce similar spectral signatures
\citep{RackhamEtAl2018,Madhusudhan2019}. Atmospheric characterisation is
consequently an inverse problem: the data constrain a family of physical
explanations rather than measuring a unique atmospheric state directly
\citep{BarstowHeng2020}. Any atmospheric interpretation can therefore only be
as reliable as the spectrum supplied to the retrieval.

% Question: Is a transmission spectrum the final atmospheric result?
% Answer: No. Detector data are first used to infer a transmission spectrum,
% which is then interpreted with forward models or atmospheric retrievals.
% Evidence: Brown (2001) and Madhusudhan (2019).

The transmission spectrum is itself an inferred data product. Detector
measurements are calibrated and modelled to estimate transit depth as a function
of wavelength. A separate atmospheric forward model or retrieval then maps that
spectrum to composition, temperature, clouds, and other physical quantities.
These two inference stages answer different questions. A retrieval can be
statistically precise while inheriting a bias from the recovered spectrum, and
a well-recovered spectrum can still admit several atmospheric interpretations.
The present work concerns the first stage: how accurately the transmission
spectrum is recovered.

\subsection{From Arecibo to JWST}
\label{sec:arecibo-to-jwst}

% Question: Which discoveries established exoplanets as an observational field?
% Answer: Pulsar timing at Arecibo produced the first confirmed exoplanet
% system, followed by the ELODIE radial-velocity discovery of 51 Pegasi b around
% a solar-type star.
% Evidence: Wolszczan and Frail (1992) and Mayor and Queloz (1995).

The first confirmed exoplanet system was found through pulsar timing. Arecibo
observations revealed periodic timing variations from planets orbiting the
millisecond pulsar PSR B1257+12 \citep{WolszczanFrail1992}. Three years later,
ELODIE radial-velocity measurements established 51 Pegasi b as the first planet
found around a solar-type star \citep{MayorQueloz1995}. Its short orbital period
and Jupiter-like mass revealed a class of close-in giant planet that was not
anticipated from the architecture of the Solar System.

% Question: How did the field move from detection to atmospheric
% characterisation?
% Answer: Hubble detected sodium during transit, Spitzer measured planetary
% thermal emission, and phase-resolved observations began to map atmospheric
% temperature structure.
% Evidence: Charbonneau et al. (2002), Deming et al. (2005), and Knutson et al.
% (2007).

The next transition was from establishing that planets exist to measuring their
atmospheres. Hubble Space Telescope observations detected excess sodium
absorption during the transit of HD 209458b, demonstrating that the atmosphere
of an unresolved exoplanet could be measured spectroscopically
\citep{CharbonneauEtAl2002}. Infrared secondary-eclipse measurements then
isolated thermal emission from a planet \citep{DemingEtAl2005}, and orbital
phase observations resolved changes in its visible hemisphere
\citep{KnutsonEtAl2007}. These experiments established the transmission,
emission, and phase-curve techniques used today.

% Question: What did Kepler and TESS change?
% Answer: Kepler measured planetary occurrence and architecture, while TESS
% shifted discovery toward bright, nearby host stars suited to follow-up.
% Evidence: Borucki et al. (2010), Winn and Fabrycky (2015), Ricker et al.
% (2015), and the NASA Exoplanet Archive (2026).

Kepler changed the scale of exoplanet science by monitoring a large stellar
sample continuously for transits \citep{BoruckiEtAl2010}. Its discoveries
enabled population measurements of planet occurrence, size, period, and system
architecture \citep{WinnFabrycky2015}. TESS retained the transit method but
surveyed nearly the whole sky, prioritising bright and nearby host stars that
are favourable for mass measurements and atmospheric follow-up
\citep{RickerEtAl2015}. Alongside discoveries from other facilities, these
surveys helped expand the confirmed census beyond 6,000 exoplanets, with
thousands of additional candidates awaiting validation
\citep{NASAExoplanetArchive2026}. Together, these missions supplied both the
statistical context and many of the targets required for comparative atmosphere
studies.

% Question: What changed with JWST?
% Answer: JWST combines a large cold aperture, broad near- to mid-infrared
% coverage, stable time-series modes, and performance that met or exceeded
% commissioning expectations.
% Evidence: Gardner et al. (2006) and Rigby et al. (2023).

JWST was designed as a 6.6-metre, cryogenic, infrared-optimised observatory with
instruments spanning the near- to mid-infrared
\citep{GardnerEtAl2006}. Its collecting area increases photon counts, its cold
optics reduce thermal background over much of the infrared, and its instrument
suite gives access to multiple molecular bands over a much broader wavelength
range than a single Hubble or Spitzer mode. Commissioning showed that the
observatory's sensitivity, image quality, stability, and spectral performance
met or exceeded the mission requirements in most areas
\citep{RigbyEtAl2023}. For transmission spectroscopy, this combination improves
precision and, equally importantly, allows several atmospheric absorbers and
continuum regions to be measured within a consistent observing framework.

\subsection{NIRISS/SOSS and the initial WASP-17 b case study}
\label{sec:soss-wasp17}

% Question: What is NIRISS/SOSS designed to measure?
% Answer: SOSS is a slitless, cross-dispersed time-series mode covering
% 0.6--2.8 micrometres at resolving power near 700, with deliberate spatial
% defocus to observe bright stars.
% Evidence: Albert et al. (2023).

The Single Object Slitless Spectroscopy mode of NIRISS was designed
specifically for time-series observations of transiting exoplanets
\citep{AlbertEtAl2023}. The GR700XD grism produces cross-dispersed spectral
orders covering approximately $0.6$--$2.8\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, with resolving power
near $R=700$ at $1.25\,\mu\mathrm{m}$. A weak cylindrical lens spreads the trace over
roughly 23 pixels in the cross-dispersion direction, reducing peak counts and
permitting observations of bright targets. The bandpass includes strong water
bands, alkali features at optical wavelengths, and spectral regions sensitive
to methane, carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, helium, clouds, and hazes.

% Question: How precise is SOSS in practice?
% Answer: Commissioning demonstrated near photon-limited stability to 20 ppm
% over 40-minute bins after removal of a long-term trend, while exoplanet
% spectral precision depends on target and binning.
% Evidence: Albert et al. (2023) and Louie et al. (2025).

Precision must be quoted for a defined target, cadence, and wavelength bin.
There is no single factor by which JWST is more precise than earlier
observatories. During commissioning, a SOSS white-light time series binned down
close to photon noise at the 20 parts-per-million level on a 40-minute
timescale after a long-term trend was removed \citep{AlbertEtAl2023}. For the
WASP-17 b observation used in this project, three independent analyses reported
median spectroscopic uncertainties between 46 and 73 parts per million across
the two useful orders \citep{LouieEtAl2025}. These numbers demonstrate the
relevant scale while also showing why precision claims must specify the
analysis and data product.

% Question: What have early SOSS observations demonstrated?
% Answer: They have recovered broad molecular and atomic signatures and
% constrained clouds across multiple gas-giant targets, while exposing
% instrument-specific reduction challenges.
% Evidence: Feinstein et al. (2023), Radica et al. (2023), and Holmberg and
% Madhusudhan (2023).

Early SOSS studies demonstrated both the scientific power and the analysis
demands of the mode. The WASP-39 b Early Release Science spectrum covered
multiple water bands, potassium, and cloud signatures
\citep{FeinsteinEtAl2023}. Independent studies of WASP-96 b and WASP-39 b
examined background subtraction, $1/f$ noise, order overlap, field-star
contamination, limb darkening, and wavelength-correlated noise
\citep{RadicaEtAl2023,HolmbergMadhusudhan2023}. SOSS therefore provides
high-information spectra, but that information is mediated by a detector
geometry and reduction chain that must be modelled carefully.

% Question: Why begin NOVA development with WASP-17 b?
% Answer: WASP-17 b is a low-density hot Jupiter with an extended atmosphere, a
% complete SOSS transit, prior Hubble and Spitzer constraints, and three
% published reductions of the same NIRISS observation.
% Evidence: Anderson et al. (2010) and Louie et al. (2025).

WASP-17 b is the initial development case rather than the complete scope of the
project. Its low density and low surface gravity give it an extended atmosphere
\citep{AndersonEtAl2010}. It also has prior Hubble and Spitzer
measurements and a complete NIRISS/SOSS transit analysed with Ahsoka,
\texttt{transitspectroscopy}/\texttt{juliet}, and supreme-SPOON
\citep{LouieEtAl2025}. The published study found close agreement among the
three spectra and used the wider SOSS bandpass to constrain the water abundance
more tightly than the earlier data allowed. This combination makes WASP-17 b a
strong case for controlled method comparison. Later benchmark stages will
include other targets and injected atmospheric scenarios.

\subsection{From detector integrations to a transmission spectrum}
\label{sec:detector-to-spectrum}

% Question: Does JWST measure a transmission spectrum directly?
% Answer: No. It records nondestructive detector reads that are converted into
% slopes, calibrated images, extracted spectra, spectral light curves, and
% fitted transit depths.
% Evidence: Bell et al. (2022), Albert et al. (2023), and Radica et al. (2023).

JWST does not record a transmission spectrum directly. NIRISS produces groups
of nondestructive detector reads within each integration. The standard analysis
estimates count-rate slopes, applies detector and spectroscopic calibrations,
models or subtracts background and readout structure, locates the spectral
traces, extracts one-dimensional spectra, bins those spectra into wavelength
channels, and fits a transit and systematics model to each resulting light
curve. The wavelength-dependent planet-to-star radius ratio is then assembled
into the transmission spectrum \citep{BellEtAl2022,AlbertEtAl2023,RadicaEtAl2023}.

% Question: Where can analysis pipelines differ?
% Answer: They can differ at detector calibration, jump detection, 1/f
% correction, background scaling, trace estimation, extraction, outlier
% treatment, limb darkening, systematics models, and posterior inference.
% Evidence: Bell et al. (2022), Radica et al. (2023), Holmberg and Madhusudhan
% (2023), and Louie et al. (2025).

Each stage contains choices that can alter the final spectrum. Examples include
jump-detection thresholds, partially saturated ramps, bad-pixel masks, the
order and location of the $1/f$ correction, scaling of the zodiacal-background
model, treatment of dispersed field stars, trace profiles, extraction widths,
wavelength bins, limb-darkening priors, and time-domain systematics models
\citep{RadicaEtAl2023,HolmbergMadhusudhan2023,LouieEtAl2025}. Eureka! provides
a modular framework from uncalibrated data through light-curve fitting
\citep{BellEtAl2022}. Supreme-SPOON, now developed as exoTEDRF, includes
SOSS-specific detector processing and extraction
\citep{RadicaEtAl2023}. Ahsoka combines components of the official
\texttt{jwst} calibration pipeline, supreme-SPOON, nirHiss, and Eureka!, while
using several forward models in the interpretation of WASP-17 b
\citep{LouieEtAl2025}. These tools overlap, but they are not interchangeable and
do not necessarily implement the same stages.

% Question: Why is order overlap a specific SOSS problem?
% Answer: The first and second spectral orders overlap on the detector, so a
% pixel can contain flux from more than one incident wavelength and order.
% Evidence: Darveau-Bernier et al. (2022) and Radica et al. (2022).

SOSS also illustrates why extraction is a modelling problem. The first and
second spectral orders overlap on the detector, so some pixels contain flux
from both orders. ATOCA addresses this by constructing a linear model for every
detector pixel and solving for the incident spectrum of both orders
simultaneously \citep{DarveauBernierEtAl2022}. Its accuracy depends on the trace
profiles, wavelength maps, throughputs, and spectral-resolution kernels.
APPLESOSS estimates the spatial profiles needed by ATOCA directly from the
observation \citep{RadicaEtAl2022}. These algorithms are already forward models
at the extraction stage, but they infer a stellar spectrum before the
time-dependent transit inference performed later.

\subsection{The validation problem}
\label{sec:validation-problem}

% Question: What does pipeline disagreement establish?
% Answer: It establishes sensitivity to analysis choices, but it does not show
% which result is closer to the unknown true spectrum.
% Evidence: Constantinou et al. (2023), Holmberg and Madhusudhan (2023), and
% Kirk et al. (2024).

When independent reductions disagree, the result is scientifically useful but
incomplete. It identifies sensitivity to methodological choices and may reveal
an unmodelled uncertainty. For WASP-39 b NIRSpec data, different reductions
shifted retrieved abundances by as much as about one dex and altered the
detectability of weaker species \citep{ConstantinouEtAl2023}. For the sub-Earth
GJ 341 b, three reductions all supported the broad conclusion that no atmosphere
was detected, yet two pipelines produced weak candidate features at different
wavelengths and consequently favoured different molecular explanations
\citep{KirkEtAl2024}. Such comparisons diagnose robustness, but the difference
alone does not identify the more accurate spectrum.

% Question: What does pipeline agreement establish?
% Answer: It provides evidence of reproducibility across the tested analyses,
% but shared data, reference files, code, and assumptions can preserve a common
% bias.
% Evidence: Louie et al. (2025), Bell et al. (2022), and the shared components
% documented by the pipeline papers.

Agreement answers a different question. The three WASP-17 b reductions agree
closely over most of the SOSS bandpass \citep{LouieEtAl2025}, which is strong
evidence that the result is reproducible across those implementations.
Nevertheless, the analyses begin from the same observation, use common
calibration reference files, and deliberately standardise several choices.
Some also share software components. Agreement therefore bounds the effect of
the choices that differ, but it cannot exclude a bias caused by assumptions that
are common to every analysis.

% Question: Why can neither agreement nor disagreement establish absolute
% accuracy on real observations?
% Answer: The exact planetary transmission spectrum is not independently known.
% Evidence: The distinction between reproducibility and accuracy follows from
% the absence of an external truth spectrum for a real planet.

The limiting issue is the absence of ground truth. A real planet does not
provide an independently known transmission spectrum against which a recovered
spectrum can be scored. Agreement can establish reproducibility, and
disagreement can expose sensitivity, but neither directly measures absolute
recovery error. Model residuals are also insufficient because an incorrect
decomposition of source, background, and instrument terms can still fit the
observed pixels well. Absolute validation requires data for which the generating
spectrum is known.

\subsection{Structural limitations of extraction-first analyses}
\label{sec:structural-limitations}

% Question: Why does background treatment require particular attention?
% Answer: SOSS pipelines commonly estimate and subtract background before the
% transit fit, so later inference can represent its uncertainty only to the
% extent that the subtraction and covariance have been propagated.
% Evidence: Radica et al. (2023), Holmberg and Madhusudhan (2023), and Louie et
% al. (2025).

Two structural choices in the conventional chain motivate this project. The
first is background treatment. Published SOSS analyses commonly scale a
zodiacal-background model using pixels outside the trace and subtract it before
spectral extraction and transit fitting
\citep{RadicaEtAl2023,HolmbergMadhusudhan2023,LouieEtAl2025}. This is a
reasonable and often effective operation. It is not, however, the same as
jointly inferring the background with the transit. Once subtraction has
occurred, the later fit can account for uncertainty in the background only to
the extent that its estimation error and covariance were propagated into the
extracted light curves.

% Question: How can background error affect a transmission spectrum?
% Answer: Additive light allocated incorrectly between source and background
% changes the fractional transit depth and can introduce wavelength-dependent
% offsets or slopes.
% Evidence: Kelson (2003), Radica et al. (2023), and Holmberg and Madhusudhan
% (2023).

This distinction matters because background is additive while a transit is
multiplicative on the stellar contribution. If a constant amount of flux is
assigned incorrectly to the background, the inferred out-of-transit stellar
flux changes and so does the fractional depth. If that error varies across the
detector, it can alter spectral offsets, slopes, or local features. Classical
two-dimensional spectroscopy already shows the value of modelling background
in the native detector geometry before resampling
\citep{Kelson2003}. For slitless SOSS data, the problem also includes structured
zodiacal light, field-star spectra, masked pixels, and $1/f$ readout structure
\citep{RadicaEtAl2023,HolmbergMadhusudhan2023}.

% Question: Is one-dimensional extraction itself a mistake?
% Answer: No. Optimal extraction can be statistically efficient when its
% spatial profile and noise model are correct.
% Evidence: Horne (1986).

The second choice is compression from detector pixels to one-dimensional
spectra. Extraction is not inherently defective. The optimal-extraction
formalism of \citet{Horne1986} uses a spatial profile and inverse-variance
weights to improve signal-to-noise while preserving spectrophotometric
accuracy. Under its assumptions, the extracted flux is an efficient summary of
the two-dimensional data. The relevant question is therefore not whether
extraction occurs, but which likelihood information and model uncertainty are
retained when it occurs.

% Question: When can extraction lose information relevant to later inference?
% Answer: Loss occurs when the compression fixes an imperfect detector model or
% omits the covariance and resolution operator needed to reconstruct the pixel
% likelihood.
% Evidence: Bolton and Schlegel (2010), Bolton et al. (2012), and
% Darveau-Bernier et al. (2022).

Two-dimensional forward extraction makes this condition explicit.
Spectro-perfectionism models the detector image with the full
two-dimensional point-spread function and shows that a one-dimensional product
can preserve the pixel-level likelihood if the extracted spectrum is delivered
with an appropriate covariance and resolution description
\citep{BoltonSchlegel2010,BoltonEtAl2012}. ATOCA applies a related pixel model
to overlapping SOSS orders \citep{DarveauBernierEtAl2022}. Conversely, if the
trace, wavelength mixing, background, bad-pixel treatment, or order
decomposition is fixed incorrectly, and the resulting covariance is not carried
forward, the later light-curve fit cannot recover the discarded information.

% Question: What limitation remains even after a sophisticated extraction?
% Answer: Existing extraction models normally estimate the stellar spectrum
% before fitting the time-dependent transit, so background, instrument response,
% and transit depth are not inferred in one likelihood.
% Evidence: Darveau-Bernier et al. (2022), Bell et al. (2022), and Radica et al.
% (2023).

Even a forward-modelled extraction normally separates the stellar-spectrum
estimate from the later transit fit. ATOCA treats the incident spectrum as the
unknown quantity at each integration, while time-dependent transit parameters
are inferred after extraction. This ordering is practical and may be entirely
adequate. It does, however, prevent the transit model from helping to separate
stellar light, additive background, overlapping orders, and detector response
within a single likelihood. Whether that separation causes a meaningful bias
for SOSS transmission spectra is an empirical question, not an assumption of
this project.

\subsection{A controlled benchmark and detector-level inference}
\label{sec:benchmark-and-nova}

% Question: How can absolute recovery accuracy be measured?
% Answer: Generate realistic detector time series from a known injected
% transmission spectrum, give identical data to independent recovery methods,
% and reveal the injected spectrum only after predictions are fixed.
% Evidence: This is the controlled benchmark design developed in this project.

The validation problem motivates a detector-level benchmark. A forward injector
will generate time-series observations with a known transmission spectrum,
stellar signal, instrument response, additive background, noise, and detector
flags. Every recovery method will receive the same synthetic detector cube and
the same declared auxiliary information. The injected spectrum will remain
unavailable to the recovery process until its prediction has been fixed.
Recovery error can then be measured directly as a function of wavelength,
spectral morphology, order, signal level, background structure, and detector
condition.

% Question: How does NOVA address the two structural limitations?
% Answer: NOVA fits calibrated detector pixels directly and jointly models the
% time-dependent stellar transit, additive background, instrument response, and
% nuisance components before spectral compression.
% Evidence: The NOVA forward model described in the Methods section.

NOVA is the complementary recovery method. Instead of extracting a stellar
spectrum and fitting spectral light curves in separate stages, NOVA evaluates a
forward model in detector space. The wavelength-dependent transmission spectrum
is shared across the useful SOSS orders, propagated through the instrument
response, and fitted jointly with additive background and nuisance components.
Only stellar light is multiplied by the transit model. This preserves the
physical distinction between source and background and allows information from
multiple detector pixels and orders to constrain the same transmission
spectrum.

% Question: Why are both the benchmark and NOVA necessary?
% Answer: NOVA proposes a different recovery model, while the benchmark
% determines whether that model is accurate and under which conditions it
% improves on extraction-first analyses.
% Evidence: The separation of injector, recovery, and scoring in the benchmark
% protocol.

The benchmark and NOVA answer different parts of the problem. NOVA cannot
validate itself, because a successful fit to pixels does not prove that the
stellar, background, and transit components were separated correctly. The
benchmark supplies the missing truth and permits a controlled comparison with
extraction-based methods. It can also reveal conditions under which established
pipelines are already unbiased, conditions under which NOVA improves recovery,
and conditions under which every tested method fails because the instrument or
injector model is inadequate.

\subsection{Research question and objectives}
\label{sec:research-question}

% Question: What is the central research question?
% Answer: How accurately and robustly can JWST/NIRISS SOSS transmission spectra
% be recovered from detector-level time-series observations, and can joint
% detector-level inference reduce biases associated with background treatment
% and extraction-first analysis?
% Evidence: The validation problem and structural limitations established in
% the preceding sections.

This project asks:
\begin{quote}
\raggedright\itshape
How accurately and robustly can JWST/NIRISS SOSS transmission spectra
be recovered from detector-level time-series observations, and can joint
detector-level inference reduce biases associated with background treatment and
extraction-first analysis?
\end{quote}

% Question: Which objectives make the research question testable?
% Answer: Build a truth-known benchmark, develop NOVA, compare recovery methods
% under controlled conditions, and identify the assumptions that limit
% accuracy.
% Evidence: The Methods and future-work programme of this assessment.

The research question is divided into four objectives:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.,leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item develop an independent detector-level benchmark with known injected
  transmission spectra;
  \item develop NOVA as a joint detector-level inference method for
  NIRISS/SOSS transmission spectroscopy;
  \item compare NOVA with extraction-based analyses under controlled observing
  conditions and atmospheric scenarios; and
  \item identify the detector, calibration, and modelling assumptions that
  limit recovery accuracy and determine how those limitations propagate into
  atmospheric interpretation.
\end{enumerate}

% Question: What is the scope of the present assessment?
% Answer: The assessment establishes the scientific motivation, current NOVA
% formulation, first WASP-17 b experiments, and future benchmark programme
% without claiming results that have not yet passed validation.
% Evidence: The Methods, Results and Current Status, and Discussion and Future
% Work sections.

The present assessment defines the problem, documents the current method, and
sets out the experiments required to answer the research question. WASP-17 b is
the first development case. It will be followed by controlled variations in
background, spectral morphology, noise, masking, instrument response, and
target properties, followed by application to additional observations. Results
are reported only where the associated injector and recovery checks have been
completed.
