\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Exoplanets and atmospheric targets}
\label{sec:exoplanets-targets}

% Question: What are exoplanets, and why do we study them?
% Answer: Exoplanets reveal the diversity of planetary systems and constrain
% planet formation, migration, evolution, and the conditions associated with
% potentially habitable environments.
% Evidence: Foundational discovery papers, occurrence studies, population
% reviews, and planet-formation literature.

% Question: What kinds of exoplanets have been discovered?
% Answer: Exoplanets span a wide range of masses, radii, temperatures, orbital
% architectures, and bulk compositions, including populations that are not
% represented in the Solar System.
% Evidence: Mass-radius distributions, period-radius distributions, and
% occurrence-rate studies from radial-velocity and transit surveys.

% Question: Which planets are most accessible to atmospheric characterisation?
% Answer: Large and highly irradiated planets generally produce the strongest
% transmission signals. Hot Jupiters therefore provide useful initial targets
% for developing and testing atmospheric methods, while smaller and cooler
% planets remain important longer-term targets.
% Evidence: Transmission-signal scaling, atmospheric scale-height relations,
% and the target populations observed with Hubble and JWST.

Exoplanets are planets that orbit stars other than the Sun. Their known
population spans a wide range of masses, radii, temperatures, and orbital
architectures, including classes of planet with no direct Solar System
counterpart. This diversity provides empirical tests of planet formation and
evolution and places the Solar System in a broader planetary context. Large,
highly irradiated planets currently provide some of the strongest atmospheric
signals, making hot Jupiters useful initial targets for developing and testing
spectroscopic methods.

\subsection{Atmospheres as probes of planets}
\label{sec:atmospheres}

% Question: What can an atmosphere reveal about a planet?
% Answer: Atmospheric composition, temperature, clouds, hazes, chemistry, and
% escape connect present-day observations to formation history, elemental
% inventories, climate, and long-term evolution.
% Evidence: Comparative-planetology reviews, atmospheric retrieval studies,
% chemical-equilibrium and disequilibrium studies, and escape measurements.

% Question: Why is atmospheric characterisation difficult?
% Answer: Atmospheric signals are small relative to the stellar and
% instrumental signals. Their interpretation is also affected by degeneracies
% between composition, temperature, clouds, stellar contamination, and
% instrumental systematics.
% Evidence: Retrieval-degeneracy studies, stellar-contamination literature,
% instrument-systematics studies, and precision analyses.

A planetary atmosphere contains information that cannot be recovered from mass
and radius alone. Its composition, temperature, clouds, hazes, chemistry, and
escape reflect the planet's present environment and its formation and
evolutionary history. Measuring those properties is difficult because the
atmospheric contribution is small compared with the stellar and instrumental
signals, while several atmospheric and stellar effects can produce similar
spectral signatures.

\subsection{Observing exoplanet atmospheres}
\label{sec:observing-atmospheres}

% Question: How can the atmosphere of an unresolved exoplanet be observed?
% Answer: Transit, eclipse, and phase-curve observations isolate
% wavelength-dependent changes associated with the planet and its atmosphere.
% Evidence: Foundational transmission, emission, and phase-curve papers.

% Question: How does transmission spectroscopy probe an atmosphere?
% Answer: Atmospheric opacity changes the effective planetary radius with
% wavelength. The resulting wavelength-dependent transit depth forms a
% transmission spectrum.
% Evidence: Transmission-spectroscopy theory, early atmospheric detections,
% and standard radiative-transfer treatments.

% Question: What is inferred at each stage of the analysis?
% Answer: Detector measurements are used to infer a transmission spectrum, and
% the transmission spectrum is then used to infer atmospheric properties. The
% spectrum and the atmospheric retrieval are separate inference products.
% Evidence: Published reduction, light-curve fitting, and atmospheric-retrieval
% workflows.

Exoplanet atmospheres can be investigated through transmission, emission, and
phase-curve spectroscopy. This work focuses on transmission spectroscopy, in
which wavelength-dependent atmospheric opacity changes the effective planetary
radius measured during transit. The resulting transmission spectrum is an
inferred data product that can subsequently be interpreted with an atmospheric
retrieval. Recovering the spectrum and interpreting the atmosphere are separate
inference problems.

\subsection{From Arecibo to JWST}
\label{sec:arecibo-to-jwst}

% Question: When were the first confirmed exoplanets discovered?
% Answer: Pulsar-timing observations made with the Arecibo radio telescope
% revealed planets orbiting the millisecond pulsar PSR B1257+12 in 1992.
% Evidence: Wolszczan and Frail (1992).

% Question: Why was the ELODIE discovery of 51 Pegasi b a separate milestone?
% Answer: It was the first confirmed planet found around a solar-type star and
% revealed the unexpected hot-Jupiter population.
% Evidence: Mayor and Queloz (1995).

% Question: What did Hubble add?
% Answer: Hubble demonstrated that atmospheric absorption could be detected
% during exoplanet transits and established space-based transmission
% spectroscopy as a practical method.
% Evidence: Early Hubble transmission-spectroscopy detections and subsequent
% atmospheric surveys.

% Question: What did Kepler add?
% Answer: Kepler transformed individual discoveries into population science by
% measuring the occurrence and diversity of planetary systems.
% Evidence: Kepler mission papers and occurrence-rate studies.

% Question: What did TESS add?
% Answer: TESS identified transiting planets around bright and nearby stars
% that are suitable for detailed atmospheric follow-up.
% Evidence: TESS mission papers, target catalogues, and follow-up studies.

% Question: What changed with JWST?
% Answer: JWST provides high infrared sensitivity, broad wavelength coverage,
% high throughput, and dedicated time-series modes that support detailed
% atmospheric characterisation.
% Evidence: JWST and instrument papers, commissioning results, and
% representative comparisons with pre-JWST observations. Quantify precision
% using specific targets and wavelength bins rather than one universal factor.

The observational history of exoplanets progressed from detection to population
science and atmospheric characterisation. Pulsar timing with the Arecibo radio
telescope revealed the first confirmed exoplanets around PSR B1257+12. ELODIE
then detected 51 Pegasi b, the first confirmed planet around a solar-type star
and an early example of a hot Jupiter. Hubble demonstrated that atmospheric
absorption could be detected during transit. Kepler established the frequency
and diversity of planetary systems, while TESS identified nearby transiting
systems around bright stars. JWST now provides the infrared sensitivity,
wavelength coverage, throughput, and time-series observing modes needed for
more detailed atmospheric characterisation.

\subsection{NIRISS/SOSS and the initial WASP-17 b case study}
\label{sec:soss-wasp17}

% Question: Why is NIRISS/SOSS useful for transmission spectroscopy?
% Answer: NIRISS/SOSS provides high-throughput slitless time-series
% spectroscopy with multiple spectral orders and broad wavelength coverage.
% Evidence: NIRISS/SOSS instrument, commissioning, and calibration papers.

% Question: Why begin with WASP-17 b?
% Answer: Its atmospheric signal, existing NIRISS/SOSS observation, and
% published analyses make it a useful initial case for method development and
% controlled comparison.
% Evidence: WASP-17 system studies, the JWST observing programme, and published
% NIRISS/SOSS analyses.

% Question: Does WASP-17 b define the complete scope of the project?
% Answer: No. It is the initial development and validation case. The planned
% benchmark will include additional planets and injected atmospheric scenarios.
% Evidence: The future-work design and target-selection criteria developed in
% this assessment.

The Single Object Slitless Spectroscopy mode of NIRISS provides high-throughput
time-series spectroscopy across multiple spectral orders. Its broad wavelength
coverage makes it well suited to transmission spectroscopy, but its slitless
detector geometry also makes background treatment, order overlap, wavelength
mapping, and extraction important parts of the analysis. WASP-17 b is the
initial development case because it has a strong atmospheric signal, an
existing NIRISS/SOSS observation, and published analyses suitable for
comparison. It does not define the eventual scope of the benchmark, which will
include additional planets and injected atmospheric scenarios.

\subsection{From detector integrations to a transmission spectrum}
\label{sec:detector-to-spectrum}

% Question: Is a transmission spectrum measured directly by JWST?
% Answer: No. Detector integrations are calibrated, backgrounds are treated,
% spectra are extracted, spectral light curves are constructed, and transit
% models are fitted before a transmission spectrum is obtained.
% Evidence: JWST calibration documentation and published analysis workflows.

% Question: How do existing pipelines perform this analysis?
% Answer: Ahsoka, Eureka!, supreme-SPOON, and related approaches implement
% different combinations of detector calibration, background treatment,
% extraction, outlier handling, light-curve modelling, and spectral inference.
% Evidence: Pipeline papers, software documentation, and published applications,
% with the scope of each pipeline stated because they do not all cover the same
% stages of the analysis.

JWST does not measure a transmission spectrum directly. It records a time
series of two-dimensional detector integrations. These measurements are
calibrated, corrected for background and detector effects, converted into
one-dimensional spectra, divided into spectral light curves, and fitted with
transit and systematic models. Pipelines such as Ahsoka, Eureka!, and
supreme-SPOON implement different parts of this chain and make different choices
about calibration, extraction, outlier treatment, and light-curve inference.

\subsection{The validation problem}
\label{sec:validation-problem}

% Question: What does it mean when pipelines disagree?
% Answer: The recovered spectrum is sensitive to methodological choices or
% unrepresented uncertainties. The disagreement reveals a robustness problem
% but does not identify which result is more accurate.
% Evidence: Published multi-pipeline comparisons and reanalyses of common data.

% Question: What does it mean when pipelines agree?
% Answer: Agreement increases confidence but does not establish accuracy.
% Pipelines can share calibration products, masks, assumptions, extraction
% strategies, or systematic models and can therefore share a bias.
% Evidence: Common dependencies and assumptions documented across pipelines.

% Question: Why can neither agreement nor disagreement determine absolute
% accuracy?
% Answer: The exact transmission spectrum of a real planet is unknown.
% Evidence: This follows from the absence of an external truth spectrum for a
% real observation.

When independent analyses disagree, the difference demonstrates sensitivity to
methodological choices but does not identify which spectrum is more accurate.
Agreement is also insufficient to establish accuracy because analyses can share
calibration products, masks, extraction assumptions, or systematic models. In
both cases the central difficulty is the same: the exact transmission spectrum
of a real planet is unknown.

\subsection{Structural limitations of extraction-first analyses}
\label{sec:structural-limitations}

% Question: What is the potential limitation of estimating or subtracting the
% background before the transit fit?
% Answer: Uncertainty and degeneracy in the background estimate may not be
% propagated fully into the recovered transmission spectrum.
% Evidence: Pipeline methods, background-sensitivity studies, and detector
% diagnostics.

% Question: Why can background misallocation matter?
% Answer: It can alter transit depths, spectral offsets, slopes, features, and
% reported uncertainties.
% Evidence: Injection tests, reduction comparisons, and background perturbation
% studies.

% Question: What is the potential limitation of collapsing detector pixels into
% spectral light curves before fitting the transit?
% Answer: Extraction compresses the two-dimensional detector measurements and
% fixes some instrumental assumptions before the astrophysical model is fitted.
% Evidence: Extraction equations and published pipeline workflows.

% Question: Why can this compression matter?
% Answer: Spatial information relevant to background separation, bad pixels,
% overlapping orders, wavelength mixing, and the line-spread function can be
% discarded or fixed too early.
% Evidence: Optimal-extraction theory, spectro-perfectionism, detector
% simulations, and extraction-sensitivity studies.

% Question: What would an alternative analysis need to do?
% Answer: It would retain detector-level information, model the stellar and
% background signals jointly, and postpone irreversible compression until after
% the transmission spectrum has been inferred.
% Evidence: Inverse-problem literature and the design principles of NOVA.

Two common analysis choices deserve particular attention. First, the background
is often estimated or subtracted before the transit model is fitted. Uncertainty
or degeneracy in that estimate may then be propagated incompletely into the
transmission spectrum. Second, extraction collapses two-dimensional detector
measurements into one-dimensional spectral light curves before the astrophysical
fit. This compression can fix or discard information relevant to background
separation, bad pixels, overlapping orders, wavelength mixing, and the
line-spread function.

\subsection{A controlled benchmark and detector-level inference}
\label{sec:benchmark-and-nova}

% Question: How can absolute recovery accuracy be measured?
% Answer: Construct realistic detector-level observations with a known injected
% spectrum and analyse the identical data with independent recovery methods.
% Evidence: Benchmark-design principles and existing injection-recovery studies.

% Question: How can early background subtraction and detector compression be
% avoided?
% Answer: Infer the transmission spectrum directly from the detector time series
% while jointly modelling the stellar signal, background, instrument response,
% transit, and relevant nuisance components.
% Evidence: The NOVA model and related detector-level inverse methods.

% Question: Why are both responses required?
% Answer: The benchmark measures whether a recovery method is accurate. NOVA is
% the proposed recovery method and cannot validate itself.
% Evidence: The separation between the injector, recovery methods, and scoring
% procedure described in the Methods section.

These limitations motivate two complementary developments. A controlled
detector-level benchmark provides synthetic observations with a known injected
transmission spectrum, allowing absolute recovery accuracy to be measured.
NOVA provides an alternative recovery strategy that infers the transmission
spectrum directly from the detector time series while jointly modelling the
stellar signal, additive background, instrument response, transit, and nuisance
components. The benchmark evaluates the method; NOVA cannot validate itself.

\subsection{Research question and objectives}
\label{sec:research-question}

% Question: What is the central research question?
% Answer: How accurately and robustly can JWST/NIRISS SOSS transmission spectra
% be recovered from detector-level time-series observations, and can joint
% detector-level inference reduce biases associated with background treatment
% and extraction-first analysis?
% Evidence: The validation problem and structural limitations established above.

% Question: What objectives make the research question testable?
% Answer: Develop an independent detector-level benchmark with known injected
% spectra; develop NOVA as a joint detector-level inference method; compare NOVA
% with extraction-based analyses under controlled conditions; and identify the
% detector, calibration, and modelling assumptions that limit recovery accuracy.
% Evidence: The experimental design and evaluation metrics defined in the
% Methods section.

This project asks how accurately and robustly JWST/NIRISS SOSS transmission
spectra can be recovered from detector-level time-series observations, and
whether joint detector-level inference can reduce biases associated with
background treatment and extraction-first analysis. The work has four
objectives: to develop an independent detector-level benchmark with known
injected spectra; to develop NOVA as a joint detector-level inference method; to
compare NOVA with extraction-based analyses under controlled conditions; and to
identify the detector, calibration, and modelling assumptions that limit
recovery accuracy.
