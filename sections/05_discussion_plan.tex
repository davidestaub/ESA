\section{Discussion and Research Plan}
\label{sec:research-plan}

In this section, I first discuss what the work so far shows, and then set out
the plan for the rest of the project.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The main lesson of the first year concerns the benchmark rather than any one
pipeline. An injection test on real SOSS data has to decide which light
belongs to the star, and for WASP-17~b this decision reversed the ranking of
the two pipelines I tested (Section~\ref{sec:which-light}). Every injection
test into real data makes such a decision, whether or not it is stated, and
its verdict only holds under it. The benchmark of
Section~\ref{sec:measured-benchmark} constrains this choice with a separate
measurement of the star. The
price is a different star, a different field, a different readout and, at
first, a grey transit, so it cannot say how the pipelines behave on WASP-17
itself. It is designed instead to test which kinds of error each method makes
when the injected light curve is known, and the comparison of the six science
visits will be read against these errors.

NOVA now produces a first spectrum of the real WASP-17~b visit, which lies
above the published spectrum at every wavelength and most in the red
(Section~\ref{sec:nova-real}). The new benchmark will measure recovery
errors under its declared conditions, but the accuracy of either real
spectrum of WASP-17~b remains unknown. The uncertainty of the NOVA spectrum
is also not yet validated, since the first uncertainty ensemble
failed its held-out test and the second was not completed. One further lead
remains open. In the red, the faint light far from the traces of WASP-17
appears to dim during the transit by more than predictions of the stellar
halo suggest. This excess is sensitive to the baseline and the processing,
and whether it comes from the star, the background or the detector is
unresolved.

\subsection{Research plan}
\label{sec:plan}

I plan five papers, all built on NOVA and on the benchmark of
Section~\ref{sec:measured-benchmark}. The first presents the method, the
benchmark and WASP-17~b. The second applies the same procedure to the five
other science visits, and the third turns to repeated visits of active stars.
The last two extend NOVA to NIRSpec and to secondary eclipses.
Figure~\ref{fig:gantt} shows the schedule.

\paragraph{Paper 1: NOVA, the benchmark and WASP-17~b.} The aim is to test
whether NOVA recovers transmission spectra more accurately than the
extraction-based pipelines, and whether it changes what is concluded about
WASP-17~b. I will build and test
the benchmark of Section~\ref{sec:measured-benchmark} and score NOVA and
exoTEDRF on it, together with Ahsoka and \texttt{transitspectroscopy} where
their native workflows run end to end. The benchmark tests
how each method separates starlight from background and extracts the
spectrum. The first test uses one common light curve on an A-type star, with
the limb-darkening controls and limits described in
Section~\ref{sec:benchmark-limits}, and its verdict applies to the cooler
hosts of the science targets only by argument. NOVA's spectrum of the real
WASP-17~b visit will then be refitted with the current geometry criterion,
with its uncertainty from an ensemble that corresponds to the updated fit
and passes its held-out test, and compared with the three published
reductions. I aim to finish this paper before the
HWO2026 conference in Paris at the end of November 2026, where I will
present this work.

\paragraph{Paper 2: Five further SOSS visits.} The aim is to find out whether
the atmospheric conclusions for other SOSS targets change when their
observations are analysed with NOVA, and if so, why. The five remaining science visits, of HAT-P-26~b, WASP-39~b,
HAT-P-18~b, WASP-96~b and WASP-80~b, are analysed as real data with NOVA
and the extraction-based pipelines of Paper 1. They use a common analysis
procedure, with any settings specific to a visit declared before the
recovered spectra are compared. Agreement or disagreement on real data will
still not identify the more accurate pipeline. WASP-39~b has strong
water features and well-studied spectra
\citep{FeinsteinEtAl2023,CarterEtAl2024}, whilst the published analyses of
WASP-96~b disagree on whether its spectrum shows a scattering slope
\citep{TaylorEtAl2023,WangEtAl2026}. NOVA has no model of field stars yet.
Where they contaminate the traces, I will first exclude the affected columns,
and only build a model of the contaminating spectra where the loss of
coverage matters for the science. The same procedure can later be extended
to the rest of the public SOSS archive, as was done for Hubble data
\citep{TsiarasEtAl2018}.

\paragraph{Paper 3: Repeated visits and stellar variability.} The aim is to
test whether repeated visits of the same planet give the same spectrum once
instrumental and stellar effects are accounted for. I will start with the
two SOSS transits of GJ~9827~d \citep{PiauletGhorayebEtAl2024}, then turn to
the four of LHS~1140~b \citep{CadieuxEtAl2024,GressierEtAl2026} and the five
of TRAPPIST-1~f, each of which contains a flare \citep{LimEtAl2026}. NOVA will gain a flare
model, whose timing is
shared across wavelengths whilst its brightness is fitted at each wavelength,
and a model of unocculted spots and faculae. A planned pair of MRes
projects, starting in January 2027, will study stellar variability and flares
in the out-of-transit SOSS data, which would feed directly into this paper.

\paragraph{Paper 4: Joint NIRISS and NIRSpec analysis.} The aim is to test
whether observations of the same planet with both instruments can be
explained by one transmission spectrum. Spectra from different instruments agree better once they are
analysed with the same choices \citep{CarterEtAl2024}, but agreement under
shared choices does not show that the choices are right. I will therefore
extend NOVA and the benchmark to NIRSpec, validate each mode on injected
data, and only then compare the real spectra, starting with WASP-39~b and
LHS~1140~b \citep{DamianoEtAl2024}.

\paragraph{Paper 5: Secondary eclipses.} The aim is to find out whether a
detector-level analysis makes weak eclipse measurements more reliable. WASP-17~b is the starting point,
since the retrieved properties of its SOSS eclipse spectrum were sensitive to
whether negative eclipse depths were allowed at optical wavelengths
\citep{GressierEtAl2024}. NOVA will
replace the transit by an eclipse model with signed amplitudes, and the
benchmark will add a known planetary signal that disappears during the
eclipse.

\paragraph{Dependencies and risks.} Paper 1 provides the benchmark that
every later paper reuses. Paper 2 settles the treatment of field stars, and
Paper 3 the stellar model that the later papers need for active hosts. In every
paper, the recovered spectra are fixed before any atmospheric retrieval is
run, so that they cannot be tuned towards an atmospheric answer. The first
risk is that an injected case fails one of its tests. Such a case would be
reported and excluded, with the scope of the remaining benchmark stated,
whilst a pipeline that fails on a valid case keeps that failure in its
score. Both the three- and the four-group versions will be reported.
A second risk is that the rerun uncertainty ensemble fails its held-out test
again. The
spectrum of WASP-17~b would then be published without a validated
uncertainty, or not at all, and I would state which.
Contamination by field stars may also remove more wavelength coverage
than a science question can tolerate, in which case the affected spectra are
released with the reduced coverage and the limitation is stated. The
NIRSpec extension is the largest technical development of the later
papers. If it does not validate in time, Paper 4 is reduced to comparing
independently validated reductions of the two instruments, with their
limitations stated, and Papers 4 and 5 are the
adjustable part of the plan. Finally, NOVA may not outperform the
extraction-based pipelines. A fair benchmark would still be a useful result,
because it would document the errors of the methods under the tested
conditions.
