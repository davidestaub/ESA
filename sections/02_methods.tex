\section{Methods and Benchmark Design}
\label{sec:methods}

% Question: What do the methods of this project consist of?
% Answer: NOVA, the detector-level inference method, and the injector, which
% writes a known transit into the raw ramps of real JWST data so that
% recovery accuracy can be measured absolutely.
% Evidence: The structure of this section; NOVA_ASTRA_EXECUTION_20260905_R1/
% STATUS.md.

In this section I describe the two methods developed in this project.
Firstly, NOVA (Section~\ref{sec:nova}) fits a forward model of the
calibrated detector time series directly to the detector pixels, with the
transit geometry inferred from the same data. Secondly, the injector
(Section~\ref{sec:injector}) writes a known transit into the raw ramps of
the first 229 integrations of the real WASP-17 visit, all of which were
taken before the real transit, so that the accuracy of a recovery can be
measured against a known spectrum. The constants of an earlier, frozen
release of NOVA (26 August 2026) are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:nova};
where the present configuration differs from that release, the values given
in this section are the current ones.

\subsection{\nova{}}
\label{sec:nova}

% Question: What problem does NOVA solve, and in what form?
% Answer: It fits one forward model of the detector to all retained
% samples, conditional on calibration products and a white-light geometry
% estimated beforehand from the same input.
% Evidence: Sections 1.3 and 1.4; config/MATCHED_COVARIANCE_ENSEMBLE_R1.json
% (estimator.rebuild).

NOVA describes the observation with a single forward model of the detector
data. The model maps the transmission spectrum $D(\lambda)$, the limb
darkening $\bm{u}(\lambda)$, the stellar continuum and the additive
background, through a spatial response, to a prediction for every retained
detector sample, and all four are then estimated together by minimising one
objective over these samples. The fit is conditional on quantities which
are estimated beforehand from the same data and then held fixed: the
spatial response, the transit geometry from NOVA's white-light fit, a
source-curvature factor which corrects for slow curvature in the brightness
of the star, the temporal pattern and anchor of the background, and the
uncertainty scales. No one-dimensional spectrum is extracted before the fit,
and the spectrum is only mapped onto wavelength bins at the very end, for
reporting (Section~\ref{sec:reporting}).

I run NOVA both on the real WASP-17 visit, using all 720 integrations after
detector processing, and on the injected inputs of
Section~\ref{sec:injector}, which are built from the first 229 integrations
of the same visit. Three calibration products are carried over from the
earlier release: the eight spatial background maps, the two per-order
uncertainty scales and the functional form of the source-curvature factor.
Every other calibration product, and the white-light geometry, is
re-estimated on each input. In the rest of this section I describe the
forward model and each of its components, the objective which NOVA
minimises and how it is minimised, the white-light fit which provides the
transit geometry and, finally, how the uncertainty of the recovered spectrum
is estimated.

\subsubsection{The detector forward model}

% Question: What does NOVA predict for each detector sample?
% Answer: Continuum times exposure-integrated transit times spatial
% response, corrected by a source-curvature factor, plus an additive
% background; in the implementation the transit is evaluated on fine
% wavelength nodes and projected through the response.
% Evidence: proposals/NOMENCLATURE_PRESENTATION.md; proposals/
% handoff_nova_s_20260828/01_EXACT_NOVA_S_METHOD.md section 3;
% NOVA_SP_NOVA_S_SCIENCE_FREEZE_20260826_R1/code/sparse_varpro/
% fine_grid_transit_adapter.py (5,454 fine nodes) and response_aware_varpro.py;
% reports/OFFTRACE_TRANSIT_COMPONENT_DIAGNOSIS_20260921.md section 2.

To build the model, the retained detector samples are organised into
wavelength groups, where a group $g$ contains the pixels $P_g$ of one
spectral order which lie in the same detector column, and I write $g(p)$
for the group of a given order which contains pixel $p$. Denoting by
$Y_{tp}$ the calibrated count rate recorded in pixel $p$ during integration
$t$, NOVA predicts
\begin{equation}
 \widehat Y_{tp}
 = \gamma_t\, C_{tg}\,
   \mathcal{T}_{tg}(D, \bm{u};\, \Omega)\,
   q^\star_{pg}
 + B_{tp},
 \label{eq:nova-forward}
\end{equation}
where $C_{tg}$ is the brightness of the stellar continuum in group $g$ at
time $t$, $\mathcal{T}_{tg}$ is the transit factor, which dims this light
according to the depth $D$ and limb darkening $\bm{u}$ at the wavelengths of
the group and the orbital state $\Omega$, $\gamma_t$ is the source-curvature
factor, $q^\star_{pg}$ is the spatial response, which distributes the light
of the group over its pixels, with $\sum_{p\in P_g} q^\star_{pg}=1$, and
$B_{tp}$ is the additive background. Where the traces of the two orders
overlap, the stellar term is summed over the groups of both orders which
contain the pixel.

Equation~\ref{eq:nova-forward} is written per group for clarity. In the
implementation, the continuum of each column is first spread onto a fine
grid of 5{,}454 wavelength nodes, the transit is evaluated at each node, and
the result is projected onto the pixels through the spectral-response
(line-spread) kernel and the spatial response, so that each column receives
light from a small range of wavelengths. The positions of the groups on the
detector, their wavelengths and the kernel follow the public trace and
wavelength calibration \citep{BainesEtAl2023Trace,BainesEtAl2023Wavelength}
and the associated spectral-response kernel
\citep{DarveauBernierEtAl2022,JWSTPipeline2025}. Note that the transit
factor and $\gamma_t$ multiply only the stellar term, so any light which is
assigned to the background is treated as light that does not dim during the
transit.

The support of the model contains 97{,}557 pixel positions per integration,
in 2{,}720 groups, after six groups at the edge of the detector without a
reliable wavelength mapping have been excluded. Individual samples are then
removed if their value is not finite, their uncertainty is not positive, or
they carry the \texttt{DO\_NOT\_USE} flag of the JWST data-quality array.

\subsubsection{The shared spectrum and transit model}

% Question: How are the depths and the transit generated?
% Answer: One spectrum of 160 piecewise-constant coefficients shared by both
% orders, an exact Keplerian transit averaged over each exposure at the
% geometry from NOVA's own white-light fit, and small penalised
% limb-darkening deviations around a STAGGER theory reference.
% Evidence: NOVA_SP_NOVA_S_SCIENCE_FREEZE_20260826_R1/reporting/
% v50_cycle2_reporting_inputs/shared_adaptive_observed_k160.json (basis type,
% widths, domains); configuration/variant_E12.json; reports/
% ONEOVERF_CAUSAL_INVESTIGATION_20260909.md (Delta bound); NOVA_SP_NOVA_S_
% SCIENCE_FREEZE_20260826_R1/code/project_sources/nonlinear_detector_sp_
% v0p1_20260720_src/nonlinear_sp/limb_darkening.py (lines 9-12, 935-965);
% reports/NOVA_LIMB_DARKENING_WORDING_CORRECTION_20260923.md; public Ahsoka
% S5_fit_par_wasp17_230320_nirissO1.epf (period); appendices/appendix_nova.tex.

Both orders see the same transmission spectrum $D(\lambda)$. It is
represented by $K=160$ piecewise-constant (top-hat) basis functions in
wavelength, which span 0.62 to 2.84~$\mu\mathrm{m}$ and are about
0.01~$\mu\mathrm{m}$ wide below 2~$\mu\mathrm{m}$ and about
0.04~$\mu\mathrm{m}$ wide above it, so that the depth at any wavelength is
the coefficient of the basis function which contains it. The coefficients
are parameterised as
\begin{equation}
  D_j = D_{\mathrm{global}}\,
  \frac{\exp\!\left[(\bm{H}_v\bm{v})_j\right]}
       {\sum_k w_k \exp\!\left[(\bm{H}_v\bm{v})_k\right]} ,
  \label{eq:depth-basis}
\end{equation}
where $D_{\mathrm{global}}$ is the achromatic depth, $\bm{v}$ are the free
shape coordinates, $\bm{H}_v$ maps them onto the subspace orthogonal to a
constant, and $w_k$ are fixed basis-width weights. This form separates the
achromatic depth from a chromatic shape whose weighted mean is zero, and it
keeps every depth positive. The two coefficients above
2.76~$\mu\mathrm{m}$ lie where the validated wavelength calibration gives no
response support, and so they are held constant, which leaves 158 free depth
coordinates. Together with the eight limb-darkening coordinates described
below, these form the nonlinear parameters of the fit. Apart from this
parameterisation the spectrum is left free, i.e.\ no atmospheric template,
no smoothing between neighbouring wavelengths and no prior on its shape is
imposed. Note also that no term allows the depths of the two orders to
differ. The earlier release contained such a term, $\Delta$, which offset
the two orders by $\pm\Delta$ and was disabled by bounding it to
$\pm10^{-12}$; at these small but nonzero values, however, the derivatives
of the penalty terms were not exactly those of a model without $\Delta$, and
so the term is now fixed at exactly zero.

The orbital state is
$\Omega=(P,\,a/R_\star,\,e,\,\omega_{\mathrm{peri}},\,b,\,t_0)$. The period
$P=3.73548546$~d is fixed at the value of the public Ahsoka configuration of
\citet{LouieEtAl2025}, and the orbit is taken to be circular, $e=0$, with
$\omega_{\mathrm{peri}}=90^\circ$ (which has no effect for a circular orbit,
so the value of zero listed for the earlier release in
Appendix~\ref{app:nova} is equivalent). The mid-transit time $t_0$, the
scaled semi-major axis $a/R_\star$ and the impact parameter $b$ come from
NOVA's white-light fit to the same input (Section~\ref{sec:own-white}), and
they are held fixed during the spectral fit. The limb-darkened transit is
computed with jaxoplanet \citep{Jaxoplanet2025}, which solves the Keplerian
orbit exactly and evaluates the occulted flux with a quadrature of order 10,
and $\mathcal{T}_{tg}$ is its average over each exposure, evaluated with 21
Gauss--Legendre samples per integration. The limb darkening follows the
quadratic law \citep{MandelAgol2002}, whose coefficients $u_1$ and $u_2$ are
written in terms of the coordinates $q_1$ and $q_2$ of \citet{Kipping2013},
which each lie between 0 and 1,
\begin{equation}
 u_1=2\sqrt{q_1}\,q_2, \qquad
 u_2=\sqrt{q_1}\,(1-2q_2).
 \label{eq:kipping-coordinates}
\end{equation}

In the spectral fit, the limb darkening may deviate from a theory reference,
but these deviations are penalised. The reference is computed from STAGGER
specific intensities \citep{MagicEtAl2015}, using the 6500~K plane of the
grid, which is the closest complete plane to the stellar effective
temperature of 6550~K, interpolated bilinearly in $\log g$ and
$[\mathrm{Fe}/\mathrm{H}]$ to $\log g=4.149$ and
$[\mathrm{Fe}/\mathrm{H}]=-0.25$. The reference file is fixed, and its
content hash is recorded so that exactly the same reference can be
identified later. Around this reference, the fit has one offset and one
slope in normalised log-wavelength for each coordinate and order, i.e.\
eight coordinates in total. These are penalised in two ways: for deviating
from the theory, with a scale of 0.5 in logit space, and for curvature in
wavelength, with a scale of $2.0\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{-2}$. Fine wavelength nodes
which fall outside the wavelength range of the theory grid are excluded, and
the theory is never extrapolated. Only the spectral fit uses this reference;
the white-light fit leaves the limb darkening free. The injector, by
contrast, computes its transit from MPS-ATLAS intensities
(Section~\ref{sec:per-read}), so this reference is independent of the limb
darkening of the injected signal.

\subsubsection{The empirical spatial response}

% Question: Where does q-star come from?
% Answer: The transit itself reveals where the starlight lands: a per-pixel
% Huber regression on a unit-depth transit template, with amplitudes
% normalised within each group and smoothed with a strength chosen by
% cross-fold prediction, rebuilt on every input.
% Evidence: products/original_v45_reference_qstar_r1_package/source/
% build_v47_empirical_template.py and aggregate_v47_qstar.py; products/
% original_A_group_calibration_adaptation_r1/build_nova_s_real_wasp17_responses_r1.py;
% products/w17_measured_production_recovery_20260923_v7_r1_package/recipes/qstar/
% source/run_original_A_current_input_qstar.py (tau); proposals/
% handoff_nova_s_20260828/01_EXACT_NOVA_S_METHOD.md section 6 (red-end bias).

The spatial response $q^\star$ is estimated from the data themselves.
Because starlight dims during the transit whilst the additive background
does not, the part of a pixel's time series which follows the transit
measures how much starlight lands on that pixel. Firstly, for each order
$o$, I build a template $\phi_o(t)$ of the shape of the transit. I select
the bright, stable pixels of the order, i.e.\ those with at least 95\% of
their out-of-transit samples valid, a positive baseline, and an
out-of-transit level above the 35th percentile of the stable pixels of that
order. For each selected pixel I compute its fractional departure from its
own linear out-of-transit baseline, and the template is the median of these
departures at each integration, with any linear trend removed and scaled so
that its deepest point is exactly $-1$, so that it carries the shape of the
transit but no information about its depth. Secondly, every pixel of the model support is
fitted, with Huber weights, as
\begin{equation}
 Y_{tp} = A_{pg}\,\phi_{o}(t) + \ell_p + \kappa_p\,\tau_t + \varepsilon_{tp},
 \label{eq:qstar-fit}
\end{equation}
where $A_{pg}$ is the transit-correlated amplitude of the pixel, $\ell_p$ is
its level, $\kappa_p\tau_t$ is a linear drift, $\tau_t$ is the time of
integration $t$ measured from the mean out-of-transit time in units of the
out-of-transit time span, and $\varepsilon_{tp}$ is the residual. The
response is the normalised amplitude,
\begin{equation*}
 q^\star_{pg}=\frac{A_{pg}}{\sum_{p'\in P_g}A_{p'g}},
\end{equation*}
so that $q^\star$ only describes how the light of a group is distributed over
its pixels, whilst the total brightness of the group is carried entirely by
the continuum $C_{tg}$.

The normalised amplitudes are smoothed along the detector columns, with a
robust second-difference penalty applied at a fixed position within the
trace, i.e.\ at a fixed rank of the pixel when the pixels of each group are
numbered by detector row. The strength of the smoothing is chosen by
cross-validation. The integrations are split into two alternating folds,
stratified across the transit phases, and the strength is the one for which
the response smoothed on one fold best predicts the unsmoothed response of
the other. The response used in the fit is built from all integrations with
this strength, and it is only accepted if the two fold estimates agree in
their flux distribution and centroid (Appendix~\ref{app:nova}). Negative
values are set to zero, and the response is renormalised within each group.

This approach has a known limitation. Because the smoothing works at a fixed
integer position relative to the trace, the centroid of the response
occasionally jumps, and at the faintest red columns the response is roughly
1--2\% too strong compared with the light that is actually delivered
(Appendix~\ref{app:nova}). Since the continuum sets the total brightness of
each group whilst $q^\star$ only sets its spatial profile, this excess acts
like a dilution, and it biases the recovered transit to be too shallow at
these columns.

\subsubsection{The continuum and the additive background}

% Question: How are the stellar continuum and the background modelled and
% calibrated?
% Answer: A level and slope per group for the continuum; eight fixed spatial
% maps with a constant and one temporal pattern for the residual background
% left after the detector processing, the temporal pattern and anchor
% recomputed on the off-trace pixels of each input.
% Evidence: build_e13b_basis_products.py (cluster-only); products/
% original_A_group_calibration_adaptation_r1/build_nova_s_real_wasp17_e13b_r1.py
% (per-integration fits, 244,657 safe pixels, SVD, reference transit);
% source/run_nova_original_A_group_detector.py (exoTEDRF background steps);
% reports/OFFTRACE_TRANSIT_COMPONENT_DIAGNOSIS_20260921.md section 2.

The continuum of each group is linear in time,
$C_{tg}=\beta_{0g}+\beta_{1g}\tau_t$, where $\beta_{0g}$ and $\beta_{1g}$
are its level and slope, which gives two coefficients for each of the
2{,}720 groups, i.e.\ 5{,}440 linear coefficients in total. The background is
modelled as a sum of eight fixed spatial maps $\Phi_{pk}$, each with a
constant amplitude and an amplitude which follows one temporal pattern
$G_1(t)$,
\begin{equation}
 B_{tp}=\sum_{k=1}^{8}\Phi_{pk}
 \left[\alpha_{k0}+\alpha_{k1}\,G_1(t)\right],
 \label{eq:background-model}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_{k0}$ and $\alpha_{k1}$ are the constant and time-varying
amplitudes of map $k$, sixteen coefficients in total. Note that the input
images have already had a scaled model of the zodiacal background subtracted
by the background steps of the detector processing
(Section~\ref{sec:detector-processing}), and so $B_{tp}$ describes the
background which remains after that subtraction, including any error in the
scaling of the model. The first map is a standardised map of the
calibration background and the other seven are low-order polynomial
surfaces, orthonormalised on the off-trace pixels. The maps were chosen by
blocked cross-validation on the off-trace pixels of the earlier release, and
are carried over unchanged.

All other parts of the background model are estimated from the off-trace
pixels of the input being analysed. Firstly, the eight maps are fitted by
weighted least squares to the 244{,}657 off-trace background pixels of each
integration separately, which gives a time series of eight coefficients.
The temporal pattern $G_1$ is then the first of the principal components of
this time series whose absolute correlation with the transit template (the
mean of $\phi_o$ over the two orders) is at most 0.25, which limits how much
transit-shaped variation the background can absorb. On the real visit, the
leading component itself follows the transit closely, and so this criterion
keeps it out of the background model; it returns in the uncertainty
ensemble (Section~\ref{sec:ensemble}). Secondly, one joint weighted fit of
all sixteen coefficients to every off-trace pixel of every integration gives
an expected coefficient vector $\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{off}}$ with precision
matrix $\bm{N}_{\mathrm{off}}$. These enter the objective as a soft anchor,
i.e.\ a quadratic penalty on the background coefficients, and the background
is refitted together with the continuum at every step, so that no background
is held fixed within the fit. The objective contains no Gaussian-process
term, and outliers are down-weighted by the Huber weights described below.

\subsubsection{The source-curvature factor and detector uncertainties}

% Question: What are gamma_t and sigma_tp?
% Answer: An out-of-transit curvature correction of fixed common form,
% recomputed on each input, and per-sample uncertainties scaled per order
% and per pixel from two-way out-of-transit cross-validation.
% Evidence: products/original_A_group_calibration_adaptation_r1/
% build_nova_s_real_wasp17_source_curvature_r1.py; config/
% CURVATURE_POLICY_AUTHORIZATION_20260919.json; reports/
% SOURCE_FREEZE_EXECUTION_AUTHORIZATION_20260919.md; NOVA_SP_NOVA_S_SCIENCE_FREEZE_
% 20260826_R1/code/runtime/N_SCALE_CONTRACT.json (outside the execution root);
% reports/offtrace_transit_diagnosis_20260921/sources/v47_oot_guard.py and
% original_A_group_nova_binding.py (per-pixel scale, response-weighted N).

The factor $\gamma_t$ corrects for slow curvature in the brightness of the
star over the visit. To build it, the background-subtracted flux of the
stable trace pixels of each order, i.e.\ those which are valid in every
out-of-transit integration, is summed in each out-of-transit integration,
and the resulting light curve is fitted with a weighted quadratic in time.
The ratio of this quadratic to its straight-line (affine) approximation,
normalised to unit mean out of transit, describes only the curvature of the
stellar flux, since the linear trend is already carried by the continuum.
The factor used by NOVA is the geometric mean of the factors of the two
orders. This form, a single factor common to both orders, was chosen on the
earlier release by comparing its held-out $\chi^2$ on two folds with that of
no correction and of a separate factor for each order
(Appendix~\ref{app:nova}), and it is carried over.

On each input, the factor itself is recomputed from that input's
out-of-transit integrations, and two further factors are computed from two
alternating folds of these integrations (175 integrations each for the real
visit). The factor is only accepted if the means of the two fold factors
over the in-transit integrations differ by at most 250~ppm, and if its
largest deviation from unity is at most 1000~ppm. For the real visit, the
two fold factors must also be correlated with a coefficient of at least
0.95. For the realisations of the uncertainty ensemble
(Section~\ref{sec:ensemble}) this correlation is only reported. This rule was
changed after the correlation was found to reject realisations whose
corrections were only 1--15~ppm, since two negligible corrections of
opposite sign in the two folds are strongly anticorrelated even when both
amplitude limits are met. The factor multiplies only the stellar term of
Equation~\ref{eq:nova-forward} and adds no fitted parameters.

The uncertainty of each sample is
\begin{equation*}
 \sigma_{tp}=\mathrm{ERR}_{tp}\,N_{o}\,s_{p},
\end{equation*}
where $\mathrm{ERR}_{tp}$ is the calibrated uncertainty array from the
pipeline, and $N_o$ is a per-order scale carried over from the earlier
release, equal to 3.07 for order 1 and 4.19 for order 2
(Appendix~\ref{app:nova}); pixels which belong to both orders take the mean
of the two, weighted by the response of each order. The factor $s_p\geq1$
is a scale for each pixel, estimated by two-way cross-validation on the
out-of-transit integrations of each input. These are split into two
alternating folds, a level and a slope in time are fitted to each pixel on
one fold and used to predict the other, and vice versa, and $s_p$ is set by
the scatter of the standardised prediction residuals, with a minimum of
one. Pixels whose out-of-transit residuals exceed their nominal
uncertainties are therefore down-weighted. These scales only set the
weights of the fit; the uncertainty of the recovered spectrum comes from the
ensemble of Section~\ref{sec:ensemble}.

\subsubsection{The complete objective}

% Question: What does NOVA minimise?
% Answer: A Huber detector mismatch over the retained mask plus the
% off-trace background anchor and the limb-darkening penalties.
% Evidence: NOVA_SP_NOVA_S_SCIENCE_FREEZE_20260826_R1/code/sparse_varpro/
% production_irls.py and code/project_sources/nonlinear_detector_sp_v0p3_
% huber_discrepancy_factorial_20260722_r4_src/nonlinear_sp_huber_factorial/
% robust.py (HUBER_DELTA 1.345), both outside the execution root.

Writing the standardised residual as
\begin{equation}
 r_{tp}=\frac{Y_{tp}-\widehat Y_{tp}
 (D,\bm{u},\bm{\beta},\bm{\alpha};\,q^\star,\Omega)}{\sigma_{tp}},
 \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
where the fitted parameters appear before the semicolon and the fixed inputs
after it, the NOVA estimate is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
 &(\widehat D,\widehat{\bm{u}},\widehat{\bm{\beta}},
  \widehat{\bm{\alpha}})
 = \arg\min\Big\{ \\
 &\quad\sum_{(t,p)\in\mathcal{M}}\rho_{1.345}(r_{tp}) \\
 &\quad+\tfrac{1}{2}(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{off}})^{\mathsf T}
   \bm{N}_{\mathrm{off}}(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{off}}) \\
 &\quad+\tfrac{1}{2}\|\bm{r}_{\mathrm{LD,theory}}(\bm{u})\|_2^2
  +\tfrac{1}{2}\|\bm{r}_{\mathrm{LD,curv}}(\bm{u})\|_2^2
 \Big\},
\end{split}
 \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{M}$ is the mask of retained samples, $\rho_{1.345}$ is the
Huber loss, which is quadratic for $|r_{tp}|\leq1.345$ and linear beyond
(Appendix~\ref{app:nova}), and $\bm{r}_{\mathrm{LD,theory}}$ and
$\bm{r}_{\mathrm{LD,curv}}$ are the deviations of the limb darkening from
theory and its curvatures in wavelength, each divided by its scale. The first
term measures the mismatch between the model and the detector data, the
second is the soft anchor on the background, and the last two are the
limb-darkening penalties.

\subsubsection{The nested solver}

% Question: How is the objective minimised, and what counts as convergence?
% Answer: Huber weights outside, bounded trust-region steps in the middle,
% an exact linear solve inside; acceptance requires convergence checks on
% two fixed summary grids; among eligible starts the lowest objective wins.
% Evidence: NOVA_SP_NOVA_S_SCIENCE_FREEZE_20260826_R1/code/sparse_varpro/
% production_irls.py (_rms_change, common40/legacy_f2 gates) and code/runtime/
% STRICT_IRLS_POLICY.json (outside the execution root); products/
% w17_measured_production_recovery_20260923_v7_r1_package/recipes/closure/source/
% close_causal_reduced_starts.py (selection rule); reports/
% PHYSICAL_ENDPOINT_THIRD_START_20260918.md; reports/
% PHYSICAL_INJECTOR_PROTOCOL_20260918.md section 4.

The parameters split into a nonlinear block $\bm{\theta}=(D,\bm{u})$ and a
linear block $(\bm{\beta},\bm{\alpha})$, and the objective is minimised in
three nested loops. In the innermost loop, with $\bm{\theta}$ and the
weights held fixed, variable projection \citep{GolubPereyra1973} solves the
linear problem exactly,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
 (\widehat{\bm{\beta}},\widehat{\bm{\alpha}})=
 \arg\min_{\bm{\beta},\bm{\alpha}}\;
 &\frac{1}{2}\sum_{(t,p)\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{w_{tp}}{\sigma_{tp}^2}
 \left[Y_{tp}-\widehat Y_{tp}\right]^2 \\
 &+\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{off}})^{\mathsf T}
   \bm{N}_{\mathrm{off}}(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{off}}),
\end{split}
 \label{eq:varpro}
\end{equation}
by factorising the sparse continuum block and reducing the coupling to the
background to a sixteen-dimensional Schur complement. The Jacobian of the
profiled residual with respect to $\bm{\theta}$ is obtained by
differentiating through this exact inner solution, so that it includes the
response of the continuum and background coefficients to a change in
$\bm{\theta}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:nova}). In the middle loop, bounded
trust-region reflective least squares \citep{BranchColemanLi1999} minimises
the profiled objective over $\bm{\theta}$. In the outer loop, the Huber
weights \citep{Huber1964} of Equation~\ref{eq:varpro} are updated as
$w_{tp}=\min(1,\,1.345/|r_{tp}|)$, and they are held fixed during each
trust-region solve.

A fit is only accepted once the weights and the objective have stopped
changing and the spectrum has converged. For the latter, the spectrum is
mapped onto two fixed summary grids inherited from the earlier release, one
of 40 bins and one of 96 bins, and the root-mean-square change between
successive outer iterations must be below 0.01~ppm on the first and
0.05~ppm on the second. In addition, first-order optimality must be
satisfied, a confirmation fit with the recomputed final weights must
reproduce the solution, and a final check must find no significant decrease
of the objective along the scaled Cauchy and Gauss--Newton directions
(Appendix~\ref{app:nova}).

To check that the solution does not depend on where the optimiser starts,
every analysis is run from at least two starting points. The first starts
from the white-light depth with a flat spectral shape and the theory limb
darkening, whilst the second adds a fixed 250~ppm perturbation to the
spectral shape and small offsets to the limb darkening. I refer to starts
which pass all of the checks above as strict. For the pilot recoveries of
the injector (Section~\ref{sec:envelope}), the validation protocol adds a
third start at the midpoint of the first two, a rule which uses no
information from the injected spectrum. Among the strict starts, the one
with the lowest Huber objective is selected, and the agreement between the
starts is reported but is not itself a condition.

\subsubsection{White-light geometry inference}
\label{sec:own-white}

% Question: Where do t0, a/R* and b come from?
% Answer: From NOVA's own joint two-order white-light fit on optimally
% weighted curves built from its own detector data, sampled with nested
% sampling through public Eureka!, accepted by a grazing-mass rule (the fifth
% version, fixed before calibration but not blind to the real posterior),
% and handed over as whole-posterior weighted medians.
% Evidence: source/nova_own_white_eureka.py, source/
% nova_own_white_nested_20260922_r5.py, source/nova_own_white_geometry.py,
% source/v7_grazing_statistic_20260923.py; evidence/
% group_ownwhite_native_sources_r1/white_light.py (build_order_white_light_curves);
% config/V7_GEOMETRY_GATE_REGISTRATION_20260923.json (substantive revision 5,
% real grazing mass known zero before registration); reports/
% V7_CALIBRATION_RESULT_20260923.md; public Ahsoka WhiteLightCurve_Fits epf files
% (k and noise priors).

The spectral fit holds the transit geometry fixed, and so this geometry must
first be estimated. Before the spectral fit, NOVA therefore infers $t_0$,
$a/R_\star$ and the inclination $i$ from the same detector data, and hence
the impact parameter $b=(a/R_\star)\cos i$ for a circular orbit, without
using any value from another pipeline or from the injector. For each order,
the white-light flux of an integration is the sum over detector columns of a
profile-weighted (optimal) amplitude, computed from the retained samples of
the column with an out-of-transit spatial profile built on the same input;
pixels which belong to both orders are left out of both curves. The two
curves are fitted jointly with the light-curve stage of the public Eureka!
package \citep{BellEtAl2022}, as two channels which share $t_0$, $a/R_\star$
and $i$, whilst each order has its own radius ratio $k_o$, two quadratic
limb-darkening coefficients $u_1$ and $u_2$ \citep{MandelAgol2002}, a linear
baseline and a white-noise multiplier; $P$, $e$ and $\omega_{\mathrm{peri}}$
are fixed as above.

The priors on the geometry are wide: $t_0$ is uniform over the observed
window, $a/R_\star$ is uniform on $[1.01,\,100]$ and $i$ is uniform on
$[0^\circ,\,90^\circ]$. The other priors are copied from the public Ahsoka
white-light configuration of \citet{LouieEtAl2025} and use no fit to these
data: $k_o$ is normal with mean 0.1255 and standard deviation 0.085, $u_1$
and $u_2$ are each uniform on $[0,1]$, the baseline level and slope are
normal about 1 and 0 with standard deviations 0.05 and 0.01, and the noise
multiplier is normal with mean 1.4 and standard deviation 2.0. The posterior
is sampled with dynesty \citep{Speagle2020} through Eureka!, using 2{,}048
live points, multi-ellipsoid bounds and random-walk proposals, and sampling
stops when the estimated contribution of the remaining prior volume to the
evidence, $\Delta\ln Z$, falls below 0.01. I use nested sampling for every
white-light fit because, on the inputs injected at the raw level, the
affine-invariant ensemble sampler emcee \citep{ForemanMackeyEtAl2013} became
stranded: about 12\% of the posterior mass of the failed chains lay on
grazing or non-transiting geometries, and the chains did not reach the
required 50 autocorrelation lengths.

Before a white-light fit is used, it must pass an acceptance rule which
tests directly for this failure, by measuring how much of the posterior lies
on grazing or non-transiting geometries. For every posterior sample, $b$ is
computed from that sample's $a/R_\star$ and $i$, and the sample counts as
grazing or non-transiting if
\begin{equation}
 b > 1-\max(k_1,\,k_2).
 \label{eq:grazing}
\end{equation}
All samples are kept, each with its nested-sampling weight. The grazing mass
is the weighted fraction of grazing samples, and its central 95\% interval is
estimated from 200 bootstrap resamplings of the nested-sampling run, made
with dynesty's own resampling; the half-width of this interval must be at
most 0.01. If no sample with positive weight is grazing, the upper end of the
interval is instead set to $\min(1,\,3/\lfloor N_{\mathrm{ESS}}\rfloor)$,
where $N_{\mathrm{ESS}}$ is the Kish effective sample size; this ``rule of
three'' is only an approximation here, since nested samples are not
independent. A fit passes if the upper end of the interval is below 1\%, and
passes with its grazing mass reported if it lies between 1 and 5\%. It fails
if the upper end is 5\% or more, if the sampler did not reach its stopping
criterion, or if the interval is too wide. A failed fit is recorded as a
failure: the run is not continued, reseeded or trimmed, and no geometry from
elsewhere is used in its place.

This rule is the fifth revision of the acceptance criterion for the
white-light geometry. It was fixed before it was calibrated, but after the
earlier versions had been abandoned and after the real posterior was known
to contain no grazing samples of positive weight, so it is not blind to the
real data. It was then tested on saved posteriors: it had to reject the
three emcee chains which had failed their length assessment, whose grazing
masses were about 12\%, and to accept both the chains which had passed it
and the nested posterior of the real visit.

When a fit is accepted, the weighted medians of $t_0$, $a/R_\star$, $i$ and
each $k_o$ over the whole posterior are passed to the spectral fit, and $b$
is computed from the medians of $a/R_\star$ and $i$. The white-light depth of
each order is $10^6k_o^2$~ppm, and the spectral fit starts from the mean of
the two. Diagnostics of multimodality and of the posterior tails are also
reported, although they play no part in acceptance.

\subsubsection{The uncertainty ensemble}
\label{sec:ensemble}

% Question: How are NOVA's spectral uncertainties obtained and validated?
% Answer: By refitting from scratch on realisations built from the accepted
% real model plus resampled real residual blocks; the ensemble errors define
% a finite-ensemble Student likelihood that must pass held-out coverage.
% Evidence: config/MATCHED_COVARIANCE_ENSEMBLE_R1.json; products/
% generator_v2_20260923_v7_r2_package (REGISTRY.json, source/
% generator_v2_draw_20260922.py, pilot_noise.py circular_oot_schedule); source/
% finite_ensemble_likelihood.py; source/covariance_validation.py; config/
% COVARIANCE_ANALYSIS_PROTOCOL_R1.json; reports/COVARIANCE_VALIDATION_RESULT_20260921.md;
% evidence/reviewer_plan_reporting_rank_20260920_r1.json (rank 128).

The profiled normal equations give a covariance for the spectrum, but only
conditional on one fixed set of calibration products. I therefore estimate
the spectral uncertainty of NOVA by repeating the whole analysis on
simulated realisations of the real visit,
\begin{equation}
 Y^{(n)}_{tp}=\widehat Y^{\mathrm{ref}}_{tp}+\bar r_p+a_p\,\psi_t
 +\mathrm{ERR}_{tp}\,z_{d_n(t),\,p},
 \label{eq:ensemble-draw}
\end{equation}
where $n$ labels the realisation, $\widehat Y^{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the accepted
final prediction for the real visit on the trace pixels, together with the
fitted mean background off the trace, and $\bar r_p$ is the mean, in each
pixel, of the real out-of-transit residual, which is added back so that any
static mismatch between the model and the real data is also present in the
realisations. The term $a_p\psi_t$ returns the leading off-trace temporal
component, which the correlation criterion of the background model keeps out
of the fit because it resembles the transit: $a_p$ is its amplitude in each
pixel, obtained by projecting the component onto the transit decrement of
the accepted white-light fit, $\psi_t=\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{w}}_t-1$, which
is exactly zero outside the transit. The last term is the real ERR array
multiplied by a centred, standardised out-of-transit residual $z$ taken from
a donor integration $d_n(t)$.

The donors are whole frames, drawn in blocks of 64 consecutive
integrations. Each block starts at a randomly chosen out-of-transit
integration and continues within the same out-of-transit interval,
$[0,230)$ or $[600,720)$, wrapping round to the start of that interval if
necessary, so that no block spans the transit, and blocks are placed one
after another until all 720 integrations are filled. The same donor schedule
is used for the trace and the off-trace pixels. Each realisation keeps the
real ERR and data-quality arrays, and any sample which becomes invalid in the
draw is also flagged.

The ensemble contains 352 realisations, each with a fixed random seed and a
fixed role. 256 are used to estimate the covariance and 32 are held out to
validate it, whilst two further sets of 32 reuse the held-out seeds with
blocks of 128 and 192 integrations, to test how sensitive the result is to
the block length. Such a block, if drawn from the 120-integration
interval $[600,720)$, wraps round and repeats some of its frames. Each
realisation is analysed from scratch, with a fresh spatial response,
background pattern and anchor, curvature factor and uncertainty scales, its
own nested white-light fit under the rule of Section~\ref{sec:own-white},
and two strict starts. No failed realisation is replaced or dropped: the
covariance is only formed once all 256 estimation and 32 held-out
realisations have completed, and a held-out realisation which failed would
count as not covered in the validation below.

The error of a realisation is its recovered spectrum minus the reference
spectrum, i.e.\ the accepted NOVA spectrum of the real visit from which
$\widehat Y^{\mathrm{ref}}$ was built. The 147 reported bins are produced
from the 160 native coefficients by the fixed linear map of
Section~\ref{sec:reporting}. Below 1~$\mu\mathrm{m}$ and above
2~$\mu\mathrm{m}$ the reporting bins are narrower than the native basis
functions, so this map has rank 128, and the bin errors contain only
$N_{\mathrm{m}}=128$ independent modes; I express each error in these modes.
From the $N=256$ estimation errors, I form the sample mean $\bm{\mu}$ and the
unbiased sample covariance $\bm{S}$ directly, without shrinkage or jitter and
keeping all 128 modes. Because $\bm{S}$ is itself estimated from a finite
number of realisations, a Gaussian likelihood with this covariance would be
too narrow. For the likelihood of a new error $\bm{x}$ I therefore use the
multivariate Student distribution of \citet{SellentinHeavens2016}, with an
additional factor $1+1/N$ because the mean $\bm{\mu}$ is also estimated,
i.e.\ a distribution centred on $\bm{\mu}$ with $N-N_{\mathrm{m}}$ degrees
of freedom and scale matrix
\begin{equation}
 \bm{\Sigma}=\left(1+\frac{1}{N}\right)\frac{N-1}{N-N_{\mathrm{m}}}\,\bm{S},
 \label{eq:finite-ensemble}
\end{equation}
so that $(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})^{\mathsf T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})/
N_{\mathrm{m}}$ follows an $F(N_{\mathrm{m}},\,N-N_{\mathrm{m}})$
distribution.

The likelihood is validated on the 32 held-out realisations. A held-out error
is covered if its statistic lies below the 95th percentile of this $F$
distribution, and the validation fails if a one-sided exact binomial test
rejects 95\% coverage at a significance level of 0.025, which happens when
27 or fewer of the 32 are covered. In addition, the Mardia skewness and
kurtosis of the 256 estimation errors are compared with those of 512
simulated Gaussian ensembles of the same size, and a departure at the same
significance level is reported as a warning. The validation is run once all
352 realisations have finished, and the likelihood is only used for
atmospheric retrieval if it passes.

\subsection{The injector}
\label{sec:injector}

% Question: How is a truth-known benchmark built from real JWST data?
% Answer: A known transit signal is written into the raw group ramps of real
% pre-transit integrations, dimming only modelled target starlight, so that
% detector processing, background and noise are real and the truth is known.
% Evidence: source/emit_original_A_group_segment.py, source/
% original_v45_group_signal.py, source/group_accumulated_transport.py;
% evidence/matrix_trip_bindings_r2.json; reports/rate_native_pilot_preparation_20260921/
% ENTRY_STAGE_DECISION.md (pilot input).

The injector addresses the validation problem of
Section~\ref{sec:detector-to-validation}. Real observations come with no
ground truth, and so I create one by writing a transit with a known spectrum
into real JWST data. The transit is written into the raw detector ramps,
before any calibration, so that each recovery method can run its own
detector processing, including its own group-level $1/f$ correction, on the
same injected frames. The injector is used in two stages. In the pilot, a
few injected cases are recovered to test the injector and to measure how
sensitive the recovered spectra are to its assumptions; in production, a
larger set of atmospheres is injected into each of six visits and recovered
by every method (Section~\ref{sec:envelope}). The subsections below describe,
in turn, the real data which carry the signal, which light is dimmed, how the
transit is computed and written into the raw counts, the detector processing,
the checks that the signal was delivered, and how the recoveries are run,
compared and scored.

\subsubsection{Baseline and null}
\label{sec:baseline}

% Question: Which real data carry the injected signal, and what is the
% control?
% Answer: The first 229 raw integrations of the WASP-17 visit, all before
% the real transit; the null is the same frames through the same processing
% with nothing injected, and a rate-level control adds the final-read change
% after ramp fitting.
% Evidence: evidence/matrix_trip_bindings_r2.json (baseline_provenance);
% reports/PHYSICAL_MATCHED_NATIVE_NULL_20260918.md; products/
% w17_measured_raw_emission_20260923_v7_r3_prepared/source/
% emit_conditional_matched_rate_20260920.py and fixed_primary_family_signal_20260920.py
% (final cumulative read change divided by its accumulated seconds, flat of ones).

The injected transit needs real data which contain no transit of their own.
For this I use the raw, group-level NIRISS/SOSS observation of WASP-17
(program 1353, observation 101, visit 1), restricted to its first 229
integrations (numbered 0 to 228), which I refer to as the baseline; they are
stored in two raw segments of 178 and 51 integrations. The real transit,
together with buffer integrations on either side of it, occupies
integrations 230 to 599 of the 720-integration visit, so every baseline
integration was taken before the real transit. Each integration consists of
eight nondestructive reads of the $256\times2048$ subarray, with one frame per
group, a group time of 5.494~s and no dropped frames, so the accumulated
exposure times run from 5.494 to 43.952~s.

I also pass the same 229 raw integrations, with nothing injected, through the
identical detector processing, and refer to this as the null. As a control,
the signal is also injected at the rate level, i.e.\ after ramp fitting: the
count change accumulated by the final read, divided by its accumulated
exposure time of 43.952~s, is added to the calibrated rate images of the
null. Since these images have already been flat-fielded, the flat-field
factor which returns the raw-level signal to raw counts
(Section~\ref{sec:transport}) is omitted, and the uncertainty and quality
arrays of the null are left untouched. The difference between the raw-level
and rate-level recoveries measures what the group-level processing does to
the injected signal, together with the effect of adding the signal as a
final-read average instead of through the ramp: in integrations in which the
transit changes, a ramp fit to the injected reads does not return exactly
this average.

\subsubsection{Which light is dimmed}
\label{sec:dimmed-light}

% Question: Which photons transit?
% Answer: A map of modelled target starlight in three zones by distance from
% the traces: a fixed core, measured wings, and a far region injected at a
% fraction of the measured residual light, with two bracketing members and a
% measured per-column member.
% Evidence: products/light_based_family_20260921_r1/FAMILY.json and
% FAR_PIXEL_ACCOUNTING.json; products/full_light_outer_shape_controls_20260916_r1/
% SOURCE_ESTIMATE.json; products/full_photon_source_projection_diagnostic_r1/
% SOURCE_PROJECTION.npz and job 4015158 (core); products/measured_per_column_20260922_r1/
% REGISTRATION.json (depth definition, upper bound pending pre-1/f);
% source/freeze_measured_columns_20260922.py; reports/
% EVENING_FAR_LIGHT_AND_WHITE_CHECKS_20260921.md (safe pixels); products/
% w17_measured_raw_emission_20260923_v7_r3_prepared/source/
% fixed_primary_family_signal_20260920.py; reports/ENDPOINT_HOLD_VERIFICATION_20260920.md.

In a real transit only the light of the target star is dimmed, and so the
injector needs a map of where on the detector the starlight of the target
falls. This map, which I call the source, gives the count rate of the
target in each pixel, in $\mathrm{DN\,s^{-1}}$ at a reference time, and it is
divided into three zones according to the distance $d$ of each pixel from
the nearer of the order-1 and order-2 traces. In the core, $d\leq16$ pixels
(119{,}277 pixels), the source is a projection of the stellar spectrum
through the public ATOCA spectral-trace model \citep{DarveauBernierEtAl2022},
with a wing correction, a per-column flux normalisation and a per-pixel
out-of-transit response calibrated on the same data; this projection was made
earlier in the project and is reused exactly as it was. In the wings,
$16<d\leq80$ pixels (257{,}554 eligible pixels), the source is the
out-of-transit light measured on the same data after background subtraction,
estimated without smoothing in bands of 64 columns and separately for odd and
even rows. Because background subtraction leaves some pixels negative, this
estimate is constrained to be nonnegative, whilst the signed total of each
band is preserved. In the far region, $d>80$ pixels (121{,}811 eligible
pixels), the source is the light which remains after background
subtraction. Whether this light belongs to the target has not been
established, so it is injected at a fraction $m$ of the measured field.

This defines a family of injectors which differ only in $m$. Two members
bracket the possibilities, one which injects none of the far light ($m=0$)
and one which injects all of it ($m=1$). A third, midpoint member ($m=0.5$)
is only used in the delivery check. Far pixels with no measured light
(33{,}758) receive nothing in every member.

A fourth member uses a measured far-light fraction. The idea is that far
light which belongs to the star dims by the same fraction as the star during
the transit, whereas the sky background does not, so if all of the far-light
dimming came from starlight, the ratio of the far-light dip to the transit
depth would equal the stellar share of that light. On the final calibrated
images of the full real visit, the mean signal of the 102{,}308 far pixels
which lie inside the pipeline's background mask drops by 0.834\% during the
real transit, with all of these pixels weighted equally. The measurement
uses 350 out-of-transit and 315 in-transit integrations, and excludes 55
buffer integrations. In each detector column, the dip is divided by the mean
fractional decrement of the accepted real white-light model over the same
315 integrations, averaged over the two orders (0.013629); a later refit of
the white-light curve changes this value by only $3\times10^{-7}$, which
leaves the member unchanged. The same achromatic value is used for every
column. The ratio is averaged over a 128-column window, using only the
1{,}884 columns with a direct measurement, and bounded to $[0,1]$. The
resulting multiplier $m_x$, where $x$ denotes the detector column, has a
minimum of 0.103, a median of 0.606 and a maximum of 1, with 84 columns at
the upper bound, and in total this member injects 61.5\% of the measured far
light. Because $m_x$ is measured after the $1/f$ correction of the pipeline,
which may leave an imprint on the statistic measured in the far rows, it is
treated as an upper bound on the stellar fraction of the far light until the
measurement can be repeated before that correction, and not as a measurement
of that fraction. The 12{,}808 pixels flagged in the F277W exposure, a direct
image which is used to locate contaminating field sources, keep their
modelled target light but are excluded from the empirical estimates, and the
10{,}208 reference pixels receive no light.

Each zone also needs a description of how its light is distributed in
wavelength and between the two orders. The core keeps the wavelength and
order kernels of its original projection, whilst the wings and the far region
take their mixture from a separate model of the outer light, rescaled pixel
by pixel to the measured map, so that the outer light has its own spectrum
and rate of change in time whilst its brightness is set entirely by the
measured map. As a check, the two components are projected separately and
summed, and the sum must reproduce the source map to a relative precision of
$5\times10^{-13}$ (an absolute precision of $10^{-10}\,\mathrm{DN\,s^{-1}}$).
In the core, the stellar spectrum $S(\lambda,\tau)$, where $\tau$ is time,
varies linearly between two calibrated endpoint spectra, and outside the
calibration interval it is held at the endpoint value, which only affects
the reads of integration~0. The outer component also varies linearly in
time, but its two endpoints are defined at the start and the end of the full
interval covered by the reads.

\subsubsection{The per-read transit signal}
\label{sec:per-read}

% Question: How is the transit computed?
% Answer: With ExoTETHyS TRIP on MPS-ATLAS intensities (official routines,
% with a row-wise occulted-flux step regression-tested bit for bit), averaged
% over each of the eight reset-to-read windows of every integration, at the
% control-file orbit with a/R* rescaled so that the transit fits the baseline.
% Evidence: products/original_A_causal_validation_trip_reference_r1_package/
% source/run_original_v45_group_trip.py (official_fast_trip_regression,
% final-read check against integration-level reference); source/
% B_mixed_native_trip.py (integration-level tables, 5e-13 edge gate); evidence/
% matrix_trip_bindings_r2.json (geometry: target_T14, b retained); reports/
% ENDPOINT_NULL_ROUNDOFF_CONSULTATION_20260920.json (three audited packets).

The injected spectrum $D(\lambda)$ is defined on the injector's own
wavelength grid of 15{,}326 nodes, and the radius ratio at each node is
$\sqrt{D(\lambda)}$. The fraction of the stellar flux which remains visible,
$T(\lambda,\tau)$, is computed with the TRIP routine of ExoTETHyS
\citep{MorelloEtAl2020} using 20{,}000 annuli. The injector calls the public
ExoTETHyS code for the stellar intensity profile and the total stellar flux,
and replaces only the calculation of the occulted flux with a version which
evaluates the same expression one planet position at a time; before every
run, a regression test requires this version to reproduce the public routine
bit for bit. The stellar intensities come from the MPS-ATLAS grid (set~1)
\citep{KostogryzEtAl2022,KostogryzEtAl2023} for $T_{\mathrm{eff}}=6550$~K,
$\log g=4.149$ and $[\mathrm{M}/\mathrm{H}]=-0.25$.

The injected orbit keeps the period, the circular orbit and the impact
parameter ($b\simeq0.339$) of the public control file of the published
WASP-17~b analysis \citep{LouieEtAl2025}. The real transit of WASP-17~b,
however, lasts about 4.3~h and would not fit into the 229-integration
baseline, and so $a/R_\star$ and the inclination are rescaled, with $b$ held
fixed, until the transit lasts about 1.74~h, from the start of
integration~51 to the end of integration~177, with mid-transit at the centre
of this span. None of the recovery methods receives these values.

Each read $n$ of integration $t$ records the charge accumulated since the
detector was reset, and so it sees the transit averaged over the window from
the reset to the end of that read. The transit is averaged over each of these
eight nested windows by Gauss--Legendre quadrature, using the same window
for every pixel, and grids of 41 and 61 nodes must agree to better than
1~ppm; the 61-node result is used. The results are stored in precomputed
transit tables, which hold, for each integration, read and wavelength node,
the window mean $\langle T\rangle_{tn}(\lambda)$ and the first time moment
about the integration midpoint $\tau_t$,
$\mu_{tn}(\lambda)=\langle(\tau-\tau_t)(T-1)\rangle_{tn}$; the moment is
needed because the stellar source itself changes during the integration
(Section~\ref{sec:transport}).

These tables are checked against independently computed tables of the
transit averaged over each whole integration: the final read window of each
integration must reproduce the integration average to within
$10^{-6}$~ppm, and in the integration-averaged tables the first and last
integrations must be unocculted to within $5\times10^{-13}$. Integration~0,
the only integration whose read windows extend beyond the calibration
interval of the stellar spectrum, must carry no transit signal at all, i.e.\
its remaining fraction must be exactly 1 and its time moment exactly zero.
In three tables from an earlier stage of the project, a round-off error of
$2\times10^{-16}$ in this fraction was verified to be harmless and set to
exactly 1; in new tables, any such departure is recorded as a failure. On the
229-integration baseline, the transit of an atmospheric spectrum reduces the
flux in integrations 50 to 178 (129 integrations) and leaves the other 100
unaffected, whilst the grey signal used in the delivery check affects the
narrower range 51 to 177 (127 integrations).

\subsubsection{Transport to raw counts}
\label{sec:transport}

% Question: How does the signal enter the raw ramps?
% Answer: As an accumulated count change per read, projected to the
% detector, returned to unflattened counts, and written as floats through the
% inverse of the pipeline's linearity correction via an affine bridge.
% Evidence: source/original_v45_group_signal.py; source/
% group_accumulated_transport.py; config/DEVELOPMENT_GROUP_TRANSPORT_R1.json
% (tolerances); NOVA_SP_MULTIVISIT_SCENE_ROBUSTNESS_20260826_R1/source/
% stage3b_detector_transport_r2.py (affine bridge, step sequence); evidence/
% exo250_raw_configuration_bindings_20260923_r1.json (scale 1.0, flat range);
% stage3b_float_sci_persistence_adapter_r2.py (float writing, protected masks).

For a source which is linear in time, the count change accumulated by read
$n$ of integration $t$ at wavelength node $\lambda$ is the integral of
$S\,(T-1)$ over the read window, and its projection onto the detector gives
the change in the counts of pixel $p$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
 \Delta N_{tn}(\lambda) = {}& s_n\big[S_t(\lambda)\,
 \big(\langle T\rangle_{tn}(\lambda)-1\big) \\
 &\quad +\dot S(\lambda)\,\mu_{tn}(\lambda)\big], \\
 \Delta Y^{\mathrm{raw}}_{tnp} = {}& f_p
 \sum_{o}\big[\bm{A}_o\,\Delta N_{tn}\big]_p ,
\end{split}
 \label{eq:v45-injector}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta N_{tn}(\lambda)$ is the count change at node $\lambda$ before
projection, $\Delta Y^{\mathrm{raw}}_{tnp}$ is the resulting change in the
counts of pixel $p$, $s_n=5.494\,n$~s is the accumulated exposure time of
read $n$, which is the same in every integration, $S_t$ is the stellar
spectrum at the midpoint of the integration, $\dot S$ is its rate of change,
$\langle T\rangle_{tn}$ and $\mu_{tn}$ are the window mean and time moment
defined above, $\bm{A}_o$ is the detector projection of order $o$, and $f_p$
is the flat-field coefficient of pixel $p$. The projection $\bm{A}_o$
combines the sparse spectral-response matrix of the public ATOCA kernel, a
wing correction, a per-column normalisation and a per-pixel out-of-transit
response calibrated on the data. The equation is evaluated separately for
the core and the outer components, each with its own spectrum and
projection, and the results are summed. The projection gives the loss in
flat-fielded counts, and multiplying by $f_p$, taken from the pipeline
reference file \texttt{jwst\_niriss\_flat\_0275}, converts it back to raw
counts; over the 507{,}606 pixels of the two-order support, $f_p$ ranges from
0.2021 to 1.5027, and the quality flags of the reference file are left in
place. The count change is zero on the reference pixels and must be
nonpositive in every pixel, since a transit can only remove light; otherwise
the injection stops.

The change $\Delta Y^{\mathrm{raw}}$ is defined in linearised counts,
whereas the raw ramp records counts before the linearity correction, and so
it has to be passed through the inverse of that correction. To do this, the
raw counts are first carried to the input of the pipeline's linearity step,
i.e.\ through its data-quality initialisation, saturation, superbias and
reference-pixel steps. Since the superbias and reference-pixel corrections
act on each sample as fixed additive offsets, this bridge is affine, and its
offsets are computed once from a pair of raw and pre-linearity frames of the
baseline; it remains valid because the injection never changes the reference
pixels. Newton's method is then used to solve the pipeline's linearity
polynomial for the raw count whose linearised value equals the original
linearised value plus $\Delta Y^{\mathrm{raw}}$, and the inverse bridge maps
the result back to the raw file. The modified ramps are written as
floating-point values, so the injected change is not rounded to integer DN.
The bridge must reproduce the input of the pipeline's linearity step to
within 0.015625~DN, the delivered change must match the requested change to
within the same tolerance, and the linearity polynomial must be inverted to
within $10^{-8}$~DN. The two orders are summed before this transport.
Samples which are flagged as saturated in the group quality array, or which
are not finite at the linearity input, keep their original values, and the
part of the decrement which could not be delivered is recorded. In NOVA's
own detector processing, moreover, the group-level background and $1/f$ steps
are applied between the reference-pixel correction and the linearity step,
so the counts which its linearity step sees differ from those used in the
inversion by these two corrections. Everything else in the raw data, i.e.\
the observed noise, background and field sources, is left untouched, and no
scene mask is used anywhere in the injection.

\subsubsection{Detector processing}
\label{sec:detector-processing}

% Question: What happens to the injected ramps before recovery?
% Answer: NOVA's input runs jwst group steps to refpix, exoTEDRF 2.4.3
% group-level background and 1/f steps, jwst linearity/jump/ramp/gain, then
% exoTEDRF Stage 2 (including background, flat and bad-pixel steps); the
% comparator pilots start at extraction from the rate-level injection, and in
% production each comparator runs its own Stage 1 on the raw frames.
% Evidence: source/run_nova_original_A_group_detector.py (lines 55-100,
% baseline_ints [50,-50], space/time thresholds 15, skip_steps);
% reports/PHYSICAL_MATCHED_NATIVE_NULL_20260918.md (exoTEDRF 2.4.3); evidence/
% native_null_completion_20260918_r1.json (NATIVE_F277W_MASK_USED.json is
% cluster-only); reports/rate_native_pilot_preparation_20260921/ENTRY_STAGE_DECISION.md.

NOVA's input is prepared from the injected raw segments with the same
processing chain that it uses for the real data, which combines public steps
from two packages. Firstly, the group-level steps of Stage~1 of the JWST
calibration pipeline \citep{JWSTPipeline2025}, the stage which turns raw
ramps into count-rate images, are run up to and including the
reference-pixel correction. Secondly, the group-level background and $1/f$
steps of exoTEDRF 2.4.3 \citep{Radica2024} are applied. Thirdly, the
pipeline's linearity, jump-detection, ramp-fitting and gain steps are run,
without a dark-current subtraction. Finally, Stage~2 of exoTEDRF, which
includes its own background subtraction, the flat field and a bad-pixel
correction with thresholds of 15 in space and time, gives rate images in
$\mathrm{DN\,s^{-1}}$. On the injected inputs, the background and $1/f$
steps take the first and last 50 integrations as their out-of-transit
reference, which are exactly the integrations that the injected transit
leaves unaffected; this choice follows from the design of the injection, not
from an inspection of the data. The $1/f$ step masks the contaminating
sources identified in the F277W exposure, and I check that the mask built
when the pipeline runs is identical to the reference mask of 12{,}808
pixels. No scene mask of the project's own, i.e.\ a veto of whole detector
columns affected by field stars, is applied, and the null follows exactly the
same chain.

In the pilot comparisons, the three comparison pipelines do not run their
own detector processing. They start at their own extraction step from the
rate-level injection of the $m=0$ member, i.e.\ from the Stage~2 products of
the null with the signal added (Section~\ref{sec:baseline}), and their own
flat-field, background and bad-pixel steps are not repeated, so the pilot
tests their extraction and light-curve fitting only. In production, each
pipeline will instead take the same injected raw frames and run its own
Stage~1 and Stage~2, including its own group-level $1/f$ treatment.

\subsubsection{Delivery checks}

% Question: How is it known that the requested signal reached the data?
% Answer: Protected-pixel, read and positivity checks on the written files,
% an exact aperture-sum check inside the 1/f step, and a grey linearity test:
% a constant 16,000 ppm depth injected under none, half and all of the far
% light must respond linearly to within 10 ppm in 2,188 order-column pairs.
% Evidence: reports/LIGHT_BASED_GREY_DELIVERY_RESULT_20260921.md;
% reports/MEASURED_GREY_DELIVERY_RESULT_20260922.md; source/
% run_fixed_family_delivery_report_20260920.py and run_measured_grey_report_20260922.py
% (pairs, gains, secant residual); stage3b_float_sci_persistence_adapter_r2.py
% (protected masks); products/light_based_family_20260921_r1/FAMILY.json (delivery).

Every injection run must pass a set of checks before its files are used. The
written files must have the expected number of reads, the count changes must
be nonpositive, and the protected pixels, i.e.\ the reference pixels and
every pixel outside the support of the injected light, must be bit-for-bit
identical to the input, as must all other extensions of the raw files and
every saturated or inactive sample. During the detector processing, the
aperture sums of each order and column are also recorded before and after
the $1/f$ step, and the frames written by the step must reproduce the
recorded sums exactly, which shows that recording them did not alter the
data.

The family is then tested with a grey signal, i.e.\ a constant depth of
16{,}000~ppm at every wavelength node, injected under each of the three
far-light assumptions and processed with the detector chain above. In each
pair of order and column, a gain $g_m$ is measured by regressing the aperture
signal after the $1/f$ step on that before it over integrations 50 to 178,
where each signal is the difference between the injected run and the null.
If the delivered response is linear in the far-light fraction, the gain at
$m=0.5$ should lie on the straight line (the secant) between the gains at
$m=0$ and $m=1$. The departure from this secant, expressed as an equivalent
depth, $16{,}000\,[g_{0.5}-(g_0+g_1)/2]$~ppm, is evaluated in 2{,}188 pairs
of order and column (1{,}742 in order 1 between 1.0 and 2.7~$\mu\mathrm{m}$,
and 446 in order 2 between 0.65 and 0.85~$\mu\mathrm{m}$), in which all three
gains are finite and the signal before the correction exceeds
1~$\mathrm{DN\,s^{-1}}$, and delivery only passes if the largest departure is
below 10~ppm. The measured-fraction member is checked in the same way,
against the secant evaluated at its own $m_x$, with the same 10~ppm limit.
Passing this check only shows that the signal is delivered
consistently across the family; whether the far light actually comes from
the star remains open.

\subsubsection{Carrying the far-light uncertainty into the results}
\label{sec:envelope}

% Question: How is the far-light uncertainty carried into a result?
% Answer: The reference atmosphere is recovered by NOVA at both bracketing
% members; the spread between its two raw-level recoveries decides how the
% family's results are described, not whether they are released.
% Evidence: products/light_based_family_20260921_r1/FAMILY.json
% (endpoint_criterion: 10 ppm budget for an intended 30 ppm claim;
% chromatic.cases); reports/PHYSICAL_INJECTOR_PROTOCOL_20260918.md section 5;
% reports/PHYSICAL_EMISSION_RUNTIME_AND_ARMS_20260918.md (dga_00, dga_01);
% reports/OVERNIGHT_PRODUCTION_ADDENDUM_20260921.md; reports/
% W17_PRODUCTION_BINDING_INVENTORY_20260923.md; reports/
% SOURCE_MAP_A_DECISION_20260920_R2.json (core and wing checks).

Because the stellar share of the far light is not known, its effect is
carried into the results as an envelope. In the pilot, a reference
atmosphere is injected under both bracketing assumptions, $m=0$ and $m=1$,
at both the raw and the rate level, and recovered with NOVA, and the
envelope is the root-mean-square difference between its two raw-level
recoveries over the 147 reporting bins. The reference atmosphere is a member
of the generic ATMO grid of transmission spectra \citep{GoyalEtAl2019},
rescaled to WASP-17~b, with rainout condensation, a temperature of 2600~K, a
surface gravity of $5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, 200 times solar metallicity,
$\mathrm{C/O}=0.56$, a haze factor of 1100 and a cloud factor of 1. A
second pilot atmosphere from the same grid, with local condensation, 2400~K,
$10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, 200 times solar metallicity, $\mathrm{C/O}=1.0$, a
haze factor of 10 and a cloud factor of 0.2, is recovered at the raw level
only, and only at $m=1$; it was chosen, before any score had been seen, as
the first distinct case of the existing list of atmospheres. Each pilot case
gets its own calibration products, its own white-light geometry and three
strict starts (Section~\ref{sec:nova}).

If the envelope is 10~ppm or less, I describe results obtained with the
family as validated within the scope of the source family; if it is larger
than 10~ppm, I describe them as usable and quote the envelope alongside
them. The envelope therefore sets how results are described, and no result
is withheld because of it. Note that the two endpoints bracket the far-light
assumption and are not confidence bounds, and that the envelope measures
NOVA's sensitivity to this assumption, not the accuracy of the core map. The
10~ppm threshold was chosen as a budget for an intended claim about
differences of 30~ppm between pipelines; it is a design choice, not a
measured quantity.

In production, each atmosphere will be recovered once, with the
measured-fraction member, and its results will be described according to the
envelope measured in the pilot. There are 24 atmospheres per visit, drawn from
the same generic grid, twelve with local condensation and twelve with
rainout chemistry, and each is fitted with two strict starts. The programme
contains six visits, of WASP-17, HAT-P-26, WASP-39, HAT-P-18, WASP-96 and
WASP-80. The production analysis of each visit only goes ahead once its own
white-light geometry has been accepted and it has passed the same checks as
WASP-17: its core map must not exceed the observed out-of-transit aperture
light in any wavelength region, its wing map must be stable between two
folds of the data (a difference in source fraction of at most 0.15\%, with
a held-out wing residual within 0.25\%), and its family must pass the grey
delivery check. Any exception to these rules is declared and reported with
the results.

\subsubsection{Comparison pipelines and fairness}

% Question: Against what is NOVA compared, and on what terms?
% Answer: Three public pipelines run as their authors run them, on the same
% injected data, each with its own white-light inference; only the geometry
% priors are shared, and the transitspectroscopy joint white fit and
% unspecified settings are declared departures.
% Evidence: reports/COMPARATOR_AUTHOR_PROTOCOL_AUTHORIZATION_20260923.md;
% config/COMPARATOR_SHARED_GEOMETRY_PRIORS_20260923_R1.json; reports/
% TRANSITSPECTROSCOPY_JOINT_WHITE_AUTHORIZATION_20260922.md; reports/
% TRANSITSPECTROSCOPY_REMAINING_CONFIGURATION_20260922.md; reports/
% TS_MPS2_SPECTRAL_CONTINUATION_PREPARATION_20260923_R1.md; public Ahsoka
% S5_wasp17_230320_nirissO1.ecf and epf files; reports/EXOTEDRF_AUTHOR_RATE_RESULT_20260923.md.

NOVA is compared with three public pipelines, each run on the same injected
data in the way its authors run it: the Ahsoka analysis behind the published
WASP-17~b spectrum \citep{LouieEtAl2025}, exoTEDRF 2.5.0 \citep{Radica2024}
and \texttt{transitspectroscopy} 0.3.12
\citep{Espinoza2022TransitSpectroscopy}. Each keeps the sampler, run length
and acceptance criteria of its authors. Ahsoka fits the two orders separately
with the \texttt{emcee} ensemble sampler \citep{ForemanMackeyEtAl2013} in
Eureka! \citep{BellEtAl2022}, using 200 walkers and 1{,}000 steps, of which
the first 500 are discarded, and in the spectral fit of each order it fixes
$t_0$, $a/R_\star$ and $i$ to the posterior medians of that order's
white-light fit. The exoTEDRF pipeline fits a joint two-order white-light
curve with its own nested sampler. The \texttt{transitspectroscopy} pipeline
traces the median image with its double-Gaussian template, extracts a box
aperture with a radius of 15 pixels, and fits the spectral light curves with
ExoTETHyS limb darkening from MPS-ATLAS set~2, in Kipping coordinates. Its
white-light fit is a declared departure from the published analysis:
\citet{LouieEtAl2025} fitted the white-light curves of the two orders
separately, whereas here the two are fitted jointly, following the package
author's own joint two-order implementation, with \texttt{juliet}
\citep{EspinozaEtAl2019} nested sampling and a Mat\'ern-3/2 process for each
order. Where the published description leaves a setting of
\texttt{transitspectroscopy} open, I fixed it before any recovery: ten spline
knots, a 5-sample, $5\sigma$ temporal outlier filter, top-hat passbands, and
the limb-darkening grid, for which MPS-ATLAS replaced the STAGGER grid because
the latter has no models at this star's gravity above 6500~K.

The published priors of Ahsoka describe the real planet: its mid-transit
time lies about 2.5~h after the end of the injected window, and its
$a/R_\star$ of about 7 is inconsistent with the injected orbit. In every
white-light fit, the priors on $t_0$, $a/R_\star$ and $i$ are therefore set
to those which NOVA uses for this observation, i.e.\ $t_0$ uniform over the
observed window, $a/R_\star$ uniform on $[1.01,\,100]$ and $i$ uniform on
$[0^\circ,\,90^\circ]$, with $P$, $e$ and $\omega_{\mathrm{peri}}$ fixed, so
that all four methods infer the geometry under the same wide priors. No
pipeline receives the injected geometry or the fit of another pipeline. For
each comparison pipeline I also compute my own convergence statistics, but
these are reported for information only and are not used to reject any of
its results, and failed runs are reported alongside the successful ones.
Every method has an estimate, or a recorded failure, in every reporting bin
before the injected spectrum is opened.

\subsubsection{Reporting support and evaluation metrics}
\label{sec:reporting}

% Question: On what wavelength support are methods compared, and with which
% metrics?
% Answer: 147 whole bins of a common R=100 grid inside the validated
% wavelength calibration, scored by RMSE, mean bias and shape RMS against the
% unweighted bin average of the injected spectrum.
% Evidence: products/w17_frozen_postclosure_score_20260923_r1_prepared/source/
% score_w17_frozen147.py, original_score_reference.py and
% original_truth_averaging.py, and config/CASE_TEMPLATE_UNBOUND.json (regions);
% source/run_aperture_terminal_covariance_r1.py (overlap-weighted map);
% products/control_B_scored_20260921_r1/COMPARISON.npz; AGENTS.md (8 September
% decision); reports/CLAUDE_SECOND_REVIEW_20260918.md (origin of the summary
% ranges); appendices/appendix_nova.tex (bin 149).

Recovered and injected spectra are compared on a common $R=100$ grid of 150
bins between 0.63 and 2.81~$\mu\mathrm{m}$. NOVA's 160 native coefficients
are mapped onto this grid by a fixed map, in which each bin takes the mean of
the native coefficients it overlaps, weighted by the length of the overlap,
and every retained bin must be completely covered. Bins 147 and 148 are
excluded because their detector support lies beyond the validated range of
the public wavelength calibration, where the calibration is only an
extrapolation, and bin 149, the reddest bin of the grid, is excluded by
construction, as in the earlier release. The retained support is therefore
bins 0 to 146, i.e.\ 147 bins, the last of which ends at
$2.740\,\mu\mathrm{m}$. The same rule is applied to every method, and only
at the reporting stage. No interpolation or gap filling is allowed, so if a
method has no estimate in a retained bin, that bin is reported as missing.
The injected depth in each bin, $D_i^{\mathrm{inj}}$, is the unweighted
average of $D(\lambda)$ over the bin.

I score each recovery with the root-mean-square error
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{RMSE}=
 \left(\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{b}}}\sum_i e_i^2\right)^{1/2}
 \label{eq:absolute-rmse}
\end{equation}
and the shape RMS
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{RMS}_{\mathrm{shape}}=
 \left[\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{b}}}\sum_i(e_i-\bar e)^2\right]^{1/2},
 \label{eq:morphology-rmse}
\end{equation}
where $e_i=\widehat D_i-D_i^{\mathrm{inj}}$ is the difference between the
recovered depth $\widehat D_i$ and the injected depth in retained bin $i$,
$N_{\mathrm{b}}$ is the number of retained bins, and $\bar e$, the mean of
the $e_i$, is the mean bias. I also report $\bar e$ itself and the maximum
absolute error. Since
$\mathrm{RMSE}^2=\bar e^{\,2}+\mathrm{RMS}_{\mathrm{shape}}^2$, these
scores separate a constant offset from an error in the shape of the
spectrum, and no offset correction is applied to any reported spectrum. The
same scores are also reported on six contiguous wavelength ranges, with
boundaries at 1.0, 1.6, 1.8, 2.3 and 2.7~$\mu\mathrm{m}$, and on two summary
ranges, below 2.1~$\mu\mathrm{m}$ (120 bins) and from 2.12 to
2.76~$\mu\mathrm{m}$ inclusive (26 bins, ending in practice at
2.740~$\mu\mathrm{m}$). The two summary ranges separate the red end, where
earlier comparisons showed the largest differences, and the one bin whose
centre lies between 2.10 and 2.12~$\mu\mathrm{m}$ belongs to neither.

The injected spectrum is only compared with a recovery once that recovery's
estimates in all 147 bins have been recorded. Until then, every choice of
calibration, model and starting point depends only on the data and the
instrument reference files (Appendix~\ref{app:benchmarking}), apart from the
out-of-transit reference integrations of the background and $1/f$ steps
described in Section~\ref{sec:detector-processing}. Each score is reported
together with the standardised residuals of the fit which produced it, its
covariance conditional on the calibration products, and its weight,
conditioning and convergence diagnostics. For NOVA, the uncertainty which I
quote for the spectrum is the one from the ensemble of
Section~\ref{sec:ensemble}, once it has passed its validation; for the
comparison pipelines, their own reported uncertainties are used, and these
are not validated in the same way.
