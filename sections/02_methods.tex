\section{Methods and Benchmark Design}
\label{sec:methods}

% Question: What do the methods of this project consist of?
% Answer: NOVA, the detector-level inference method, and the injector, which
% writes a known transit into the raw ramps of real JWST data so that
% recovery accuracy can be measured absolutely.
% Evidence: The structure of this section; NOVA_ASTRA_EXECUTION_20260905_R1/
% STATUS.md. Settings and tolerances: appendices/appendix_implementation.tex.

In this section I describe the two methods developed in this project.
Firstly, NOVA (Section~\ref{sec:nova}) fits a forward model of the detector
time series directly to the detector pixels, with the transit geometry
inferred from the same data. Secondly, the injector
(Section~\ref{sec:injector}) writes a known transit into the raw ramps of a
real observation, so that the accuracy of a recovery can be measured against
a known spectrum. Both are described as they are currently configured. NOVA
grew out of an earlier release, frozen on 26 August 2026, whose constants
are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:nova}; where the two differ, the values
given here are the current ones. To keep the explanation readable, the
individual settings, tolerances and checks are collected in
Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\subsection{\nova{}}
\label{sec:nova}

% Question: What problem does NOVA solve, and in what form?
% Answer: It fits one forward model of the detector to all retained
% samples, conditional on calibration products and a white-light geometry
% estimated beforehand from the same input.
% Evidence: Sections 1.3 and 1.4; config/MATCHED_COVARIANCE_ENSEMBLE_R1.json
% (estimator.rebuild).

The aim of NOVA is to infer the transmission spectrum from the detector
pixels themselves, so that the split between starlight and background is
decided in the same fit as the transit. To do this, NOVA describes the
observation with a single forward model, which maps the transmission
spectrum $D(\lambda)$, the limb darkening $\bm{u}(\lambda)$, the brightness
of the star and the additive background to a prediction for every retained
detector sample, and all four are estimated together by minimising one
objective over these samples. No one-dimensional spectrum is extracted for
this fit, and the spectrum is only mapped onto wavelength bins at the very
end, for reporting (Section~\ref{sec:reporting}).

Some ingredients of the model are, however, estimated beforehand from the
same data and then held fixed: the way the starlight is spread over the
pixels, a correction for slow changes in the brightness of the star, the
temporal pattern and expected level of the background, the uncertainty
scales, and the transit geometry. The geometry comes from a separate fit to
white-light curves, and this is the one place where NOVA does extract
(Section~\ref{sec:own-white}). I run NOVA on the real WASP-17 visit, using
all 720 integrations, and on the injected inputs of
Section~\ref{sec:injector}, which are built from the first 229 integrations
of the same visit. A few calibration products are carried over from the
earlier release, namely the spatial maps of the background, the two
per-order uncertainty scales and the functional form of the brightness correction, together with the pixel
layout of the model and the set of off-trace pixels used for the background; every other fitted
calibration product, and the white-light geometry, is re-estimated on each
input. In the rest of this section I describe the
forward model and each of its components, the objective and how it is
minimised, the white-light fit which provides the geometry and, finally, how
the uncertainty of the recovered spectrum is estimated.

\subsubsection{The detector forward model}

% Question: What does NOVA predict for each detector sample?
% Answer: Continuum times exposure-integrated transit times spatial
% response, corrected by a source-curvature factor, plus an additive
% background; in the implementation the transit is evaluated on fine
% wavelength nodes and projected through the response.
% Evidence: proposals/NOMENCLATURE_PRESENTATION.md; proposals/
% handoff_nova_s_20260828/01_EXACT_NOVA_S_METHOD.md section 3;
% NOVA_SP_NOVA_S_SCIENCE_FREEZE_20260826_R1/code/sparse_varpro/
% fine_grid_transit_adapter.py and response_aware_varpro.py.

The light of each spectral order falls on the detector as a curved trace,
and within one detector column the pixels of a trace see nearly the same
wavelength. NOVA therefore organises the detector samples into groups, where
a group $g$ contains the pixels $P_g$ of one order which lie in the same
column, and I write $g(p)$ for the group which contains pixel $p$. Denoting
by $Y_{tp}$ the calibrated count rate recorded in pixel $p$ during
integration $t$, NOVA predicts
\begin{equation}
 \widehat Y_{tp}
 = \gamma_t\, C_{tg}\,
   \mathcal{T}_{tg}(D, \bm{u};\, \Omega)\,
   q^\star_{pg}
 + B_{tp},
 \label{eq:nova-forward}
\end{equation}
where $C_{tg}$ is the brightness of the star in group $g$ at time $t$ (the
continuum), $\mathcal{T}_{tg}$ is the transit factor, which dims this light
according to the depth $D$ and limb darkening $\bm{u}$ at the wavelengths of
the group and the orbital state $\Omega$, $\gamma_t$ is a small correction
for slow curvature in the brightness of the star, $q^\star_{pg}$ is the
spatial response, which distributes the light of the group over its pixels,
with $\sum_{p\in P_g} q^\star_{pg}=1$, and $B_{tp}$ is the additive
background. The transit factor and $\gamma_t$ multiply only the stellar
term, so any light which the model assigns to the background is treated as
light which does not dim during the transit.

Equation~\ref{eq:nova-forward} is written per group for clarity. In
practice, each column receives light from a small range of wavelengths, and
so the continuum of each column is spread onto a fine wavelength grid, the
transit is evaluated at each wavelength of that grid, and the result is
projected onto the pixels through the spectral-response (line-spread)
kernel and the spatial response. The positions of the groups, their
wavelengths and the kernel follow the public trace and wavelength
calibration \citep{BainesEtAl2023Trace,BainesEtAl2023Wavelength} and the
associated spectral-response kernel
\citep{DarveauBernierEtAl2022,JWSTPipeline2025}. The supports of the two
orders do not share any pixel, so no pixel is modelled as containing light
from both orders. The support and the rules for removing individual samples
are given in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\subsubsection{The shared spectrum and transit model}

% Question: How are the depths and the transit generated?
% Answer: One spectrum of 160 piecewise-constant coefficients shared by both
% orders, an exact Keplerian transit averaged over each exposure at the
% geometry from NOVA's own white-light fit, and penalised limb-darkening
% deviations around a STAGGER theory reference.
% Evidence: NOVA_SP_NOVA_S_SCIENCE_FREEZE_20260826_R1/reporting/
% v50_cycle2_reporting_inputs/shared_adaptive_observed_k160.json;
% configuration/variant_E12.json; reports/ONEOVERF_CAUSAL_INVESTIGATION_20260909.md
% (Delta); code/project_sources/nonlinear_detector_sp_v0p1_20260720_src/
% nonlinear_sp/limb_darkening.py (lines 9-12, 935-965); reports/
% NOVA_LIMB_DARKENING_WORDING_CORRECTION_20260923.md; public Ahsoka epf (period).

A planet has one transmission spectrum, whichever order its light is
recorded in, and so both orders see the same spectrum $D(\lambda)$. It is
represented by $K=160$ top-hat basis functions in wavelength, about
0.01~$\mu\mathrm{m}$ wide below 2~$\mu\mathrm{m}$ and about
0.04~$\mu\mathrm{m}$ wide above it, so that the depth at any wavelength is the
coefficient of the basis function which contains it. The coefficients are
parameterised as
\begin{equation}
  D_j = D_{\mathrm{global}}\,
  \frac{\exp\!\left[(\bm{H}_v\bm{v})_j\right]}
       {\sum_k w_k \exp\!\left[(\bm{H}_v\bm{v})_k\right]} ,
  \label{eq:depth-basis}
\end{equation}
where $D_{\mathrm{global}}$ is the achromatic depth, $\bm{v}$ are the free
shape coordinates, $\bm{H}_v$ maps them onto the subspace orthogonal to a
constant, and $w_k$ are fixed basis-width weights. This form separates the
overall depth from the shape of the spectrum, whose weighted mean is zero,
and it keeps every depth positive. Two coefficients at the red end have no
response support and are held constant, which leaves 158 free depth
coordinates. Beyond this parameterisation the spectrum is left free: I
impose no atmospheric template, no smoothing between neighbouring
wavelengths and no prior on its shape. The model also contains no term which
allows the depths of the two orders to differ; the order-difference term of
the earlier release is now fixed at exactly zero
(Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}).

The orbital state is
$\Omega=(P,\,a/R_\star,\,e,\,\omega_{\mathrm{peri}},\,b,\,t_0)$. The period
is fixed at the value used by \citet{LouieEtAl2025} and the orbit is taken to
be circular, whilst the mid-transit time $t_0$, the scaled semi-major axis
$a/R_\star$ and the impact parameter $b$ come from NOVA's white-light fit to
the same input (Section~\ref{sec:own-white}) and are held fixed during the
spectral fit. Because each integration lasts about 44~s, during which the
planet moves across the stellar disc, $\mathcal{T}_{tg}$ is not the transit
at a single instant but its average over the exposure. The instantaneous
limb-darkened transit is computed with jaxoplanet \citep{Jaxoplanet2025},
which solves the Keplerian orbit exactly. The limb darkening follows the
quadratic law \citep{MandelAgol2002}, whose coefficients $u_1$ and $u_2$ are
written in terms of the coordinates $q_1$ and $q_2$ of \citet{Kipping2013},
which each lie between 0 and 1,
\begin{equation}
 u_1=2\sqrt{q_1}\,q_2, \qquad
 u_2=\sqrt{q_1}\,(1-2q_2).
 \label{eq:kipping-coordinates}
\end{equation}

Limb darkening and transit depth are partly degenerate, and so, instead of
fitting the limb darkening freely at every wavelength, the spectral fit
allows only a smooth deviation from a theoretical reference and penalises
it. The reference is computed from STAGGER specific intensities
\citep{MagicEtAl2015}, as close to the stellar parameters of WASP-17 as the
grid allows. Around it, the fit has one offset and one slope in normalised log-wavelength for each
coordinate and order, eight coordinates in total, and these are penalised both for
deviating from the theory and for curvature in wavelength
(Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). The white-light fit, by contrast,
leaves the limb darkening free. The injector computes its transit from a
different stellar model, MPS-ATLAS (Section~\ref{sec:per-read}), so this
reference is independent of the limb darkening of the injected signal.

\subsubsection{The empirical spatial response}

% Question: Where does q-star come from?
% Answer: The transit itself reveals where the starlight lands: a per-pixel
% Huber regression on a unit-depth transit template, with amplitudes
% normalised within each group and smoothed with a strength chosen by
% cross-fold prediction, rebuilt on every input.
% Evidence: products/original_v45_reference_qstar_r1_package/source/
% build_v47_empirical_template.py and aggregate_v47_qstar.py; products/
% original_A_group_calibration_adaptation_r1/build_nova_s_real_wasp17_responses_r1.py;
% NOVA-S handoff 01_EXACT_NOVA_S_METHOD.md section 6 (red-end limitation).

To distribute the light of a group over its pixels, NOVA needs to know where
the starlight lands, and this is estimated from the data themselves. Because
starlight dims during the transit whilst the background does not, the part
of a pixel's time series which follows the transit measures how much
starlight falls on that pixel. I first build, for each order $o$, a template
$\phi_o(t)$ of the shape of the transit, from the median behaviour of the
bright, stable pixels of the order, scaled so that it carries the shape of
the transit but no information about its depth. Every pixel of the support is
then fitted, with Huber weights, as
\begin{equation}
 Y_{tp} = A_{pg}\,\phi_{o}(t) + \ell_p + \kappa_p\,\tau_t + \varepsilon_{tp},
 \label{eq:qstar-fit}
\end{equation}
where $A_{pg}$ is the transit-correlated amplitude of the pixel, $\ell_p$ is
its level, $\kappa_p\tau_t$ is a linear drift in the time $\tau_t$, and
$\varepsilon_{tp}$ is the residual. The response is the normalised
amplitude,
\begin{equation*}
 q^\star_{pg}=\frac{A_{pg}}{\sum_{p'\in P_g}A_{p'g}},
\end{equation*}
so that $q^\star$ only describes how the light of a group is spread over its
pixels, whilst the total brightness of the group is carried by the continuum
$C_{tg}$.

Each amplitude is measured from a dip of only about one and a half per cent,
and so the individual values are noisy. The normalised amplitudes are
therefore smoothed across neighbouring columns, at a fixed position within
the trace, and the strength of the smoothing is chosen by cross-validation
between two halves of the integrations. The same two halves also serve as a
check: the response is only accepted if they agree in how they distribute
the flux and where they place its centre. The details, including how the
response is combined with the amplitudes measured in the wings of the trace,
are given in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

This approach has a known limitation. Because the smoothing works at a fixed
integer position relative to the trace, the centre of the response
occasionally jumps from one column to the next. In the audits of the earlier
release, this defect came with a response at the faintest red columns which
placed roughly 1--2\% more amplitude inside the fitted pixels than the light
actually delivered there, and this biased the recovered transit to be too
shallow at those columns (Appendix~\ref{app:nova}). The same estimator is
used on every input, but the size of the effect has not yet been re-measured
for the response built on each input.

\subsubsection{The continuum and the additive background}

% Question: How are the stellar continuum and the background modelled and
% calibrated?
% Answer: A level and slope per group for the continuum; eight fixed spatial
% maps with a constant and one temporal pattern for the residual background
% left after the detector processing, the pattern and anchor recomputed on
% the off-trace pixels of each input.
% Evidence: build_e13b_basis_products.py (cluster-only); products/
% original_A_group_calibration_adaptation_r1/build_nova_s_real_wasp17_e13b_r1.py;
% source/run_nova_original_A_group_detector.py; reports/
% OFFTRACE_TRANSIT_COMPONENT_DIAGNOSIS_20260921.md section 2.

Over the course of a visit, the brightness of the star in each group can
drift slowly, and so the continuum of each group is taken to be linear in
time, $C_{tg}=\beta_{0g}+\beta_{1g}\tau_t$, where $\beta_{0g}$ and
$\beta_{1g}$ are its level and slope. With 2{,}720 groups this gives 5{,}440
linear coefficients. The background is modelled as a sum of eight fixed
spatial maps $\Phi_{pk}$, each with a constant amplitude and an amplitude
which follows one temporal pattern $G_1(t)$,
\begin{equation}
 B_{tp}=\sum_{k=1}^{8}\Phi_{pk}
 \left[\alpha_{k0}+\alpha_{k1}\,G_1(t)\right],
 \label{eq:background-model}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_{k0}$ and $\alpha_{k1}$ are the constant and time-varying
amplitudes of map $k$, sixteen coefficients in total. Note that the images
which NOVA receives have already had a scaled model of the zodiacal
background subtracted during the detector processing
(Section~\ref{sec:detector-processing}), and so $B_{tp}$ describes the
background which remains after that subtraction, including any error in the
scaling of the model. The first map is a standardised map of the calibration
background and the other seven are smooth polynomial surfaces; they were
chosen by cross-validation on the pixels away from the traces in the earlier
release, and are carried over unchanged.

The rest of the background model is learnt from the pixels away from the
traces. The eight maps are
fitted to these off-trace pixels in every integration, and the temporal
pattern $G_1$ is the first principal component of the resulting time series
whose correlation with the shape of the transit is weak (an absolute value
of at most 0.25). This restriction limits how much transit-shaped variation the background can
absorb. On the real visit, the leading component itself
follows the transit closely, which means that part of the off-trace light
does dim with the star. The criterion keeps this component out of the
background model, the uncertainty ensemble puts it back
(Section~\ref{sec:ensemble}), and the far-light family of the injector is
designed to bracket its effect (Section~\ref{sec:dimmed-light}). A joint fit
to all off-trace pixels also gives an expected value
$\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{off}}$ of the sixteen coefficients, with precision
matrix $\bm{N}_{\mathrm{off}}$. This enters the objective as a soft anchor,
i.e.\ a quadratic penalty on the background coefficients, so that the
background is refitted together with the continuum at every step but cannot
wander far from what the off-trace pixels show.

\subsubsection{The source-curvature factor and detector uncertainties}

% Question: What are gamma_t and sigma_tp?
% Answer: An out-of-transit curvature correction of fixed common form,
% recomputed and cross-checked on each input, and per-sample uncertainties
% scaled per order and per pixel.
% Evidence: products/original_A_group_calibration_adaptation_r1/
% build_nova_s_real_wasp17_source_curvature_r1.py; config/
% CURVATURE_POLICY_AUTHORIZATION_20260919.json; NOVA_SP_NOVA_S_SCIENCE_FREEZE_
% 20260826_R1/code/runtime/N_SCALE_CONTRACT.json; reports/
% offtrace_transit_diagnosis_20260921/sources/v47_oot_guard.py.

A linear continuum cannot follow a gentle curvature in the brightness of the
star over the visit, and a curvature of this kind can leak into the depth.
The factor $\gamma_t$ corrects for it. The out-of-transit flux of the trace
of each order is fitted with a quadratic in time, and $\gamma_t$ is the
ratio of this quadratic to its straight-line approximation, normalised to
unit mean out of transit, so that it contains only the curvature, since the
linear trend is already carried by the continuum. The same factor is used
for both orders, a choice made on the earlier release by comparing it with no correction and
with a separate factor for each order (Appendix~\ref{app:nova}). The factor
multiplies only the stellar term of Equation~\ref{eq:nova-forward} and adds
no fitted parameters.

A correction of this kind is only useful if it describes the star, and not
the noise of one particular set of integrations. On each input, the factor
is therefore also computed from two alternating halves of the out-of-transit
integrations, and it is only accepted if the two halves predict nearly the
same correction during the transit and if the correction itself stays small
(Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). For the real visit and the injected
inputs, the two halves must also be strongly correlated. For the
realisations of the uncertainty ensemble this correlation is only reported,
a rule which was changed after the correlation was found to reject
realisations whose corrections were only 1--15~ppm, since two negligible
corrections of opposite sign are anticorrelated even when both are
harmless.

The uncertainty of each sample is
\begin{equation*}
 \sigma_{tp}=\mathrm{ERR}_{tp}\,N_{o}\,s_{p},
\end{equation*}
where $\mathrm{ERR}_{tp}$ is the uncertainty array from the pipeline, $N_o$
is a scale for order $o$ carried over from the earlier release (3.07 for order 1 and 4.19 for order 2; Appendix~\ref{app:nova}), and $s_p\geq1$ is a scale for each pixel,
estimated on each input by predicting one half of the out-of-transit
integrations from the other. Pixels whose out-of-transit behaviour is noisier
than their nominal uncertainties suggest are therefore down-weighted. These
scales only set the weights of the fit; the uncertainty of the recovered
spectrum comes from the ensemble of Section~\ref{sec:ensemble}.

\subsubsection{The complete objective}

% Question: What does NOVA minimise?
% Answer: A Huber detector mismatch over the retained mask plus the
% off-trace background anchor and the limb-darkening penalties.
% Evidence: NOVA_SP_NOVA_S_SCIENCE_FREEZE_20260826_R1/code/sparse_varpro/
% production_irls.py and code/project_sources/nonlinear_detector_sp_v0p3_
% huber_discrepancy_factorial_20260722_r4_src/nonlinear_sp_huber_factorial/
% robust.py (HUBER_DELTA 1.345).

Writing the standardised residual as
\begin{equation}
 r_{tp}=\frac{Y_{tp}-\widehat Y_{tp}
 (D,\bm{u},\bm{\beta},\bm{\alpha};\,q^\star,\Omega)}{\sigma_{tp}},
 \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
where the fitted parameters appear before the semicolon and the fixed inputs
after it, the NOVA estimate is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
 &(\widehat D,\widehat{\bm{u}},\widehat{\bm{\beta}},
  \widehat{\bm{\alpha}})
 = \arg\min\Big\{ \\
 &\quad\sum_{(t,p)\in\mathcal{M}}\rho_{1.345}(r_{tp}) \\
 &\quad+\tfrac{1}{2}(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{off}})^{\mathsf T}
   \bm{N}_{\mathrm{off}}(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{off}}) \\
 &\quad+\tfrac{1}{2}\|\bm{r}_{\mathrm{LD,theory}}(\bm{u})\|_2^2
  +\tfrac{1}{2}\|\bm{r}_{\mathrm{LD,curv}}(\bm{u})\|_2^2
 \Big\},
\end{split}
 \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{M}$ is the mask of retained samples, $\rho_{1.345}$ is the
Huber loss, which is quadratic for $|r_{tp}|\leq1.345$ and linear beyond, so
that outliers do not dominate the fit (Appendix~\ref{app:nova}), and
$\bm{r}_{\mathrm{LD,theory}}$ and $\bm{r}_{\mathrm{LD,curv}}$ are the
deviations of the limb darkening from theory and its curvatures in
wavelength, each divided by its scale. The first term measures the mismatch between the model and the detector
data, the second is the soft anchor on the background, and the last two are
the limb-darkening penalties. The objective contains no Gaussian-process term
for correlated noise; outliers are handled by the Huber loss alone.

\subsubsection{The nested solver}

% Question: How is the objective minimised, and what counts as convergence?
% Answer: Huber weights outside, bounded trust-region steps in the middle,
% an exact linear solve inside; acceptance requires convergence checks; among
% strict starts the lowest objective wins, agreement is reported.
% Evidence: code/sparse_varpro/production_irls.py and code/runtime/
% STRICT_IRLS_POLICY.json; products/w17_measured_production_recovery_20260923_
% v7_r1_package/recipes/closure/source/close_causal_reduced_starts.py;
% reports/PHYSICAL_ENDPOINT_THIRD_START_20260918.md.

The model has thousands of parameters, but most of them enter linearly: for
given depths and limb darkening, the prediction is linear in the continuum
and background coefficients, whereas the depths and limb-darkening
coordinates enter nonlinearly, through the transit. NOVA
exploits this by splitting the parameters into a nonlinear block
$\bm{\theta}=(D,\bm{u})$ and a linear block $(\bm{\beta},\bm{\alpha})$ and
minimising the objective in three nested loops. In the innermost loop, with
$\bm{\theta}$ and the weights held fixed, variable projection
\citep{GolubPereyra1973} solves the linear problem exactly,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
 (\widehat{\bm{\beta}},\widehat{\bm{\alpha}})=
 \arg\min_{\bm{\beta},\bm{\alpha}}\;
 &\frac{1}{2}\sum_{(t,p)\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{w_{tp}}{\sigma_{tp}^2}
 \left[Y_{tp}-\widehat Y_{tp}\right]^2 \\
 &+\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{off}})^{\mathsf T}
   \bm{N}_{\mathrm{off}}(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{\mathrm{off}}),
\end{split}
 \label{eq:varpro}
\end{equation}
which remains tractable because the continuum block is sparse, each
coefficient being coupled only to those of neighbouring columns through the
line-spread kernel, and the sixteen background coefficients are handled
separately through a Schur complement. The
nonlinear search then only has to explore $\bm{\theta}$, and its Jacobian is
obtained by differentiating through this exact inner solution, so that it
includes how the continuum and background respond to a change in
$\bm{\theta}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:nova}). In the middle loop, bounded
trust-region reflective least squares \citep{BranchColemanLi1999} minimises
this profiled objective over $\bm{\theta}$. In the outer loop, the Huber
weights $w_{tp}=\min(1,\,1.345/|r_{tp}|)$ \citep{Huber1964} are updated, and
they are held fixed during each trust-region solve.

Because the weights and the spectrum are updated in turn, a fit could stop
whilst one of them is still drifting, and so a fit is only accepted once the
weights, the objective and the spectrum have all settled, first-order
optimality is reached, and a final cycle with the recomputed weights
reproduces the solution (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). To see whether
the solution depends on where the optimiser starts, every analysis is run
from at least two starting points: one from the white-light depth with a flat
spectral shape, and one with a fixed perturbation of the shape and of the
limb darkening. I call a start strict if it passes all of these checks, and
among the strict starts the one with the lowest value of
Equation~\ref{eq:objective} is selected. The agreement between the starts is
reported but is not required for acceptance; in the cases run so far they agree to within about 0.01~ppm. For the pilot recoveries of the injector
(Section~\ref{sec:envelope}), a third start is added at the midpoint of the
first two.

\subsubsection{White-light geometry inference}
\label{sec:own-white}

% Question: Where do t0, a/R* and b come from?
% Answer: From NOVA's own joint two-order white-light fit on optimally
% weighted curves built from its own detector data, sampled with nested
% sampling through public Eureka!, accepted by a grazing-mass rule, and
% handed over as whole-posterior weighted medians.
% Evidence: source/nova_own_white_eureka.py, source/nova_own_white_geometry.py,
% source/v7_grazing_statistic_20260923.py; evidence/
% group_ownwhite_native_sources_r1/white_light.py; config/
% V7_GEOMETRY_GATE_REGISTRATION_20260923.json; reports/V7_CALIBRATION_RESULT_20260923.md;
% reports/SIZED_WHITE_CONTINUATION_RESULTS_20260921.json; public Ahsoka epf files.

The spectral fit holds the transit geometry fixed, and so the geometry must
be estimated first. NOVA infers $t_0$, $a/R_\star$ and the inclination $i$
from its own detector data, and hence $b=(a/R_\star)\cos i$ for a circular orbit, without using any
fitted value from another pipeline or any value from the injector. For
each order, I build a white-light curve by summing, over the detector
columns, a profile-weighted (optimal) estimate of the light in each column,
using an out-of-transit spatial profile from the same input. The two curves
are fitted jointly with the light-curve stage of the public Eureka! package
\citep{BellEtAl2022}, as two channels which share $t_0$, $a/R_\star$ and $i$,
whilst each order has its own radius ratio $k_o$, two quadratic
limb-darkening coefficients \citep{MandelAgol2002}, a linear baseline and a white-noise multiplier; the period, eccentricity and
argument of periastron are fixed as in the spectral fit. The priors on the
geometry are deliberately wide:
$t_0$ is uniform over the observed window, $a/R_\star$ uniform on
$[1.01,\,100]$ and $i$ uniform on $[0^\circ,\,90^\circ]$. The remaining priors are copied from the public Ahsoka configuration of
\citet{LouieEtAl2025} and use no fit to these data
(Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}).

The posterior is sampled with the nested sampler dynesty \citep{Speagle2020},
run through Eureka!. I use nested sampling for all injected inputs and
ensemble realisations because, on the inputs injected at the raw level, the
affine-invariant ensemble sampler emcee \citep{ForemanMackeyEtAl2013} did not
reach the required 50 autocorrelation lengths: some of its walkers became
stranded, and a later check found about 12\% of the samples of each failed
chain on grazing or non-transiting geometries.

Such geometries are the failure that matters here, and so a white-light fit
is only used if little of its posterior lies on them. For every posterior
sample, $b$ is computed from that sample's $a/R_\star$ and $i$, and the
sample counts as grazing or non-transiting if
\begin{equation}
 b > 1-\max(k_1,\,k_2),
 \label{eq:grazing}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ if the planet's disc is not wholly inside the stellar disc at
mid-transit. The grazing mass is the weighted fraction of such samples, with
an uncertainty estimated by resampling the nested-sampling run. A fit passes
if the upper end of its 95\% interval is below 1\%, and passes with its
grazing mass reported if it lies between 1 and 5\%; otherwise it fails. A failed fit is recorded as a failure: the run is not continued, reseeded or
trimmed, and no geometry from elsewhere is used in its place.

This rule has an important limitation: it is not blind to the real data. It
is the fifth revision of the acceptance criterion for the white-light
geometry, and I wrote it after the earlier versions had been abandoned and
after it was known that the real posterior contains no grazing samples of
positive weight. I did, however, fix it before testing it on saved
posteriors, where it had to reject the three emcee chains which had failed
their length assessment and to accept both the chains which had passed that
assessment and the nested posterior of the real visit.

When a fit is accepted, the weighted medians of $t_0$, $a/R_\star$, $i$ and
each $k_o$ over the whole posterior are passed to the spectral fit, which
starts from the mean white-light depth of the two orders, $10^6k_o^2$~ppm.
One caveat applies to the real visit: its accepted NOVA spectrum, which is
also the reference of the uncertainty ensemble below, was fitted at the geometry of an earlier accepted emcee fit. The later nested
fit of the real visit passes the rule above and reproduces that geometry
closely.

\subsubsection{The uncertainty ensemble}
\label{sec:ensemble}

% Question: How are NOVA's spectral uncertainties obtained and validated?
% Answer: By refitting from scratch on realisations built from the accepted
% real model plus resampled real residual blocks; the ensemble errors define
% a finite-ensemble Student likelihood that must pass held-out coverage.
% Evidence: config/MATCHED_COVARIANCE_ENSEMBLE_R1.json; products/
% generator_v2_20260923_v7_r2_package (REGISTRY.json, generator_v2_draw_20260922.py,
% pilot_noise.py); source/finite_ensemble_likelihood.py; source/
% covariance_validation.py; config/COVARIANCE_ANALYSIS_PROTOCOL_R1.json;
% evidence/reviewer_plan_reporting_rank_20260920_r1.json (rank 128).

The covariance which comes out of a single fit is conditional on its
calibration products, such as the spatial response and the geometry, which
are themselves uncertain. I therefore estimate the uncertainty of NOVA's
spectrum by repeating the whole analysis on simulated realisations of the
real visit,
\begin{equation}
 Y^{(n)}_{tp}=\widehat Y^{\mathrm{ref}}_{tp}+\bar r_p+a_p\,\psi_t
 +\mathrm{ERR}_{tp}\,z_{d_n(t),\,p},
 \label{eq:ensemble-draw}
\end{equation}
where $n$ labels the realisation, $\widehat Y^{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the accepted
final prediction for the real visit on the trace pixels, together with the
fitted mean background off the trace, and $\bar r_p$ is the mean, in each
pixel, of the real out-of-transit residual, which is added back so that any
static mismatch between the model and the real data is also present in the
realisations. The term $a_p\psi_t$ puts back the transit-shaped off-trace
component which the background model keeps out of the fit:
$\psi_t=\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{w}}_t-1$ is the transit decrement of the
accepted white-light fit, which is exactly zero outside the transit, and
$a_p$ is the amplitude obtained, in each off-trace pixel, by projecting the
component onto $\psi_t$; on the trace pixels, $a_p$ is the extension of the
background model under the trace, not a measured amplitude. The last term
adds real noise: the real ERR array multiplied by a centred, standardised
out-of-transit residual $z$ taken from a donor integration $d_n(t)$.

Real detector noise is correlated between neighbouring integrations, and so
the donors are whole frames drawn in blocks of 64 consecutive
integrations, each taken from within one of the two out-of-transit intervals
so that no block spans the transit. The ensemble contains 352 realisations,
each with a fixed seed and a fixed role. Of these, 256 are used to estimate
the covariance and 32 are held out to test it, whilst two further sets of 32
reuse the held-out seeds with longer blocks, of 128 and 192 integrations, to
check whether the result depends on the block length
(Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). Each realisation is analysed from
scratch, with its own spatial response, background pattern and anchor,
curvature factor and uncertainty scales, its own nested white-light fit, and
two strict starts. Because a realisation which fails may differ
systematically from one which succeeds, no failed realisation is replaced or
dropped: the covariance and its validation are only formed once all 256
estimation and all 32 held-out realisations have completed and passed their
checks, and otherwise the ensemble is reported as incomplete.

The error of a realisation is its recovered spectrum minus the reference
spectrum, i.e.\ the accepted NOVA spectrum of the real visit. The 147
reported bins are produced from the 160 native coefficients by the fixed map
of Section~\ref{sec:reporting}, which has rank 128, so the bin errors contain
only $N_{\mathrm{m}}=128$ independent modes, and I express each error in
these modes. From the $N=256$ estimation errors, I form the sample mean
$\bm{\mu}$ and the unbiased sample covariance $\bm{S}$ directly, without shrinkage or jitter
and keeping all 128 modes. Because $\bm{S}$ is itself estimated from a finite number of
realisations, a Gaussian likelihood with this covariance would be too narrow.
For the likelihood of a new error $\bm{x}$ I therefore use the multivariate
Student distribution of \citet{SellentinHeavens2016}, with an additional
factor $1+1/N$ because the mean $\bm{\mu}$ is also estimated, i.e.\ a
distribution centred on $\bm{\mu}$ with $N-N_{\mathrm{m}}$ degrees of freedom
and scale matrix
\begin{equation}
 \bm{\Sigma}=\left(1+\frac{1}{N}\right)\frac{N-1}{N-N_{\mathrm{m}}}\,\bm{S},
 \label{eq:finite-ensemble}
\end{equation}
so that $(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})^{\mathsf T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})/
N_{\mathrm{m}}$ follows an $F(N_{\mathrm{m}},\,N-N_{\mathrm{m}})$
distribution.

A likelihood is only useful for a retrieval if its error bars are honest, and
so it is tested on the 32 held-out realisations, which played no part in
estimating it. If the likelihood is right, about 95\% of their errors should
fall inside its 95\% region, and it fails the test if significantly fewer do.
The shape of the distribution of the estimation errors is also compared with
simulated Gaussian ensembles, as a warning sign rather than a condition
(Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). The likelihood is only used for
atmospheric retrieval if it passes.

\subsection{The injector}
\label{sec:injector}

% Question: How is a truth-known benchmark built from real JWST data?
% Answer: A known transit signal is written into the raw group ramps of real
% pre-transit integrations, dimming only modelled target starlight, so that
% detector processing, background and noise are real and the truth is known.
% Evidence: source/emit_original_A_group_segment.py, source/
% original_v45_group_signal.py, source/group_accumulated_transport.py;
% evidence/matrix_trip_bindings_r2.json.

The injector addresses the validation problem of
Section~\ref{sec:detector-to-validation}. Real observations come with no
ground truth, and so I create one by writing a transit with a known spectrum
into real JWST data. The transit is written into the raw detector ramps (the
uncal files), before any calibration, so that every recovery method can run
its own detector processing, including its own group-level correction of the $1/f$ noise, on the same
injected frames. The injector is used in two stages. In
the pilot, a few injected cases are recovered with NOVA to test the injector
and to measure how sensitive the recovered spectra are to its assumptions;
in production, a larger set of atmospheres is to be injected into each of six
visits and recovered by every method. The subsections below describe, in
turn, the real data which carry the signal, which light is dimmed, how the
transit is computed and written into the raw counts, the detector
processing, the checks that the signal was delivered, and how the recoveries
are run, compared and scored.

\subsubsection{Baseline and null}
\label{sec:baseline}

% Question: Which real data carry the injected signal, and what are the
% controls?
% Answer: The first 229 raw integrations of the WASP-17 visit, all before
% the real transit; the null is the same frames with nothing injected, and a
% rate-level control adds the final-read change after ramp fitting.
% Evidence: evidence/matrix_trip_bindings_r2.json (baseline_provenance);
% reports/PHYSICAL_MATCHED_NATIVE_NULL_20260918.md; products/
% w17_measured_raw_emission_20260923_v7_r3_prepared/source/
% emit_conditional_matched_rate_20260920.py.

The injected transit needs real data which contain no transit of their own.
For this I use the raw NIRISS/SOSS observation of WASP-17, restricted to its
first 229 integrations, which I refer to as the baseline. The real transit,
together with buffer integrations on either side of it, only begins at
integration 230, so every baseline integration was taken before it. Each
integration consists of eight nondestructive reads of the detector, about
5.5~s apart (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}).

Two controls are built from the same data. The first, which I call the null,
is the baseline passed through the identical detector processing with
nothing injected, and it shows what the processing does to the data alone.
The second adds the signal after ramp fitting instead of before it: the count
change accumulated by the final read, divided by its exposure time, is added
to the calibrated rate images of the null. Comparing recoveries from the two
injections shows what the group-level processing does to the injected
signal. The comparison also contains the effect of adding the signal as an
average over the whole integration, since a ramp fit to reads during which
the transit changes does not return exactly this average.

\subsubsection{Which light is dimmed}
\label{sec:dimmed-light}

% Question: Which photons transit?
% Answer: A map of modelled target starlight in three zones by distance from
% the traces: a fixed core, measured wings, and a far region injected at a
% fraction of the measured residual light, with two bracketing members and a
% measured per-column member.
% Evidence: products/light_based_family_20260921_r1/FAMILY.json and
% FAR_PIXEL_ACCOUNTING.json; products/full_light_outer_shape_controls_20260916_r1/
% SOURCE_ESTIMATE.json; products/full_photon_source_projection_diagnostic_r1/
% SOURCE_PROJECTION.npz; products/measured_per_column_20260922_r1/REGISTRATION.json;
% reports/EVENING_FAR_LIGHT_AND_WHITE_CHECKS_20260921.md.

In a real transit only the light of the target star is dimmed, and so the
injector needs a map of where on the detector the starlight of the target
falls. This map, which I call the source, gives the count rate of the target in
each pixel, in $\mathrm{DN\,s^{-1}}$ at a reference time, and it is divided into three zones according to the distance
$d$ of each pixel from the nearer of the two traces. In the core, within 16
pixels of a trace, the source is a projection of the stellar spectrum through
the public ATOCA model of the traces \citep{DarveauBernierEtAl2022},
calibrated on the same data. Further out, the source is measured directly from the data: it is the out-of-transit light which remains after background subtraction,
estimated without smoothing and constrained to be nonnegative, since
background subtraction leaves some pixels negative, whilst the total light of
each band of columns is preserved (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). The wings, between 16 and 80 pixels, take this
estimate as it stands.

In the far region, more than 80 pixels from the traces, it is not known how
much of the remaining light belongs to the target and how much to the sky,
and so this light is injected at a fraction $m$ of the measured field. This
defines a family of injectors which differ only in $m$. Two members bracket
the possibilities, one which injects none of the far light ($m=0$) and one
which injects all of it ($m=1$), and a third, midpoint member ($m=0.5$) is
only used in the delivery check. Pixels which are not eligible for the
measured estimate, for example because they are contaminated by field stars
identified in the F277W exposure, keep the modelled light of the core
projection (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}).

A fourth member uses a measured far-light fraction. The idea is that far
light which belongs to the star dims by the same fraction as the star during
the transit, whereas the sky does not, so if all of the far-light dimming
came from starlight, the ratio of the far-light dip to the transit depth
would equal the stellar share of that light. On the calibrated images of the
full real visit, the far pixels do dim during the real transit, by 0.834\% on
average. I measure this dip in each detector column, divide it by the mean fractional
dimming of the star in the white-light model over the same integrations, a
single value used for every column, average the ratio over
128 columns and bound it to $[0,1]$. The resulting multiplier $m_x$, where
$x$ denotes the detector column, has a median of 0.606, and in total this
member injects 61.5\% of the measured far light. Because $m_x$ is measured
after the $1/f$ correction of the pipeline, which may imprint on the far-row
statistic, it is treated as an upper bound on the stellar fraction of the
far light, pending a repeat of the measurement before that correction, and
not as a measurement of it.

Each zone also needs a description of how its light is spread in wavelength
and between the two orders. The core keeps the wavelength and order
structure of its projection, whilst the wings and the far region take theirs
from a separate model of the outer light, rescaled pixel by pixel to the
measured map. The stellar spectrum of the core varies linearly in time between two
calibrated spectra (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}).

\subsubsection{The per-read transit signal}
\label{sec:per-read}

% Question: How is the transit computed?
% Answer: With ExoTETHyS TRIP on MPS-ATLAS intensities, averaged over each of
% the eight reset-to-read windows of every integration, at the control-file
% orbit with a/R* rescaled so that the transit fits the baseline.
% Evidence: products/original_A_causal_validation_trip_reference_r1_package/
% source/run_original_v45_group_trip.py; source/B_mixed_native_trip.py;
% evidence/matrix_trip_bindings_r2.json (geometry); config/
% COMPARATOR_SHARED_GEOMETRY_PRIORS_20260923_R1.json (t0 prior).

To write a transit into the data, the injector first needs the fraction of
the star's light which remains visible, $T(\lambda,\tau)$, at every
wavelength $\lambda$ and time $\tau$. For the injected spectrum $D(\lambda)$
the radius ratio at each wavelength is $\sqrt{D(\lambda)}$, and $T$ is
computed with the TRIP routine of ExoTETHyS \citep{MorelloEtAl2020}, which
integrates the occulted light over thin annuli of the stellar disc. The
stellar intensities come from the MPS-ATLAS grid (set~1)
\citep{KostogryzEtAl2022,KostogryzEtAl2023} at the stellar parameters of
WASP-17.

The injected orbit keeps the period, the circular orbit and the impact
parameter ($b\simeq0.339$) of the public control file of the published
WASP-17~b analysis \citep{LouieEtAl2025}. The real transit of WASP-17~b,
however, lasts about 4.3~h and would not fit into the 229-integration
baseline, and so $a/R_\star$ and the inclination are rescaled, with $b$ held
fixed, until the transit lasts about 1.74~h, with mid-transit at the centre
of the baseline. None of the recovery methods is given the rescaled
$a/R_\star$ or inclination. Note, however, that the recoveries share the period, eccentricity and argument of periastron of
the injected orbit, and that the prior on $t_0$ of every white-light fit is
centred on, and started at, the middle of the observed window, which
is the injected mid-transit time by construction.

Each read of an integration records the charge accumulated since the
detector was reset, and so read $n$ of integration $t$ sees the transit
averaged over the window from the reset to the end of that read. The
transit is therefore averaged over each of these eight nested windows, by
Gauss--Legendre quadrature, and stored in precomputed transit tables. For
each integration, read and wavelength, a table holds the window mean
$\langle T\rangle_{tn}(\lambda)$ and the first time moment about the
integration midpoint $\tau_t$,
$\mu_{tn}(\lambda)=\langle(\tau-\tau_t)(T-1)\rangle_{tn}$; the moment is
needed because the stellar source itself changes during the integration
(Section~\ref{sec:transport}). The tables are checked against
independently computed transit averages over whole integrations, and
integration~0, the only one whose reads extend beyond the time range of the
stellar calibration, must carry no transit signal at all
(Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). On the 229-integration baseline, the
grey signal used in the delivery check affects integrations 51 to 177.
Because the contact times depend on the radius ratio, wavelengths at which
an atmospheric spectrum is deeper are occulted slightly earlier and for
slightly longer, and so an atmospheric transit affects integrations 50 to
178 and leaves the other 100 unaffected.

\subsubsection{Transport to raw counts}
\label{sec:transport}

% Question: How does the signal enter the raw ramps?
% Answer: As an accumulated count change per read, projected to the
% detector, returned to unflattened counts, and written as floats through the
% inverse of the pipeline's linearity correction via an affine bridge.
% Evidence: source/original_v45_group_signal.py; source/
% group_accumulated_transport.py; config/DEVELOPMENT_GROUP_TRANSPORT_R1.json;
% NOVA_SP_MULTIVISIT_SCENE_ROBUSTNESS_20260826_R1/source/
% stage3b_detector_transport_r2.py; stage3b_float_sci_persistence_adapter_r2.py.

For a source which varies linearly in time, the count change accumulated by
read $n$ of integration $t$ at wavelength $\lambda$ is the integral of
$S\,(T-1)$ over the read window, and its projection onto the detector gives
the change in the counts of pixel $p$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
 \Delta N_{tn}(\lambda) = {}& s_n\big[S_t(\lambda)\,
 \big(\langle T\rangle_{tn}(\lambda)-1\big) \\
 &\quad +\dot S(\lambda)\,\mu_{tn}(\lambda)\big], \\
 \Delta Y^{\mathrm{raw}}_{tnp} = {}& f_p
 \sum_{o}\big[\bm{A}_o\,\Delta N_{tn}\big]_p ,
\end{split}
 \label{eq:v45-injector}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta N_{tn}(\lambda)$ is the count change at wavelength $\lambda$
before projection, $\Delta Y^{\mathrm{raw}}_{tnp}$ is the resulting change in
the linearised counts of pixel $p$ before flat-fielding, $s_n=5.494\,n$~s is
the accumulated exposure time of read $n$, $S_t$ is the stellar spectrum at
the midpoint of the integration, $\dot S$ is its rate of change, $\bm{A}_o$
is the detector projection of order $o$, and $f_p$ is the flat-field
coefficient of pixel $p$. The projection $\bm{A}_o$ combines the
spectral-response matrix of the public ATOCA kernel, a wing correction and
the calibrations of the source map; it is applied separately to the core and
outer components, each with its own spectrum, and the results are summed.
The projection gives the loss in flat-fielded counts, and multiplying by the
effective flat $f_p$ of the pipeline's flat-field step converts it back. The
count change is zero on the reference pixels and must be nonpositive in every pixel, since a transit can only remove
light; otherwise the injection stops.

The injected change is defined in linearised counts, whereas the raw ramp
records counts before the linearity correction, and so the change has to be
passed through the inverse of that correction. To do this, the raw counts are
first carried to the input of the pipeline's linearity step. The steps
before it only add fixed offsets to each sample, so this bridge is affine,
and it remains valid because the injection never changes the reference
pixels. Newton's method then solves the pipeline's linearity polynomial for
the input value whose linearised value equals the original one plus
$\Delta Y^{\mathrm{raw}}$, and the inverse bridge maps this value back to
the raw sample. The modified ramps are written as floating-point values, so
the injected change is not rounded to integer counts. Samples which are
saturated keep their original values, and the part of the decrement which
could not be delivered is recorded. Everything else in the raw data, i.e.\
the observed noise, background and field stars, is left untouched. The
tolerances of each step are given in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\subsubsection{Detector processing}
\label{sec:detector-processing}

% Question: What happens to the injected ramps before recovery?
% Answer: NOVA's input runs jwst group steps to refpix, exoTEDRF 2.4.3
% group-level background and 1/f steps, jwst linearity/jump/ramp/gain, then
% exoTEDRF Stage 2 (background, flat and bad-pixel steps); each comparison
% pipeline runs its own processing from the same raw frames.
% Evidence: source/run_nova_original_A_group_detector.py (lines 55-100);
% reports/PHYSICAL_MATCHED_NATIVE_NULL_20260918.md (exoTEDRF 2.4.3); evidence/
% native_null_completion_20260918_r1.json.

NOVA's input is prepared from the injected raw segments with the same
processing chain as for the real data, which combines public steps from the
JWST calibration pipeline \citep{JWSTPipeline2025} and from exoTEDRF
\citep{Radica2024}. Firstly, the pipeline's group-level steps are run up to
and including the reference-pixel correction. Secondly, exoTEDRF removes the
$1/f$ noise at group level: it temporarily subtracts a scaled model of the
zodiacal background so that the stripes can be estimated, removes them, and
adds the background back. Thirdly, the pipeline's linearity, jump-detection,
ramp-fitting and gain steps turn the ramps into count rates. Finally, Stage~2
of exoTEDRF subtracts the scaled background model, applies the flat field
and corrects bad pixels, giving the rate images which NOVA fits. The $1/f$
step masks the field stars identified in the F277W exposure, and I apply no
further scene mask of my own. On the injected inputs, the background and
$1/f$ steps take the first and last 50 integrations as their out-of-transit
reference, which are exactly the integrations that the injected transit
leaves unaffected. The null follows exactly the same chain. Each comparison
pipeline instead takes the same injected raw frames and runs its own
detector processing from the raw ramps onwards, including its own group-level treatment of the $1/f$ noise
(Section~\ref{sec:comparison}).

\subsubsection{Delivery checks}

% Question: How is it known that the requested signal reached the data?
% Answer: Checks on the written files, an exact aperture-sum check inside the
% 1/f step, and a grey linearity test across the family.
% Evidence: reports/LIGHT_BASED_GREY_DELIVERY_RESULT_20260921.md; reports/
% MEASURED_GREY_DELIVERY_RESULT_20260922.md; source/
% run_fixed_family_delivery_report_20260920.py; stage3b_float_sci_persistence_adapter_r2.py.

Before an injection is used, I check that it changed only what it was meant
to change. The written files must have the expected number of reads, every
count change must be nonpositive, and every pixel outside the support of the
injected light, including the reference pixels, must be identical to the
input bit for bit. During the detector processing, the light in each order
and column is also recorded before and after the $1/f$ step, so that its
effect on the injected signal can be measured.

The family is then tested with a grey signal, i.e.\ a constant depth of
16{,}000~ppm at every wavelength, injected under each of the three far-light
assumptions and processed with the chain above. The concern is that the
$1/f$ correction, which is estimated partly from the far rows, might respond
to the far light in a non-linear way, so that results obtained with one
member could not be interpolated to another. For each order and column, I
measure how much of the injected signal survives the $1/f$ step, i.e.\ its
gain $g_m$. If the response is linear in the far-light fraction, the gain at
$m=0.5$ lies on the straight line between the gains at $m=0$ and $m=1$, and
the check requires the largest departure from this line, expressed as an
equivalent depth, to be below 10~ppm in every order and column, within fixed wavelength ranges
of the two orders, in which the signal is measurable
(Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). The member with the measured fraction is checked in
the same way, at its own $m_x$. Passing this check only shows that the signal
is delivered consistently across the family; whether the far light actually
comes from the star remains open.

\subsubsection{Carrying the far-light uncertainty into the results}
\label{sec:envelope}

% Question: How is the far-light uncertainty carried into a result?
% Answer: The reference atmosphere is recovered by NOVA at both bracketing
% members; the spread between its two raw-level recoveries decides how the
% family's results are described, not whether they are released.
% Evidence: products/light_based_family_20260921_r1/FAMILY.json
% (endpoint_criterion, chromatic.cases); reports/
% PHYSICAL_INJECTOR_PROTOCOL_20260918.md section 5; reports/
% PHYSICAL_EMISSION_RUNTIME_AND_ARMS_20260918.md; reports/
% SOURCE_MAP_A_DECISION_20260920_R2.json.

Because the stellar share of the far light is not known, its effect is
carried into the results as an envelope. In the pilot, a reference
atmosphere, a member of the generic ATMO grid of transmission spectra
\citep{GoyalEtAl2019} rescaled to WASP-17~b, is injected under both
bracketing assumptions, at both the raw and the rate level, and recovered
with NOVA. The envelope is the root-mean-square difference between its two
raw-level recoveries over the 147 reporting bins. A second atmosphere from the same grid, with different chemistry, is also
recovered, but only at the raw level and only at $m=1$, and so it does not
contribute to the envelope. Each
pilot case gets its own calibration products, its own white-light geometry
and three strict starts; the parameters of both atmospheres are given in
Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

If the envelope is 10~ppm or less, I describe results obtained with the
family as validated within the scope of the source family, in the limited
sense that NOVA's spectra under the two bracketing assumptions differ by at
most 10~ppm root-mean-square; if it is larger, I describe them as usable and
quote the envelope alongside them. The envelope changes how results are
described, but no result is withheld because of it. The two endpoints
bracket the far-light assumption and are not confidence bounds. The envelope
also measures only how sensitive NOVA is to this assumption; it says
nothing about the accuracy of the core map, and the comparison pipelines,
whose own processing may respond differently to the far light, are not
covered by it. The threshold of 10~ppm is a design choice, made as a budget
for an intended claim about differences of 30~ppm between pipelines.

In production, each atmosphere will be recovered once, with the
measured-fraction member, and its results will be described according to the
envelope measured in the pilot. There are 24 atmospheres per visit, drawn from
the same grid, twelve with local condensation and twelve with rainout
chemistry, and each is fitted with two strict starts. The programme contains
six visits, of WASP-17, HAT-P-26, WASP-39, HAT-P-18, WASP-96 and WASP-80. A
source map which works for WASP-17 need not work for another visit, and so
each visit only enters production once its own white-light geometry has been
accepted and its own maps pass the checks applied to WASP-17: the core map
must not exceed the light observed in the aperture, the wing map must be
stable between two halves of the data, and the family must pass the grey
delivery check (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). Any exception to these
rules is declared and reported with the results.

\subsubsection{Comparison pipelines and fairness}
\label{sec:comparison}

% Question: Against what is NOVA compared, and on what terms?
% Answer: The three pipelines of the published WASP-17 b analysis, run end to
% end on the same injected raw frames as their authors run them, each with its
% own white-light inference; only the geometry priors are shared.
% Evidence: reports/COMPARATOR_AUTHOR_PROTOCOL_AUTHORIZATION_20260923.md;
% config/COMPARATOR_SHARED_GEOMETRY_PRIORS_20260923_R1.json; reports/
% TRANSITSPECTROSCOPY_JOINT_WHITE_AUTHORIZATION_20260922.md; reports/
% TRANSITSPECTROSCOPY_REMAINING_CONFIGURATION_20260922.md; reports/
% TS_AUTHOR_WHITE_COMPLETE_LD_SUPPORT_FAILURE_20260923.md.

NOVA is compared with current public versions of the three pipelines used in
the published analysis of WASP-17~b \citep{LouieEtAl2025}: the Ahsoka
analysis, \texttt{transitspectroscopy} 0.3.12
\citep{Espinoza2022TransitSpectroscopy} and exoTEDRF 2.5.0 \citep{Radica2024},
the current release of supreme-SPOON. Because exoTEDRF 2.5.0 is the current
release, its runs are not an exact replication of the paper-era
supreme-SPOON reduction. A
comparison is only fair if each pipeline is run as its authors run it, and so
each is run end to end on the same injected raw frames, with its own
sampler, run length and acceptance criteria (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}).
Ahsoka fits the two orders separately with the \texttt{emcee} sampler
\citep{ForemanMackeyEtAl2013} in Eureka! \citep{BellEtAl2022}, exoTEDRF fits
a joint two-order white-light curve with its own nested sampler, and
\texttt{transitspectroscopy} extracts a box aperture and fits its light
curves with \texttt{juliet} \citep{EspinozaEtAl2019}.

Two further departures from the published configurations are needed.
Firstly, \citet{LouieEtAl2025} fitted the white-light curves of the two
orders separately in \texttt{transitspectroscopy}, whereas here the two are
fitted jointly, following the package author's own joint two-order
implementation, and the settings which the paper leaves open are fixed in
advance. One of these, the limb-darkening grid, had to be changed: the
STAGGER grid has no models at this star's gravity above 6500~K, which
ExoTETHyS needs in order to interpolate, so the spectral stage of the first
run failed after its white-light fit, and the grid was replaced by
MPS-ATLAS set~2 before any spectral estimate or score existed. NOVA avoids
this problem by adopting the nearest complete STAGGER plane. MPS-ATLAS set~2
comes from the same model family as the intensities of the injected transit,
so, unlike NOVA's, the limb-darkening reference of
\texttt{transitspectroscopy} is closely related to that of the injected
signal. Secondly, the published priors describe the real planet: those of
Ahsoka, for example, place the mid-transit time about 2.5~h after the end of
the injected window. In every white-light fit, the priors on $t_0$,
$a/R_\star$ and $i$ are therefore set to the wide priors which NOVA uses, so
that all four methods infer the geometry under the same assumptions.

No pipeline receives the rescaled injected geometry or the fit of another
pipeline. The injector and NOVA do, however, use the same public trace and
wavelength calibration and the same ATOCA spectral-response kernel, so an
error in these would be written into the injected data and assumed again in
NOVA's recovery, and the benchmark could not detect it. For each comparison
pipeline I also compute my own convergence statistics, which are reported
for information only and are not used to reject any of its results, and
failed runs are reported alongside the successful ones.

\subsubsection{Reporting support and evaluation metrics}
\label{sec:reporting}

% Question: On what wavelength support are methods compared, and with which
% metrics?
% Answer: 147 whole bins of a common R=100 grid inside the validated
% wavelength calibration, scored by RMSE, mean bias and shape RMS against the
% unweighted bin average of the injected spectrum.
% Evidence: products/w17_frozen_postclosure_score_20260923_r1_prepared/source/
% score_w17_frozen147.py, original_score_reference.py and
% original_truth_averaging.py; config/CASE_TEMPLATE_UNBOUND.json; source/
% run_aperture_terminal_covariance_r1.py; AGENTS.md (8 September decision).

Each method reports its spectrum on its own wavelength grid, and so all
spectra are compared on a common grid of bins at a resolving power of
$R=100$. NOVA's 160 coefficients are mapped onto it by a fixed map in which
each bin takes the mean of the coefficients it overlaps, weighted by the
length of the overlap. The reddest bins lie beyond the range over which the
public wavelength calibration is validated, where it is only an
extrapolation, and they are excluded, which leaves 147 bins ending at
$2.740\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). The same bins are used for every method, only at the reporting stage, and no
estimate is interpolated or filled in: a
method which has no estimate in one of them is recorded as a failure and is
not scored. The injected depth in each bin, $D_i^{\mathrm{inj}}$, is the
unweighted average of $D(\lambda)$ over the bin.

I score each recovery with the root-mean-square error
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{RMSE}=
 \left(\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{b}}}\sum_i e_i^2\right)^{1/2}
 \label{eq:absolute-rmse}
\end{equation}
and the shape RMS
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{RMS}_{\mathrm{shape}}=
 \left[\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{b}}}\sum_i(e_i-\bar e)^2\right]^{1/2},
 \label{eq:morphology-rmse}
\end{equation}
where $e_i=\widehat D_i-D_i^{\mathrm{inj}}$ is the difference between the
recovered depth $\widehat D_i$ and the injected depth in retained bin $i$,
$N_{\mathrm{b}}$ is the number of retained bins, and $\bar e$, the mean of
the $e_i$, is the mean bias, which I also report together with the maximum
absolute error. Since $\mathrm{RMSE}^2=\bar e^{\,2}+\mathrm{RMS}_{\mathrm{shape}}^2$,
these scores separate a constant offset from an error in the shape of the
spectrum, and no offset correction is applied to any reported spectrum. The
same scores are also given on six contiguous wavelength ranges and on two
summary ranges, below 2.1~$\mu\mathrm{m}$ and from 2.12 to
2.76~$\mu\mathrm{m}$, which separate the red end of the spectrum, where
earlier comparisons showed the largest differences.

The injected spectrum is only compared with a recovery once that recovery's
estimates in all 147 bins have been recorded, so that it cannot influence any
choice of calibration, model or starting point. These choices depend only on
the data, the instrument reference files, the published priors and theory
references given above, and the products carried over from the earlier
release (Appendix~\ref{app:benchmarking}). There are two exceptions, both of
which coincide with the design of the injection, although neither uses the
injected spectrum: the out-of-transit reference integrations of the
background and $1/f$ steps (Section~\ref{sec:detector-processing}), and the
centre of the prior on $t_0$ (Section~\ref{sec:per-read}). For NOVA's
spectrum of the real visit, the uncertainty which I quote is the one from the
ensemble of Section~\ref{sec:ensemble}, once it has passed its validation.
The ensemble simulates the full 720-integration visit, and so the injected
recoveries are instead reported with the covariance of their fit conditional
on the calibration products, together with their standardised residuals and
their weight, conditioning and convergence diagnostics. For the comparison pipelines, their own
reported uncertainties are used, and these are not validated in the same
way.
