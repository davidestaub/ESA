\section{A Benchmark with Measured Starlight}
\label{sec:measured-benchmark}

In this section, I describe the benchmark that I am now building. It uses a
calibration programme in which the star was observed twice, once at its usual
position and once moved off the part of the detector read for SOSS time
series. The difference between the two exposures measures the light of the
star, which greatly reduces the reliance of the injector on a fitted split
between starlight and background.

\subsection{Measuring the star}
\label{sec:measuring-star}

STScI programme 4476 calibrates the SOSS aperture correction. On 26 March
2024 it observed TYC 4213-1116-1, an A-type star giving about 1.6 times more
light per detector column than WASP-17, in two full-frame exposures taken
about 25 minutes apart. In the first, the star sits at its usual position on
the detector, and the exposure has ten integrations of five groups. For the
second, the telescope moved by $67.1''$, which placed the traces 1,026 rows
lower. The strip normally read for SOSS time series then contained no light
from the target apart from its far halo, and this exposure has 150
integrations covering 2.68~h.

On this strip, the difference between the two exposures gives the light of
the star. Everything that is the same in both exposures cancels. Note,
however, that this does not apply to the realised read noise, to offsets that
vary in time or to residual calibration errors. The estimated scales of the
zodiacal background in the two exposures agree to within about 1\%. The faint
halo of the moved star still reaches the strip and is subtracted with it, but
its modelled contribution to the depth of a 1.5\% transit is only 2 to
19~ppm, depending on wavelength, and it can be restored from a model. The
other stars in the field do not cancel, since they moved with the telescope.

From this difference I construct a declared image of the star, $S$, and add
it with a known transit to the raw data of the moved exposure, $O(t)$. For
the first, grey case, every pixel of the star follows the same light curve
$r(t)$, which equals one out of transit, and the expected count rate is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{E}\bigl[D(t)\bigr] = \mathrm{E}\bigl[O(t)\bigr] + S\,r(t),
  \label{eq:4476}
\end{equation}
where $D(t)$ is the count rate of the benchmark data. Everything in $O(t)$ is
real, including the sky, the field stars, the $1/f$ noise and the cosmic
rays, and none of it transits. In practice, photon arrivals from $S$ will be
drawn for successive time intervals and accumulated into the reads, in the
linear domain of the detector, before its nonlinearity is applied. No light
will be added to pixels that do not respond to it. The transit case keeps
only a fraction of the same arrivals, whilst a twin of every case keeps them
all, so the two are identical on every integration that has lost no light to
the transit since its reset (Figure~\ref{fig:4476-construction}). The
injected light curve is therefore known exactly, given the declared image of
the star. How faithfully this image
represents the physical star is a separate question, which the next
subsections address. Atmospheres with spectral features need a declared
assignment of each pixel's light to an order and a wavelength, and I leave
them until the grey case has passed its tests.

\subsection{Cleaning the image of the star}
\label{sec:cleaning-star}

Figure~\ref{fig:4476} shows the two exposures and the cleaned star. Because
the field moved with the telescope, the difference contains the field stars
of the first pointing as positive sources, which would transit together with
the target, and those of the second pointing as negative ones. I located them
in each exposure separately. The 13 sources of the first pointing were
replaced in $S$ by the profile of the star in neighbouring columns. For the
18 sources of the second pointing, $S$ uses a local estimate of the
background in place of the second exposure, so that their light is not
subtracted from the star. These sources remain in $O(t)$, where they are real
light that does not transit, as in any real observation. The largest of them
is the spectrum of a field star, which crosses order 1 between columns 484
and 1238 with up to 0.77\% of the flux in a column. At $1.874$ to
$1.893\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, it would dilute a 1.5\% transit by 96~ppm in a fixed
extraction box 40 rows wide with ideal sky subtraction
(Figure~\ref{fig:4476-dilution}), and the pipelines have to account for it as they would in
real data.

This source is consistent with the second order of a star with $G=17.7$ in
the Gaia catalogue \citep{GaiaDR3}. After registration to the measured
displacement of the target, its median separation from the predicted trace
is 1.8 detector rows over the 26 columns checked. The 13 compact sources of
the first pointing, however, had no secure Gaia association under this
projection, so their nature remains undetermined, and they were located
directly in the images. Field stars lying
exactly under the traces of the target move with it, and these two exposures
cannot separate them. Along the traces, the residual scatter is 2.4 to 2.9\%
per pixel and shows no compact excess beyond the sources already removed.

The first exposure has only ten integrations, so the faint wings of the star
are noisy. The signal-to-noise ratio per pixel is between 112 and 675 in the
cores of the traces, but only 2 to 3 at 40 to 200 rows from order 1 in the
red. Beyond 40 rows, I therefore replaced the pixels by a running median along
each trace over 61 columns, blended with the data between 30 and 40 rows.
Relative to the measured pixels, this model differs on average by about
0.16\% at 40 to 100 rows. Figure~\ref{fig:4476-profiles} shows that, after
the subtraction, the light of the star is measured well into its wings,
where it is comparable to or fainter than the background. Near the traces, $S$ keeps the measured image, with
its noise and any field light hidden under the traces. Both are part of the
injected star, so they do not change the injected light curve.

\subsection{Tests before any pipeline is scored}
\label{sec:tests}

Before the pipelines are run, the injected data must pass three tests.
Firstly, injecting nothing must return the calibrated ramps and slopes of the
moved exposure unchanged. Secondly, each case and its twin must be identical
on every integration that has lost no light to the transit. Finally, the
noise added with the star must follow photon statistics. I then plan to score
each method at two levels. Because every case has a twin, the first level is
the difference between the extracted light of a case and that of its twin,
projected onto the injected light curve. It requires no fitted transit
geometry and no limb-darkening prior. The second level is the spectrum
recovered by each pipeline's own transit fit. A pipeline that fails to
recover the injected transit is itself a result.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:benchmark-limits}

The benchmark has clear limitations. Firstly, the star and its field are not
those of WASP-17. The star is brighter and the sky is fainter, at about
2.9~DN\,s$^{-1}$ per pixel compared with 4 to 6~DN\,s$^{-1}$ for WASP-17, so
dilution by background light is a smaller problem than in the WASP-17 data.
Since the star is of A type, its second and third orders are also brighter
relative to the first, which changes the overlap of the orders that the
pipelines must handle. Secondly, the exposures are full frames read through
four outputs with 10.7~s frames, whereas SOSS time series read one output
with 5.5~s frames. After the strip's own reference-pixel correction, the
variance of the noise per read is within 6 to 9\% of that of the SOSS
subarray. For example, the $1/f$ noise per read is 4.92~DN, compared with
4.81~DN for WASP-17. An integration has at most five groups, against eight
for WASP-17, and lasts 64~s rather than 49~s. At five groups the brightest
pixels reach 89\% of saturation, and the saturation rule of one pipeline then
flags a quarter of the light in the extraction box and changes those flags
during the transit. I therefore build versions with the first three groups,
where this no longer happens, and the first four, where it still affects
0.8\% of the light in the box.

Some effects are absent by construction. The injected star does not move, so
the jitter and breathing of a real trace are missing, and no electronic
response that follows the total illumination is present during the transit.
The added photons are drawn independently in each pixel, so the coupling of
their noise between neighbouring pixels is omitted. The injected ramps of the
brightest pixels also do not curve over at high fill as real ramps do. They
lie 130 to 200~DN above the real ramps in the first group, although the rates
agree to 0.1\%. Finally, the injected limb darkening follows one quadratic
law computed from PHOENIX models over a declared passband, which none of the
pipelines uses natively.
The star is also hotter than the limb-darkening grid from which two of the
pipelines take their reference. Their own fits therefore carry a known
mismatch. The first level of the scoring avoids a fitted limb-darkening
prior, although its result can still depend on the injected light curve. In
a control, every pipeline will use the injected coefficients in both its
white-light and its spectral fits, and each native configuration will state
how it treats a star outside its grid. The injected law is therefore a
specified challenge for the pipelines.

These limitations restrict what the benchmark can show, but they do not
change the injected light curve. The benchmark has one star, and the six
science visits of WASP-17, HAT-P-26, WASP-39, HAT-P-18, WASP-96 and WASP-80
cannot be turned into benchmarks in the same way, since their starlight
cannot be separated from the background (Section~\ref{sec:which-light}). I
will therefore analyse them as real data with every method and report where
the methods disagree. Without a known truth these differences cannot be
assigned to one method. The benchmark is designed to test, under the declared
injected conditions, which kind of error each method makes.
