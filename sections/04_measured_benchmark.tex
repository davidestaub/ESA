\section{A Benchmark with Measured Starlight}
\label{sec:measured-benchmark}

The benchmark I am now building uses a calibration programme that
observed a star twice, once at its usual position and once moved off the part
of the detector read for SOSS time series. The difference between the two
exposures measures the light of the star, which greatly reduces the reliance
of the injector on a fitted split between starlight and background.

\subsection{Measuring the star}
\label{sec:measuring-star}

STScI programme 4476 calibrates the SOSS aperture correction \citep{VolkEspinoza2023}. On 26 March 2024
it observed TYC 4213-1116-1, an A-type star giving about 1.6 times more light
per detector column than WASP-17, in two full-frame exposures taken about 25
minutes apart. The first, with ten integrations of five groups, has the star at
its usual position. For the second, the telescope moved by $67.1''$,
placing the traces 1,026 rows lower. The strip normally read for SOSS time series then received no light
from the target except its far halo, and this exposure has 150 integrations covering 2.68~h.

On this strip, everything common to both exposures cancels in their
difference. The estimated scales of the zodiacal background in the two agree
to within about 1\%. However, the realised read noise, time-varying offsets
and residual calibration errors do not cancel. The faint halo of the
moved star still reaches the strip and is subtracted with it. Its modelled
contribution to the depth of a 1.5\% transit, in a 40-row extraction box with
ideal sky subtraction, is only 2 to 19~ppm, depending on wavelength, and can
be restored from a model. Field stars do not cancel either, since they moved
with the telescope.

To use this difference in an injection, I remove the field sources
and smooth the noisy far wings as described below, obtaining a cleaned
count-rate image of the star, $S$. From $S$ I generate starlight with a known transit and
add it to the raw reads of the moved exposure. For the first, grey case,
every pixel of the star follows the same light curve $r(t)$, which equals one
out of transit, and the expected count rate is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{E}\bigl[D(t)\bigr] = \mathrm{E}\bigl[O(t)\bigr] + S\,r(t),
  \label{eq:4476}
\end{equation}
where $D(t)$ and $O(t)$ are the count rates of the benchmark data and of the
moved exposure. Everything in $O(t)$ is real, including the sky, the field
stars, the $1/f$ noise and the cosmic rays, and none of it transits.


In practice, photon arrivals from $S$ will be drawn for successive time
intervals and accumulated into the reads in the linear domain of the detector,
before its nonlinearity is applied. No light will be added to pixels that do
not respond to it. The transit case keeps only a fraction of the same
arrivals, whilst a twin of every case keeps them all, so the two are identical
on every integration that has lost no light to the transit since its reset
(Figure~\ref{fig:4476-construction}). The injected light curve is therefore known exactly, given the cleaned image of the star. Atmospheres with spectral features need a declared assignment of each pixel's light to an order and a wavelength, and I leave them until the grey case has passed its tests. How faithfully the image represents the
physical star is a separate question, which the next subsections address.

\subsection{Cleaning the image of the star}
\label{sec:cleaning-star}

The first concern is the field. Figure~\ref{fig:4476} shows the two exposures
and the cleaned star. Because the field moved with the telescope, the difference contains the field stars of the first
pointing as positive sources, which would transit together with the target,
and those of the second pointing as negative ones. I located them in each
exposure separately. The 13 sources of the first pointing were replaced in $S$
by the profile of the star in neighbouring columns. For the 18 sources of the
second pointing, $S$ uses a local estimate of the background in place of the
second exposure, so that their light is not subtracted from the star. These
remain in $O(t)$ as real light that does not transit, as in any real
observation. The largest is the spectrum of a field star crossing order 1
between columns 484 and 1238 with up to 0.77\% of the flux in a column. At
$1.874$ to $1.893\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, it would dilute a 1.5\% transit by 96~ppm
in a fixed extraction box 40 rows wide with ideal sky subtraction
(Figure~\ref{fig:4476-dilution}), and the pipelines must account for it as in
real data.

This source is consistent with the second order of a star with $G=17.7$ in the
Gaia catalogue \citep{GaiaDR3}. After registration to the measured
displacement of the target, its median separation from the predicted trace is
1.8 detector rows over the 26 columns checked. The 13 compact sources of the
first pointing, however, had no secure Gaia association under this projection,
so their nature remains undetermined, and they were located directly in the
images. Field stars lying exactly under the traces of the target move with it,
and these two exposures cannot separate them. Along the traces, the residual
scatter is 2.4 to 2.9\% per pixel and shows no compact excess beyond the
sources already removed.

The second concern is the faint wings. Figure~\ref{fig:4476-profiles} shows
that, after the subtraction, the light of the star is measured well into its
wings, where it is comparable to or fainter than the background. With only ten
integrations in the first exposure, however, these wings are noisy. The
signal-to-noise ratio per pixel is between 112 and 675 in the cores of the
traces, but only 2 to 3 at 40 to 200 rows from order 1 in the red. Beyond 40
rows from the nearest trace, I therefore replaced the pixels by a running
median along each trace over 61 columns, blended with the data between 30 and
40 rows. Relative to the measured pixels, this model differs on average by
about 0.16\% at 40 to 100 rows. Near the traces, $S$ keeps the measured image,
with its noise and any field light hidden beneath them. Both are part of the
injected star, so they do not change the injected light curve.

\subsection{Tests before any pipeline is scored}
\label{sec:tests}

Before the pipelines are run, the injected data must pass three tests.
Firstly, injecting nothing must return the calibrated ramps and slopes of the
moved exposure unchanged. Secondly, each case and its twin must be identical
on every integration that has lost no light to the transit since its reset.
Finally, the noise added with the star must follow photon statistics.

I then plan to score each method at two levels. Because every case has a twin,
the first level is the difference between the extracted light of a case and
of its twin, projected onto the injected light curve. It requires no fitted
transit geometry and no limb-darkening prior. The second level is the spectrum
recovered by each pipeline's own transit fit. A pipeline that fails to recover
the injected transit is itself a result.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:benchmark-limits}

Even if the injected data pass these three tests, the recovery errors measured at either scoring level apply to the star and detector
conditions of the benchmark, which has clear limitations. Firstly,
the star and its field are not those of WASP-17. The star is brighter and the
sky is fainter, at about 2.9~DN\,s$^{-1}$ per pixel compared with 4 to
6~DN\,s$^{-1}$ for WASP-17, so dilution by background light is a smaller problem than in the WASP-17 data. Since the star is of A type, its second and third orders are also
brighter relative to the first, which changes the overlap of the orders that
the pipelines must handle. Secondly, the exposures are full frames read
through four outputs with 10.7~s frames, whereas SOSS time series read one
output with 5.5~s frames. After the strip's own reference-pixel correction,
the variance of the noise per read is within 6 to 9\% of that of the SOSS
subarray. For example, the $1/f$ noise per read is 4.92~DN, compared with
4.81~DN for WASP-17. An integration has at most five groups, against eight for
WASP-17, and integrations follow each other every 64~s rather than 49~s. At
five groups the brightest pixels reach 89\% of saturation, and the saturation
rule of one pipeline then flags a quarter of the light in the extraction box
and changes those flags during the transit. I therefore build versions with
the first three groups, where this no longer happens, and the first four,
where it still affects 0.8\% of the light in the box.

Thirdly, some effects are absent by construction. The injected star does not
move, so the jitter and breathing of a real trace are missing, and no
electronic response that follows the total illumination is present during the
transit. The added photons are drawn independently in each pixel, so the
coupling of their noise between neighbouring pixels is omitted. The injected
ramps of the brightest pixels also do not curve over at high fill as real
ramps do, and lie 130 to 200~DN above them in the first group, although the
rates agree to 0.1\%.

The injected transit also specifies the limb-darkening law against which each
pipeline is tested. This is one quadratic law computed from PHOENIX models
over a declared passband, which none of the pipelines uses natively. The star
is also hotter than the limb-darkening grid from which two of the pipelines
take their reference, so their own fits carry a known mismatch. The first
scoring level avoids a fitted limb-darkening prior, although its result can
still depend on the injected light curve. In a control, every pipeline will
use the injected coefficients in both its white-light and spectral fits, and
each native configuration will state how it treats a star outside its grid.
The injected law is therefore a specified challenge for the pipelines.

These limitations restrict what the benchmark can show, yet leave the injected
light curve unchanged. It tests recovery for one star under the declared
injected conditions. The six science visits of WASP-17,
HAT-P-26, WASP-39, HAT-P-18, WASP-96 and WASP-80 have no independently known
stellar image. The WASP-17 case illustrates why a fitted split between star
and background is insufficient to establish injection truth
(Section~\ref{sec:which-light}). These visits will therefore be analysed as
real data with NOVA and the extraction-based pipelines of Paper~1
(Section~\ref{sec:plan}). Without a known true spectrum, their disagreements
cannot establish which method is more accurate.
