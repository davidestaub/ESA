\section{A Benchmark with Measured Starlight}
\label{sec:measured-benchmark}

The benchmark I am now building uses a calibration programme that observed a
star twice: once at its usual position on the detector, and once moved off
the strip that SOSS time series read out. The difference between the two
exposures measures the light of the star directly, so the injector depends
much less on a fitted split between starlight and background. In this
section, I describe how I turn this difference into a cleaned image of the
star and inject it with a known transit, how I will test the injector and the
pipelines, and the limitations of the benchmark.

\subsection{Measuring the star}
\label{sec:measuring-star}

On 26 March 2024, programme 4476 \citep{VolkEspinoza2023} observed
TYC~4213-1116-1 in two full-frame exposures taken about 25 minutes apart.
This A-type star gives about 1.6 times as much light per detector column as
WASP-17. In the
first, with ten integrations of five reads, the star is at its usual
position. For the second, the telescope moved by $67.1''$, placing the traces
1,026 rows lower. The SOSS strip then received no light from the star except
its faint far halo. This second exposure, which I call the moved exposure, has 150 integrations
covering 2.68~h. It is the one into which I inject the star.

On the SOSS strip, the first exposure contains the star, the sky, the field
sources of the first pointing and the signal of the detector itself. The
second contains the sky, the field sources of the second pointing, the far
halo of the moved star and the signal of the detector. In their difference,
whatever is the same at the same pixels cancels: the zodiacal background,
which the grism spreads into a pattern fixed on the detector, and the static
signal of the detector, such as its dark current and bias. The estimated
levels of the zodiacal background in the two exposures agree to within about
1\%. The read noise and the offsets that vary in time, such as the $1/f$
stripes, do not cancel.

The difference is not yet a clean image of the star, for three reasons.
Firstly, the far tail of the moved star's halo still reaches the strip in the
moved exposure as a faint, smooth glow of about
0.05~DN\,s$^{-1}$ per pixel, so the difference subtracts it from the image of
the star. The benchmark keeps this glow, because it is built on the moved exposure.
The total light out of transit is therefore right, but the glow does not
dim during the transit. For a 1.5\% transit in a 40-row extraction box with
ideal sky subtraction, the undimmed glow makes the depth 2 to 19~ppm shallower, depending
on wavelength. The full frames show this halo at 700 to 800 rows from the
moved star. Extrapolating its measured fall-off to the strip gives a model of
the halo there, and I can correct the image of the star by adding this model.

Secondly, field sources do not cancel, because their positions on the detector changed when the telescope moved
(Figure~\ref{fig:4476}). They appear as compact blobs, which look like
undispersed images of faint field objects, and as faint spectra of field
stars crossing the strip. The difference contains the field sources of the
first pointing as bright spots and those of the second as dark, negative ones.
A bright spot would become part of the injected star and dim during the
transit, so I replace its pixels by the light of the star estimated from the
neighbouring columns. A dark spot in the injected star would cancel the field source in the
benchmark out of transit, and part of the source would reappear during the
transit. At its pixels, I therefore subtract an estimate of the sky alone
instead of the moved exposure. The field sources of the second pointing then stay in the benchmark
as real light that does not transit, and the pipelines must handle them as
they would in real data. Field sources lying exactly under the traces move
with the star, so the two exposures cannot separate them, and their light
becomes part of the injected star.

I find the dark spots in the moved exposure, where the star is not in the
way. The bright spots show up as compact blobs that the neighbouring columns
of the trace do not have. Most field sources, including all compact blobs of the
first pointing, have no secure counterpart in Gaia DR3 \citep{GaiaDR3}, so the
images themselves are needed to find them. Gaia, however, also tells me where
the spectra of known stars fall, even for stars that lie off the strip. A
search for compact blobs cannot find these spectra. I update each star's catalogue
position to the date of the observation, and use the recorded pointing of the
telescope to find where it would appear on the detector without the grism.
The grism slightly rotates and stretches this pattern of positions.

I fitted this rotation and stretch on the WASP-17~b data
(Figure~\ref{fig:w17-gaia}). For a Gaia star $i$, let
$\boldsymbol{\Delta}_i$ be its offset from the target on the detector without
the grism, in pixels. Its undispersed image then lands at
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{p}_i = \mathbf{A}\,\boldsymbol{\Delta}_i + \mathbf{t},
  \label{eq:gaia-map}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{A}$ is a $2\times2$ matrix that rotates and stretches the
offsets, and $\mathbf{t}$ is the position of the target's own undispersed
image, which lies off the strip. The spectra of star $i$ are those of the
target, shifted by $\mathbf{A}\,\boldsymbol{\Delta}_i$.

To locate the compact blobs, I first subtracted a running median over 51 columns from each row.
This removed everything that is smooth along the rows, such as the traces and
the sky. I then removed the $1/f$ stripes by subtracting the median of each
column, smoothed the result slightly with a Gaussian of 1.5 pixels,
and flagged groups of at least 12 touching pixels that lie more than five
times the noise above zero. Many of these detections lie along the traces and
are left over from their removal. A source counts as real if it also appears
in the F277W exposure (Section~\ref{sec:detector-processing}), or if it matches a Gaia star.

To find which detected source
belongs to which Gaia star, I held the stretch fixed and slid the whole
predicted pattern across the image, within 300 pixels of the position given
by the default mapping of the ExoCTK contamination tool \citep{BourqueEtAl2021}. At each position, I
counted how many predicted images fell within 8 pixels of a detected source.
At most positions none did, and at 99\% of them at most two did. At the best position, seven of the nine predicted images did, so this alignment stands out clearly from all others. I then fitted the six numbers in
$\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{t}$ by least squares, matching the pairs again after each fit, which added an eighth pair. The fitted mapping places these eight images to within 0.8 columns and 3.6 rows
(rms), against 4.3 columns and 14.9 rows for the default mapping. When each pair
is left out of the fit and predicted from the other seven, the errors are 1.5
columns and 7.8 rows.

Programme 4476 cannot provide such a fit. Its field is
sparse, with 208 Gaia stars within $5.4'$ of the target against 1,455 around
WASP-17. Its strip contains the undispersed image of at most one Gaia star,
which is very faint. Both observations used the same instrument setting, so I use
the WASP-17~b fit for programme 4476. I will check the faint spectra that this fit predicts in both pointings. The injector finds field sources from the images, with Gaia guiding the search. Because NOVA may later use the same Gaia predictions, agreement between NOVA and the injector would not by itself show that the field-star light was separated correctly.

Thirdly, the far wings of the difference are noisy. The subtraction removes the sky, so the
image shows the light of the star far from the traces, where it is fainter
than the sky (Figure~\ref{fig:4476-profiles}). The first exposure, however,
has only ten integrations. I estimate the uncertainty of each pixel by
combining the pipeline's error estimates for the two exposures. These
estimates agree with the actual scatter between neighbouring pixels to within
10\%. Dividing
each pixel's count rate by its uncertainty gives a signal-to-noise ratio of 112 to 675 in
the cores of the traces, 10 to 73 at 20 to 40 rows from them, and only 2 to 3
at 40 to 200 rows from order~1 in the red. Injected as it is, this noise would
become a fixed pattern in the star.

The shape of the wings is set by the
optics and changes only slowly with wavelength. Beyond 40 rows from the
nearest trace, I therefore replace each pixel by the median of the pixels at the same
distance from the trace in the 61 neighbouring columns, which span about
$0.06\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ in order~1. On average, this far-wing model differs from the measured pixels by only about 0.16\% at 40 to 100 rows, so the wings change slowly enough across the 61 columns for the median to stand in for each pixel. Where the wings of several orders overlap,
the nearest order dominates this estimate. This lowers the noise per pixel
from about 0.10 to about 0.017~DN\,s$^{-1}$, and it also removes the $1/f$
stripes left in the difference. Between 30 and 40 rows, the weight of this
far-wing model rises linearly from zero to one, so that the image passes smoothly from
the data to the model. Nearer the traces, I keep the measured light, with its
noise and stripes. These are fixed in the image of the star, whereas the real,
changing $1/f$ noise of the benchmark comes from the reads of the moved
exposure across the whole strip. The result is the cleaned image of the star, $S$, a
count rate per pixel (Figure~\ref{fig:4476}c).

\subsection{Injecting the star}
\label{sec:injecting-star}

I add the cleaned image of the star, with a known transit, to the raw reads of
the moved exposure. The count rate of each pixel $p$ of the benchmark data
is then
\begin{equation}
  Y_p(t) = O_p(t) + S_p\,r_p(t) + n_p(t),
  \label{eq:4476}
\end{equation}
where $O_p(t)$ is the count rate of the moved exposure, $S_p$ is the cleaned
image of the star, $r_p(t)$ is the fraction of the star's light that remains
at time $t$, equal to one out of transit, and $n_p(t)$ is the photon noise of
the added light, which averages to zero. Everything in $O_p(t)$ is real,
including the sky, the field sources, the $1/f$ noise and the cosmic rays,
and none of it transits.

In an atmosphere with spectral features, the transit is deeper at some
wavelengths than at others, so each pixel must dim by the depth at the
wavelengths of its light. Where two orders overlap, a pixel holds light of two
different wavelengths, and a model must split it between them. I will take the wavelength at each position along each order from PASTASOSS
\citep{BainesEtAl2023Wavelength} and, where the orders overlap, split the
light with ATOCA \citep{DarveauBernierEtAl2022}, the public model built for this
purpose. As a second case, I can instead measure the profile of each order on
its side away from the other order, and assume the same shape on the side
where they overlap. The limb darkening for these cases is still to be chosen:
either one fixed pair of coefficients for all wavelengths, or coefficients that vary with wavelength. I have not yet built these spectral cases, and I will first test the benchmark with a grey transit, in which every pixel dims by the same fraction.

A raw integration is a ramp: charge builds up in each pixel and is read five
times, every 10.7~s, without being reset. Light arrives as individual photons
at random times, so the number that arrives in an interval varies around its
average, following a Poisson distribution. This is the photon noise of real
light. For each pixel and each interval between two reads, I therefore draw
the number of photons from a Poisson distribution whose average is the light
of the star in that interval, and add them up to give the extra charge at each
read. The recorded value is not proportional to the charge. As a pixel fills,
each additional electron raises it a little less. The
pipelines undo this with a standard calibration curve. So that the added light
behaves like real light, I convert each real read into charge with this curve,
add the star's charge, and write the value that the curve gives for the new
total. A pipeline that uses the same curve then recovers the added charge, to
within the rounding of the raw values. Nothing else is added. The bias, read
noise and $1/f$ noise of the real exposure stay as they are. Pixels that did
not respond to light in the first exposure receive none.

For every transit case, I also make a copy of the same data, with the same
photons, but without the transit (Figure~\ref{fig:4476-construction}). The
transit case is this copy with photons removed during the transit: where the
light curve dips by 2\%, each photon has a 2\% chance of being removed. The two share the real exposure and every photon that the transit did not remove. They differ only by the removed light, so the only noise left in their difference is the small photon noise of that light. Running a pipeline on both and subtracting its results at any step of the processing therefore shows the transit as that step passed it on. Comparing this with the injected transit reveals whether and where the
pipeline distorts it. For example, comparing the two after the $1/f$
correction shows directly whether that correction distorts the transit in
NOVA's processing, as the comparison with the same data without the transit
did in Section~\ref{sec:raw-injection}. A single data set with a transit gives
only the total error at the end, with the noise included.

The same construction can also inject a model image of the star in place of
the measured one. Such a star has no noise and no field sources, and the
wavelength at every pixel is known. Its wings, however, are those of the
model, and a pipeline built on the same model would be favoured. I therefore
plan to use it as a control. If a pipeline recovers the transit of the model star but not that of the measured one, this would point to differences between the two images of the star. Further tests would then be needed to find out whether the faint light is the cause.

\subsection{Testing the injector and the pipelines}
\label{sec:tests}

I test the injector in two ways. Firstly, simple checks of the code confirm
that it adds exactly the light I intend and nothing else. Secondly, out of
transit, the moved exposure plus the injected star should look like the first
exposure, in which the star was at its usual position. Apart
from the field sources, which differ between the two pointings, and the noise,
their images agree by construction. The comparison therefore tests whether the
injected light builds up in the detector as real light does. Pixel by pixel,
the charge should rise from read to read in the same way, and a pipeline should
flag the same pixels as close to saturation. Where they differ, the difference
is a limitation of the benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark-limits}). Neither
test can show that the cleaned image is the true star. That rests on the
cleaning described in Section~\ref{sec:measuring-star}.

Each pipeline is set up without knowledge of the injected transit and runs
its own workflow on the same raw files. Because the strip was read as part of a
full frame, the only reference file that differs from each pipeline's usual
choice is the bias reference, which must be that of the full-frame readout. I
compare the spectrum from each pipeline's own transit fit with the injected
spectrum. This comparison gives the total error that a user would get. To find where an
error arises, I also run each pipeline on the copy without the transit (Section~\ref{sec:injecting-star}) and
compare the two runs after every step that the pipeline saves, such as the
$1/f$ correction or the extraction. Knowing
where a pipeline distorts the transit shows what a method must get right,
which guides the development of NOVA.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:benchmark-limits}

The benchmark differs from a real SOSS time series, such as the observation of
WASP-17~b, in several ways.

Firstly, it rests on one star, with its own brightness, temperature and sky,
and these set how much each source of error matters. For a fainter star or a
brighter sky, for example, errors in removing the sky would matter more. At 7,548~K, the star is also hotter than 50 of the 51 exoplanet hosts that SOSS had observed by October 2026. The median temperature of these hosts is 4,870~K. The star's second and third orders are therefore brighter relative to the first than for a typical host, and the orders contaminate each other differently.

Secondly, programme 4476 read the whole detector every 10.7~s, whereas a SOSS
time series reads only the strip every 5.5~s. The light falls on the same
detector through the same optics, but it is read out differently. The strip
has five reads per integration, each with slightly more noise than in a time
series, while its $1/f$ noise is similar. The benchmark is therefore a
realistic test of how the pipelines treat the light, and a close but not exact
test of how they treat the noise of a time series. Because the star is bright,
its brightest pixels come close to saturation after five reads, and the
saturation rule of exoTEDRF then flags about a quarter of the star's light in
the exoTEDRF extraction box. I will therefore also make versions with only the first four and the
first three reads.

Thirdly, the injected star does not move or change shape, whereas a real
trace shifts and changes slightly during a visit, so the benchmark does not
test how the pipelines correct for this. Such motion could be added by shifting and
stretching the image of the star slightly in each integration, following the
motion measured in a real time series.

The injection also adds light, but it does not add any response of the
detector's electronics to the change in light. If the detector itself reacts when the star dims, as the faint light outside the traces may suggest (Section~\ref{sec:real-data-test}), the benchmark does not contain this reaction. In addition, the
photon noise that I add lacks the slight coupling between neighbouring pixels that real detector data show. This could be added from the measured strength
of that coupling.

The exposure also lasts only 2.68~h, so the injected transit is
51.5~minutes long, to leave time before and after it. A planet crossing the
centre of this star would take hours. With the one-day orbit that I chose, the
shape of this short transit corresponds to an unphysical star, about a hundred
times denser than this one. The benchmark therefore cannot test a pipeline that
fits the transit with a prior on the density of the star. None of the four pipelines that I will run on the benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:plan}) uses such a prior. Each fits the shape
of the transit freely.

Finally, in the grey case, the injected limb darkening follows one quadratic law computed from PHOENIX models of the star \citep{HusserEtAl2013}. Using PHOENIX avoids sharing the stellar models of any pipeline, although each pipeline's errors can still depend on the injected law. The star is hotter than the STAGGER grid \citep{MagicEtAl2015}, which stops at 7,000~K. In their spectral fits, exoTEDRF uses fixed coefficients from a 7,000~K model, whereas NOVA, as currently set up, keeps its WASP-17 reference and allows deviations from it. Both fit the limb darkening freely in the white-light curves. I still need to give NOVA a reference suited to this star. In a control, every pipeline will use the injected coefficients in both fits, to show how its recovered spectrum changes when the limb darkening is matched.

With the existing data, this is as close to a real observation as I can make
the benchmark. It would improve if JWST
repeated programme 4476 for several cooler stars of different brightness, like the hosts of the planets that SOSS usually observes. Each
observation could be read out as a SOSS time series, so that the noise
matches. More integrations with the star at its usual position would make the
image of the star less noisy. A longer exposure with the star moved away would
let the injected transits last as long as real ones.
