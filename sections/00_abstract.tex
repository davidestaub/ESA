\begin{abstract}
The transmission spectrum of an exoplanet, the fraction of starlight it blocks
at each wavelength, reveals what its atmosphere is made of. Existing pipelines
subtract the background and extract a spectrum from each image before fitting
the transit. They can disagree, and agreement cannot establish accuracy because
the true spectrum of a real planet is unknown. I ask whether fitting the
background and the transit together, on detector pixels, gives a more accurate
spectrum. I use the Single Object Slitless Spectroscopy (SOSS) mode of the
James Webb Space Telescope (JWST). My method, Nonlinear Order-coupled
Variable-projection Analysis (NOVA), predicts every pixel as starlight dimmed
by the transit plus the remaining background. It refines one spectrum, shared
by both orders, until the prediction matches the data. NOVA's first spectrum of
WASP-17~b resembles the published Ahsoka spectrum below about
$1.8\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, but is deeper at every wavelength, most in the red. Its
uncertainty is not yet validated. An injection test adds a known transit to
real data, but must decide which of the observed light belongs to the star.
For one injected atmosphere, the ranking of NOVA and exoTEDRF reversed with the
share of faint light taken to be starlight, which the WASP-17~b data cannot
determine. I am therefore building a benchmark from JWST calibration programme
4476. It observed a star at its usual position and again moved off the SOSS
strip, so the difference between the exposures measures the light of the star
directly. I will dim a cleaned image of the star with a known transit and add
it to the moved exposure's raw reads. The injected transit is known exactly
even where the image is imperfect. My first paper will use this benchmark to
improve NOVA, compare it with other pipelines and refit WASP-17~b. Four more
will cover five further SOSS visits, repeated transits and stellar
variability, the Near Infrared Spectrograph and, if time allows, secondary
eclipses.
\end{abstract}
