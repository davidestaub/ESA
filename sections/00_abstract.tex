\begin{abstract}
The transmission spectrum of an exoplanet, its transit depth as a function of
wavelength, reveals what its atmosphere is made of. In existing reduction
pipelines, the detector is calibrated, a background is subtracted from each
image, a spectrum is extracted from each image, and then a light curve is
fitted at each wavelength. Different pipelines can produce different spectra
from the same dataset. Even when pipelines agree, this does not show that
their result is right, because the true spectrum of a real planet is unknown.
Two steps of this process could bias the spectrum. First, the background is
subtracted before the transit is fitted. Second, each image is compressed into
a one-dimensional spectrum. In this project, I ask how accurately a
transmission spectrum can be recovered from data taken with the NIRISS/SOSS
mode of JWST, and whether fitting the background and the transit together at
the pixel level makes it more accurate. To explore this, I have developed a
method called Nonlinear Order-coupled Variable-projection Analysis (NOVA).
NOVA takes a trial spectrum, shared by both spectral orders, and uses a model
of the instrument to predict the count rate in each pixel, including the
background. It then refines the spectrum until this prediction agrees with
the data. Applied to the hot Jupiter WASP-17~b, NOVA produced a spectrum
broadly similar to the published ones but deeper at all wavelengths,
especially at longer wavelengths. Its uncertainties are not yet validated.
Because real data cannot show which spectrum is correct, I performed tests in
which I added a known transit to real SOSS data. For one model atmosphere,
which of two pipelines came closer to the truth depended on how much of the
faint observed light I assumed to come from the star, and the WASP-17~b data
cannot constrain this fraction. I am now using data from programme 4476, in
which a star was observed at its usual position on the detector and again
with the telescope moved so that the star no longer fell on the strip that is
read out. Subtracting the two exposures measures the light of the star in
each pixel. I will dim the cleaned image of the star with a known transit, add
photon noise, and add the result to the raw data of the offset exposure. The
injected transit is then known exactly for that image. Over the rest of my
PhD, I plan to compare NOVA with published pipelines using this benchmark,
apply it to five other SOSS visits and to repeated observations of the same
planets, and adapt it to secondary eclipses and observations with NIRSpec.
\end{abstract}
