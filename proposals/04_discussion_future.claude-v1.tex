I plan six papers. The first reports NOVA, the benchmark and the WASP-17~b
analysis, and answers objectives 1 to 3 of
Section~\ref{sec:research-question}. The next three stay with NIRISS/SOSS
transmission spectroscopy and address objective 4: transfer to other
targets, repeated visits and stellar variability, and a uniform reanalysis
of the published SOSS sample. The last two extend NOVA to NIRSpec and to
secondary eclipses. Each paper has a stated research question, a defined
data set and a validation step that must pass before its scientific
results are interpreted. Figure~\ref{fig:gantt} gives the schedule.

\subsection{Paper 1: NOVA, the benchmark and WASP-17~b}
\label{subsec:fw_benchmark}

\noindent\textbf{Research question:} \textit{Does NOVA recover more
accurate transmission spectra with reliable uncertainties than
extraction-based analyses, and does its application change the
atmospheric interpretation of WASP-17~b?}

The method, the benchmark and the WASP-17~b analysis will be complete
before the assessment. The paper reports the benchmark of
Section~\ref{sec:methods}, the comparison with Ahsoka,
\texttt{transitspectroscopy} and exoTEDRF, each run as its own end-to-end
analysis \citep{LouieEtAl2025,Espinoza2022TransitSpectroscopy,Radica2024},
and the WASP-17~b transmission spectrum with an atmospheric retrieval. A
comparison with JExoRES \citep{HolmbergMadhusudhan2023} is planned for a
later release of the benchmark.

What remains after the assessment is submission in 2026 Q4, the referee
process and the public release of the benchmark package: the injector, the
injected spectra, the recovery configurations, the fixed predictions and
the score tables needed to reproduce every figure. The benchmark injects
known spectra into the out-of-transit data of six SOSS visits, of
HAT-P-26~b, WASP-17~b, WASP-39~b, HAT-P-18~b, WASP-96~b and WASP-80~b, so
the detector scenes of the first targets of Papers 2 and 4 are
characterised before those analyses begin.

\subsection{Paper 2: Transfer to other SOSS targets}
\label{subsec:fw_transfer}

\noindent\textbf{Research question:} \textit{Which atmospheric conclusions
change when other SOSS observations are analysed with NOVA, and what
causes those changes?}

I will begin with WASP-39~b and WASP-96~b. Both are gas giants with
well-studied SOSS observations, so the first transfer stays within the
class of planet used for development. WASP-39~b has strong water features
and published constraints on water abundance and clouds
\citep{FeinsteinEtAl2023,CarterEtAl2024}. WASP-96~b offers a specific
disagreement: \citet{TaylorEtAl2023} favoured an enhanced scattering slope
at short wavelengths, whereas \citet{WangEtAl2026} found no haze
signature. Both fields contain contaminating stars.

\paragraph{Transfer.}
NOVA's transit model, detector likelihood, response estimation and
covariance propagation carry over unchanged. Each visit needs its own
transit geometry, limb darkening, integration timing, excluded pixels,
trace position and background estimate. These are estimated from the new
observations by the procedures used for WASP-17~b, and the benchmark
injections of Paper 1 already test whether those procedures recover known
spectra in these two detector scenes, including weak features. Any change
to the procedure of Paper 1 will be recorded and its effect on the
WASP-17~b result reported.

\paragraph{Field-star contamination.}
NOVA has no explicit model for contaminating stars. Three properties of
the method limit their effect. The spatial response is estimated from the
transit-shaped variation of each pixel, so steady contaminant light does
not enter the response. The additive background is a smooth low-order
surface anchored on off-trace pixels and cannot represent a dispersed
contaminant trace. Pixels flagged by the detector-quality mask are
excluded. Contaminating light that falls under the target trace therefore
remains in the transit fit, where the model can attribute it only to the
star, and the depth is diluted. The WASP-17~b comparison shows how
difficult a correction becomes when little of the contaminant trace is
visible \citep{LouieEtAl2025}; the JExoRES treatment shows that a
template-based correction is possible when more of it is
\citep{HolmbergMadhusudhan2023}.

The current policy is conservative: contaminated detector columns are
excluded and the lost wavelength coverage is reported, rather than
modelled. Scene inventories built from out-of-transit frames and F277W
exposures exist for all six benchmark visits. F277W images locate
contaminants, but their brightness cannot be transferred directly to the
science exposures because the filter changes the transmitted light
\citep{HolmbergMadhusudhan2023}. Where the coverage loss matters for a
scientific question, I will extend the model with an additive template per
contaminant. Its spatial shape follows the visible part of the contaminant
trace, projected under the target by the instrument model; its amplitude
is a nuisance parameter anchored like the background, so that it cannot
trade against the transit. A template prototype and a held-out likelihood
test already exist for WASP-96~b.

\paragraph{Testing the transfer.}
Tests will inject known planetary spectra into the target star only,
leaving contaminant light in place, including in excluded pixels, and will
repeat the background, white-light, response and spectral fits. They
compare plain exclusion with the template correction on depth error,
uncertainty coverage and retained wavelength range. WASP-39~b will be used
to fix the decision rules. WASP-96~b will then be analysed under those
rules without further changes, as a test of transfer to a different
detector scene.

\paragraph{Atmospheric interpretation.}
For each planet I will compare NOVA with a conventional reduction that
uses the same calibration files, then apply matched atmospheric models and
priors, and examine whether water abundance, cloud constraints and
spectral slopes are consistent across reductions. For WASP-96~b, the
contamination treatment, limb darkening and slow brightness trends will
be varied separately to identify which choices move the short-wavelength
slope. The result may support either published interpretation or leave the
slope uncertain.

\paragraph{A geometry test.}
HAT-P-14~b transits at grazing geometry, where planet radius, transit path
and limb darkening have similar effects on the light curve. Its
observations also saturate, and updated processing removed much of the
spectral structure first reported \citep{LiuEtAl2025}. It tests how
geometric and limb-darkening uncertainty propagate into the recovered
spectrum, using a three-parameter law where the quadratic law is
insufficient \citep{KeersEtAl2024} and the empirical JWST results on FGK
limb darkening as a reference \citep{SingEtAl2026}. HAT-P-14~b will be
included in Paper 2 if it adds a distinct result.

\subsection{Paper 3: Repeated visits and stellar variability}
\label{subsec:fw_repeats}

\noindent\textbf{Research question:} \textit{Can independent visits
recover a consistent planetary spectrum once visit-specific instrumental
and stellar effects are accounted for, and are the remaining differences
explained by the uncertainties?}

GJ 9827~d has two SOSS visits and a published water-rich interpretation
\citep{PiauletGhorayebEtAl2024}. It extends the application to a smaller
planet and gives a direct repeatability test. I will first reproduce a
conservative analysis that excludes flare-affected integrations, then fit
the visits separately and examine how shared geometric information changes
their agreement. Injection tests remain necessary because repeated
observations can share a bias.

I will then add a flare model fitted alongside the transit, with timing
shared across wavelengths and amplitude and colour free. This tests
whether integrations discarded by masking carry planetary information.
Because NOVA estimates the spatial response from the transit time series,
a flare can bias that step before the spectrum is fitted. The response
estimation will therefore be repeated with the variability model included,
and alternative estimates tested when the transit is too weak to constrain
it.

Spots and faculae change the apparent spectrum because the transit chord
differs from the disc average \citep{RackhamEtAl2018,RackhamEtAl2019}, and
a spot crossing produces a feature inside the transit. These will be
modelled separately from flares and instrumental trends. Their light falls
on the same trace as the rest of the star, so separation relies on timing,
wavelength dependence and physically constrained stellar models. Simulated
flares and surface features will test whether the model recovers the
planetary signal or absorbs it.

LHS 1140~b will be the second main target. Four SOSS visits allow a test
across observing dates. Recent analyses agree that helium is not detected
\citep{GressierEtAl2026,BennettEtAl2026}, while the first broadband study
reported tentative spectral structure and stellar contamination
\citep{CadieuxEtAl2024}. I will examine whether weak broadband features
persist after accounting for stellar changes. Visit-specific complications
reported in the published analyses, such as a displaced trace or an
overlapping transit of another planet, will be modelled explicitly or
excluded under a stated rule.

TRAPPIST-1~f is the more demanding case. Its five visits contain flares,
although the published spectrum shows no evidence of contamination from
unocculted stellar structure \citep{LimEtAl2026}. It tests whether the
model developed on the first two systems handles these data without
suppressing the planetary signal; visits with too little clean data may
yield only limits or a restricted wavelength range. GJ 9827~d and
LHS 1140~b form the main paper. TRAPPIST-1~f is included if the same
extension handles it.

For each system, the separate visit spectra will be compared before any
joint fit. Planetary information from some visits will also be used to
predict another visit while that visit's own stellar and instrumental
effects are fitted. These checks establish whether the visits support a
common planetary spectrum and a common atmospheric interpretation.

\subsection{Paper 4: A uniform reanalysis of SOSS transmission spectra}
\label{subsec:fw_atlas}

\noindent\textbf{Research question:} \textit{Which differences between
published SOSS transmission spectra remain after a uniform reanalysis, and
which atmospheric conclusions are sensitive to the observations or to the
analysis choices?}

Once transfer and repeatability are established, I will apply NOVA to the
public SOSS sample. Uniform reanalyses with HST have shown the value of a
consistent treatment across planets \citep{TsiarasEtAl2018,SabaEtAl2025}.
This catalogue does the same for SOSS and adds a validation report for
each observation. The first release covers planets with public SOSS
transmission analyses and public transit observations available when the
work starts in 2028 Q1. Selection rules, non-detections, incomplete
observations and every exclusion will be documented.

The sample covers problems beyond those used to develop NOVA. HAT-P-18~b
and WASP-52~b test stellar surface effects, unocculted heterogeneity in
one case and spot crossings in the other
\citep{FournierTondreauEtAl2024,FournierTondreauEtAl2025}. GJ 357~b and
LTT 9779~b test small or muted signals
\citep{TaylorEtAl2025,RadicaEtAl2024}. K2-18~b provides a comparison with
an existing study of sensitivity to reduction and modelling choices
\citep{SchmidtEtAl2025}. Some targets will be held back until the
processing settings are fixed, as an independent test of the workflow.

The catalogue requires a workflow that prepares each observation,
identifies contaminating sources, fits the spectrum and runs its
validation tests without manual changes to the code. It reuses the
contamination policy of Paper 2, with source positions, masks and template
amplitudes determined per observation. HAT-P-18~b, HAT-P-26~b and
WASP-80~b are already prepared as benchmark hosts. Order overlap and the
dispersed zodiacal background will be tested against the established
treatments \citep{DarveauBernierEtAl2022,BainesEtAl2025}, and the
calibration will account for the count-dependent nonlinearity of the
NIRISS analogue-to-digital conversion \citep{DholakiaEtAl2026}.
Conventional reductions with matched calibration are retained for a
representative subset and wherever NOVA changes an important conclusion.

Every released spectrum will carry injection tests: weak and strong
features, broad bands, slopes and a flat spectrum, with the
data-dependent calibration steps repeated for each test. Injecting a
change into real observations tests recovery of that change; absolute
depth requires a baseline whose truth is known, and the two kinds of test
are kept distinct. Further tests vary trace motion, bad pixels,
saturation, background structure, contamination and the length of the
pre- and post-transit baseline. A visit may pass at some wavelengths and
fail at others, and those restrictions are carried into the released
spectrum.

The atmospheric comparison begins with quantities measured directly from
the spectra, water band contrasts and short-wavelength slopes,
distinguishing an overall depth shift from a change of shape, as in
earlier comparative studies \citep{SingEtAl2016,FuEtAl2025}. Retrievals
then test what the differences imply for gases and clouds, with consistent
assumptions within comparable planet classes and identical assumptions
when reductions of one planet are compared. Correlations between
wavelength bins will be retained because they affect the retrieval
\citep{IhKempton2021}. The products are separate-visit spectra, justified
combined spectra, uncertainties with their correlations, quality flags,
injection reports and the software and calibration versions used.
Spectra corrected for stellar contamination are labelled separately from
the apparent spectra measured in each visit.

\subsection{Paper 5: Joint NIRISS and NIRSpec analysis}
\label{subsec:fw_nirspec}

\noindent\textbf{Research question:} \textit{Can NIRISS and NIRSpec
observations be explained by one planetary transmission spectrum after
accounting for their separate instrumental and stellar effects?}

The WASP-39~b benchmark spectrum improved agreement across instruments
through a consistent analysis but left discrepancies in partially
saturated PRISM data \citep{CarterEtAl2024}. I will test whether a
detector-level analysis changes that agreement and whether combining
instruments produces a more reliable spectrum.

NIRSpec G235H/F170LP comes first because its band overlaps SOSS
substantially. LHS 1140~b already has G235H and G395H observations
\citep{DamianoEtAl2024} and is the initial target. G395H/F290LP then
extends the wavelength coverage, followed by dedicated PRISM tests and
selected G140 modes. The actual wavelength range and detector gap of each
configuration will be used; G395H mainly extends coverage and offers
little overlap with SOSS \citep{JDoxNIRSpecBOTS2026}. WASP-39~b PRISM is a
separate test of the documented disagreement.

The extension requires a NIRSpec description of how stellar light falls
on the detector: trace shape, wavelength mapping, spectral resolution,
slit throughput and noise, while the transit model is retained. The
injector uses the same instrument description to create tests with known
spectra, and each mode is validated separately before it enters a joint
analysis.

NIRISS and NIRSpec are first fitted independently and compared at matched
resolution in the overlap. The joint model then uses one intrinsic
spectrum with separate backgrounds, detector effects and stellar
properties for each visit. Stellar changes between dates can shift the
apparent depths even when the atmosphere is unchanged, so those
contributions are constrained by physical models and by the data rather
than by an arbitrary offset at every wavelength, which could conceal a
disagreement. The common spectrum is passed through each instrument's
response, so that the comparison accounts for the different bins and
resolutions.

Paired injections test recovery of the same planet through both
instruments with independent noise, backgrounds and stellar states, and
deliberate mismatches test whether the joint model detects incompatible
data. The products include individual and joint spectra with the
uncertainty of relative depth offsets, and data sets that remain
inconsistent are reported as such. The first NIRSpec paper covers the
modes that pass these tests; further modes follow in later releases.

\subsection{Paper 6: Secondary eclipses and the dayside atmosphere}
\label{subsec:fw_eclipses}

\noindent\textbf{Research question:} \textit{Can NOVA improve the
reliability of weak eclipse measurements and the atmospheric conclusions
drawn from them?}

WASP-17~b is the starting point. Its SOSS eclipse analysis found that
allowing negative fitted depths changed a tentative FeH constraint while
the water result was more stable \citep{GressierEtAl2024}. I will
investigate whether a detector-level analysis improves these weak
measurements and how the resulting spectrum changes the estimated dayside
temperature and composition.

During secondary eclipse the planet's thermal emission and reflected light
disappear while the stellar light remains. The planet will initially be a
uniformly bright disc whose hidden fraction is predicted through ingress
and egress and averaged over each integration. The detector model keeps
its treatment of source light and background, with an eclipse model
replacing the transit signal. Uncertainty in the orbit and eclipse timing,
uneven planetary brightness and slow changes of planet flux around the
event will be tested.

The positive depth parameters become signed eclipse amplitudes. Noise can
place a weak measurement below zero; retaining that estimate lets the
atmospheric fit use the full measurement uncertainty, while the physical
atmosphere still predicts nonnegative flux. Depth relative to the star is
distinguished from depth relative to star and planet together. Response
estimation needs its own tests, since a weak eclipse may contain too
little variation to calibrate the response through the event; the mean
stellar image and the instrument's optical model are the alternatives.

The eclipse injector adds a known planetary contribution to a star-only
baseline. The added light disappears during eclipse, and star, background
and unrelated sources are unchanged. For injection before calibration,
the accumulated planetary counts are added to each detector read with the
corresponding photon noise, which changes the signal and noise treatment
of the transit injector. Tests cover very weak emission, thermal and
reflected light, and atmospheres with and without weak molecular
features. Conventional extraction and NOVA are compared with signed and
positive-constrained fits, followed by matched retrievals, to separate
the effect of the depth constraint from the effect of the reduction. The
metrics are recovery of injected spectra, coverage of uncertainty
intervals and false detections of molecular features. The validated
procedure is then applied to WASP-17~b. Circulation models can support
later phase-curve tests.

Figure~\ref{fig:gantt} shows the dependency structure. Paper 1 releases
the benchmark and the accepted injector that every later paper reuses.
Paper 2 fixes the contamination policy that the catalogue applies without
change, and Paper 3 supplies the stellar-variability model that Papers 4
and 5 need for spotted hosts and multi-epoch data. The NIRSpec detector
description of Paper 5 is new development and starts while the catalogue
is being produced. Paper 6 reuses the injector changes of Paper 5 for
injection before calibration. In every paper the recovered spectra are
frozen before the atmospheric retrievals are run, so that detector-model
uncertainty is traced into the atmospheric constraints rather than fitted
away. The code and validation products of each paper are archived with a
citable identifier. Further extensions to MIRI, phase curves, direct
atmospheric retrieval from detector data and accelerated inference depend
on these results and are not scheduled. Progress is reviewed quarterly
against closed deliverables, and a milestone counts as complete only when
its data products, configuration, diagnostics and written interpretation
are archived together.

Five risks are foreseen. The first is contamination: if excluded columns
remove more wavelength coverage than a scientific question can tolerate
and the template correction does not validate, the affected spectra are
released with the reduced coverage and the limitation is stated. The
second is stellar variability: where flares or spot crossings cannot be
separated from the planetary signal, the analysis reports limits or a
restricted wavelength range rather than a spectrum. The third is
computational cost, which grows with the number of visits and injection
tests; variable projection, streamed derivatives and staged small cases
before visit-scale runs control it, as in Paper 1. The fourth is the
NIRSpec detector description, the largest technical development of the
later papers; if it does not validate in time, Paper 5 is reduced to
independent fits compared in the overlap region. The fifth is schedule: a
delay in the referee process or the benchmark release of Paper 1 shifts
Paper 2, and Papers 5 and 6 are the adjustable scope, while the SOSS
programme of Papers 1 to 4 is the core of the thesis. A fair result in
which NOVA does not outperform extraction-based analyses remains
scientifically useful, because it bounds the benefit of joint
detector-level inference under stated conditions.
